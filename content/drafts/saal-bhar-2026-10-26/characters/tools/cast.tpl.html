The Saal Bhar cast

"Let's see how comicbooks solve it... it's by giving characters a unique visual identity that stays consistent across boards... define each character in depth, how they would talk, how they would type, the words they'll use, the clothes they'll wear, their postures everything."
You, 26 Oct

Ten people and you. Each was written in full, then read by a comic editor (can you tell them apart in silhouette, and by their lines with the names covered?), a real-India reader (would all six of our players know these people?), and a rules and painter check, then revised.

How they stay the same in every panel

  1. Anchors before faces. Each person has an identity stack that survives any style: silhouette and height, hair as a shape, one face accessory, one garment with its own pattern, one prop, one posture. No two people share an anchor, the way Chacha Chaudhary's stick and Suppandi's flat head read at a glance.
  2. One master model sheet each, painted in today's style: front, three-quarter, profile, back, full figure, usual posture, and four to six expressions. Three tries each, picked by eye.
  3. One reference picture per look each person appears in, made from the master by the painter's edit model, which already paints from references for the trunk.
  4. Paint by scene from those references, at most two referenced people a panel, every panel checked against each person's anchors.
  5. Only if someone keeps drifting: more reference views, and only then a model trained on that one person.

The line-up

Twelve everyday moments

The voice pass gave everyone the same twelve moments to react to, so you can hear them side by side. With the names hidden, three readers matched every one of these 132 lines to the right person. Before the pass, a reader matched 43 of 55 of the old lines.

What the reviewers left for you

  1. Married women with nothing on. The rules keep every religious or married mark off the cast, so Mummy, Mausi and Mami wear no bangles, chain or forehead mark, just like widowed Nani. To older eyes in the Hindi belt, a bare married woman of 52 reads as widowed. Allow plain glass or metal bangles and a plain chain, which many communities wear?
  2. The household is all north Indian. Players from the south and east recognise the types but nothing of their own. Give one in-law a south or east Indian family, with their own words for things?
  3. Greeting Nani. Touching her feet is out, and nothing has replaced it, so arrivals at her gate feel scrubbed. A hand on her shoulder, a bow of the head, a hug? Which?
  4. Done: the voice pass. Only Papa (minutes) and Tinku (scores) still lean on numbers. Everyone has their own exclamation and their own way of saying sorry and thanks, and Mausi and Mama ji say feelings out loud. One small rule question came out of it: lines like "Tumne bahut kiya" (you did so much) praise you as the doer, and in Hindi the verb follows the deed, so they never show your gender. The rule does not list that shape yet. Add it, or have a native reader clear each such line?
  5. Spectacles. Only Nani (round) and Masterji (half-moon) wear them. May others, if the frames differ?
  6. Speech bubbles. Name tags only, or a bubble style of their own for each person (Mausi's rounder and louder, Masterji's thinner)?
  7. Next: paint the line-up (all ten as black shapes and simple figures, in each of the five looks) and then the master model sheets, through the queue on your Mac, for you to approve before any story panel is painted.
