# Saal Bhar voice matrix and targets

The owner asked: "Do the voice pass". This file is its first step. It changes how people speak, never who they are: every essence, want, fear and contradiction stays as the cast files have it.

**Read for this file:** the eleven cast entries (`characters/result.json`, key `cast`: voice, typing, essence, want_fear_contradiction, relationships, never_does), the three reviews' cast-wide and per-person voice points (key `reviews`: comics, india, rules), the cast map's sound grid (section 6) and the realism notes (sections 1 and 7), all in the scratchpad's `characters/` folder, and the rulebook `knowledge/story/ground-rules-draft-2026-10-25.md` (owner answers at the top, rules 3, 22, 37, 43, 62, 64, 66). Nothing in the repo was changed.

**The rules every target keeps**
- Rule 3: no religious word or exclamation (no Hai Ram, Bhagwan, Allah, Rab, Wahe Guru, Jesus, Ishwar, Mata ji as a goddess), no caste word, no brand.
- Rule 66: every Hindi line to or from the player marks no gender (commands, "who should" asks, lines where the player is not the doer); the player is never बेटा, भैया or दीदी; the young speak to their elders with आप and -इए.
- Rule 62: a spoken line is at most 10 words, narration at most 12. A long talker speaks in more bubbles, never in longer ones.
- Rule 22: the household never doubts a report of harassment or a dowry demand.
- British spelling; no em or en dashes.
- This pass's quotas: at most two people lean on numbers; at least two say feelings out loud in plain words; at least three speak in longer runs; each person has an exclamation of their own with no religious meaning; each has their own way of saying sorry and thanks.

**How to read it.** Part 1 is the cast today, one table per axis, with each collision tagged [C1] to [C16] and listed in 1.10. Part 2 is the target for each person, then the spread checks that prove the quotas. Part 3 is the test set.

### 1.10 The collisions

- **C1. Numbers everywhere.** Eight of eleven lean on counting or measuring: Papa (minutes), Mummy (prices, and every typed message ends on a figure), Tinku (scores), Mami (ordinals in three lines of four), Mama ji (plus ten), Captain sahab (toss countdowns, 'Nine means nine'), Nani (the finger count, age jokes) and Masterji (measures by feel). Cover the names and the counting lines cannot be sorted. The cap is two.
- **C2. Clipped lines.** Eight of eleven speak in one to seven words (Nani, Mummy, Papa, Tinku, Mausa ji, Captain sahab, Mami, you). Told apart only by trade nouns.
- **C3. One feeling key.** Seven show love only through a deed: Nani (tea), Mummy (the pen, the key, the free item), Papa (the berth), Mausa ji (half a sweet lime), Masterji (the shutter, the re-measuring), Captain sahab (your best score), Mami (the stopped readback). Tea carries four people's care (Nani's 'Chai piyo', Masterji's tumbler, Mama ji's 'Boss, chai', the tea Nani pours for Papa). Only Mausi and Mama ji say it.
- **C4. Sorry and thanks swapped for a deed.** Seven elders (Nani, Mummy, Papa, Mausa ji, Masterji, Captain sahab, Mami). Nani and Mummy use one act for both sorry and thanks. Tinku, Mama ji and you have no way at all.
- **C5. 'No charge' twice.** Mummy thanks with 'Iska bill nahin.' and Masterji with 'Paise nahin loonga.'
- **C6. Exclamations.** Papa, Tinku and you have none. Mami's 'लो' is the same word as Mausa ji's 'Lo.' ('Here.'), so a bare 'Lo.' cannot be sorted. 'Arre' sits in both Captain sahab's 'Arre waah' and Mausi's 'Arre, arre, arre'.
- **C7. 'Not out' twice at one beat.** After the flood, Captain sahab tells Mummy 'Not out, Bhabhi ji. Next ball.' and Tinku tells her 'Shutter up at nine. Not out, Mummy.'
- **C8. 'Theek.' as a one-word verdict** for both Mausa ji and Mami.
- **C9. 'Pehle X, phir Y'.** Mausi ('Pehle khao, phir baat.'), Masterji ('Pehle chai.'), Mami ('Mummy ji, pehle chai. Phir mehmaan.') and your own 'Pehle ___' tic.
- **C10. Three dodge 'How are you?' with a figure.** Mummy with a price, Papa with a time, Tinku with somebody's score.
- **C11. Trade nouns as the whole voice.** Nine of eleven. Read together, Nani's weighing, Mummy's हिसाब and Tinku's tallies can make the family read as the trader stereotype (india review). Three list-keepers (Papa, Mami, Mummy's spoken lists) beside Tinku's book.
- **C12. One mix for seven.** Nani, Mummy, Papa, Mausi, Mausa ji, Masterji and Captain sahab all use a Hindi frame with single English nouns from their trade. Mama ji and Mami both switch whole clauses.
- **C13. Dry is the house humour.** Eight of eleven are dry or deadpan.
- **C14. Anger.** Seven go quieter when cross (Nani, Mummy, Papa, Mausa ji, Masterji, Captain sahab, Mami). Each now has its own rhythm and body, and no chapter's speaking pair shares a tell, so this is mostly fixed; the targets keep every tell.
- **C15. Everyone talks mostly to you.** Right for the asks, but almost no line passes between the others, so the voice test never hears them with each other.
- **C16. The two plain feelers share a word.** Mausi's 'Haaye! Bahut yaad aayi.' and Mama ji's Hinglish 'Amma, I missed you. Bahut.'

## Part 2. Targets

Each card says what stays, then the target on every axis, the anger tell (kept from the files), what to strike, and one sample in the target shape. Samples are Hinglish | English; Hindi is written fresh later and read by the Hindi-first readers (rule 65).

### 2.1 Nani, turning 80: the grocer who weighs everyone
- **Stays:** essence, want, fear, contradiction (runs everyone's life, lets no one run hers). Her closed fragments are hers alone.
- **Length and rhythm:** the shortest in the cast. One to three words, each closed with a stop. A proverb is the one whole sentence she allows herself.
- **Words from:** her past at the counter: the scale, weights, credit, change, and real grocer's sayings in place of head-counts. 'डंडी मत मारना।' (from the india review); candidates for a Hindi-first reader to confirm, never invented: 'जितना गुड़, उतना मीठा।', 'बूँद-बूँद से घड़ा भरता है।'
- **Feeling:** turned into an order. Help becomes a job she hands out; care becomes 'Chai piyo.' Plain words once, in the album.
- **Humour:** sly. A straight face, and a proverb or a question turned on you ('Aapke timetable se suraj bhi uthta hai?').
- **Sorry:** an order: 'Chai piyo.' | 'Drink your tea.' **Thanks:** the next job: 'Ab ye bhi.' | 'Now this one too.' **Love:** she counts you in, once: 'Aur tum.'
- **Exclamation:** 'Baap re.' | 'Well I never.'
- **Numbers:** none in speech. The finger count stays silent; aloud once, in the album. At most one age joke, if rulebook Q5 allows one.
- **Mix:** the most Hindi in the cast, small-town and full of idiom; English only for nouns the world handed her.
- **Talks to:** you, her plain-truth person; jobs go to 'koi' (someone), never to a name.
- **When cross:** 'Bas.', the palm pressed down once, eyes to the window; one 'बस!' a year.
- **Sample:** 'Saare kaam. Dandi mat maarna.' | 'Every job. No thumb on the scale.'

### 2.2 Mummy, 52: the shop on the station road
- **Stays:** essence (she runs the family like the shop: everything is an account), want (the shutter at nine, owing no one), fear, contradiction (swore never to sit behind a counter like her mother; runs hers exactly like her).
- **Length and rhythm:** medium. One bubble, five to ten words, a list of people and things joined by commas, mid-fast. A list of jobs, never a list of prices.
- **Words from:** today's counter (goods, customers, power cuts, the shutter) and the one interest on the page that is not a number: her roof chillies ('Mirchi phool rahi hai.').
- **Feeling:** changes the subject to the shop. 'How are you, Mummy?' 'Copper phir mehenga.' The dodge keeps its shop words and loses its figure.
- **Humour:** none on purpose. She is the straight face; her shop greeting at home is the family's joke and she never hears it.
- **Sorry:** takes the fault on like a debt, out loud: 'Ye mere hisaab mein.' | 'Put that one on my account.' **Thanks:** 'Iska bill nahin.' | 'There's no bill for this.' (hers alone now). **Love:** the pen and the spare key laid down, turned towards you, with no word.
- **Exclamation:** 'Had hai!' | 'That's the limit!'
- **Numbers:** rare and marked: the shutter hour in her catchphrase ('Nau baje. Shutter up.') and minutes only in an emergency ('Nau bajkar das.'). Prices leave her speech and stay on the bills, painted blank. 'Ek rupaya kam nahin.' may stay: 'one' there is an idiom, not a count.
- **Mix:** Hindi frame with English trade nouns and the English verb + kar/ho frame ('check karo'), which is hers alone in the cast.
- **Talks to:** customers and the hired hands first, then Tinku at the counter; the shop greeting to everyone ('Haan ji, boliye.').
- **When cross:** politeness rises: 'ji', आप, -इए.
- **Strike:** 'Bhindi 60 rupaye?' becomes 'Bhindi itni mehngi?'; 'Pachaas hazaar.' becomes 'Poora maal.'; the joke 'Keel ke paanch rupaye.' becomes 'Keel ka bill.' Her typed tell ('every message ends on a figure') goes to the typing pass.
- **Sample:** 'Tum bill, Tinku stock, main shutter.' | 'You on bills, Tinku on stock, me on the shutter.'

### 2.3 Papa, 56: the railway parcel clerk who loves in timetables

- **Length and rhythm:** medium to long. Whole sentences of four to ten words that keep their verbs, announced one after another in one to three bubbles, each with its full stop. The verbless time correction is his one fragment.

### 2.4 Mama ji, 47: the worrier who rings first
- **Stays:** essence (generous, sure that more is safer), want, fear, contradiction (worries about everything, plans nothing). One of the two who say feelings out loud, and one of the long runs.
- **Length and rhythm:** the longest single sentence in the cast: one run-on that grows across two to four bubbles, commas and 'aur haan', no full stop till the end; worry questions stacked. With Nani alone, short whole sentences.
- **Words from:** people. His phone book (the tent man's mother's knee, the cook's son's exam) and his worry (BP, trains, weather warnings). City logistics English carries the jobs.

### 2.8 Masterji, about 60: the station road's slow measurer

## Part 3. The test set

Twelve everyday moments every one of the eleven can react to. Each is one neutral sentence: no gender, no religion, no card person, no hard topic.

1. The lights go out in the middle of dinner.
2. Someone everyone was waiting for arrives two hours late.
3. A cousin phones to say they have got their first job.
4. The tea someone has just made has far too much sugar in it.
5. In front of the family, someone shows that they had a fact wrong.
6. Someone they love is leaving, and they say goodbye on a railway platform.
7. Someone thanks them for something they did.
8. A neighbour passes on a rumour that the corner sweet shop is closing.
9. Heavy rain floods the lane outside the house.
10. Someone asks them how they are.
11. A child knocks a glass of water across the table.
12. A phone call brings good news.

**What each moment tests.** 1: exclamation, humour. 2: anger tell, numbers. 3: praise; feeling said or shown. 4: humour; a polite no. 5: sorry; anger. 6: love; feeling. 7: thanks. 8: humour; judging habits, never character (rule 22 safe: the rumour is harmless). 9: worry; where their words come from. 10: feeling; the dodges in C10. 11: comfort; kindness. 12: joy; exclamation.

**How to run it.**
- Each of the eleven reacts to each moment in one bubble of at most 10 words: Hinglish first, Hindi and English after. A long talker's shape must show inside the one bubble (Mama ji's trailing comma, Masterji's verdict and turn, Mausi's triple).
- In at least four moments (2, 4, 8, 11), the line is said to someone other than you, so the cast is heard with each other (C15).
- All the rules hold: no religious word (2.13), no number outside the two who lean and the marked exceptions, rule 66 on every line to or from you, the cousin in moment 3 kept without a gender (a noun line or a ने past), British spelling, no em or en dashes.
- Shuffle the 132 lines with their tags removed. A reader who did not write them sorts them by speaker, with only the eleven target cards (2.1 to 2.11) beside them.
- Pass: each person's lines sorted right at least 10 times in 12, and no two people mixed up with each other more than twice. Any pair mixed up three times or more goes back for a fix before any panel is written.
- Run it again in Hindi with the Hindi-first readers and in English, since a voice that only sorts in Hinglish fails two of the three modes.
