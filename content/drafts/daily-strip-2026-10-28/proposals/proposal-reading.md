# The chorus strip

Draft by one of three designers, 28 Oct 2026. Nothing here is built and nothing is decided. Rules marked **new** wait for the owner's yes; everything else follows the ground rules draft (`knowledge/story/ground-rules-draft-2026-10-25.md`, answers 1 to 17 and rules 1 to 66) and the bubble spec (`knowledge/architecture/webtoon-bubble-spec-draft-2026-10-25.md`).

## In one paragraph

A day is one everyday scene (the lane loses power; the office has a deadline; the bus is late). The three levels are three moments of it, morning, noon and evening, each a complete strip on its own: a person in charge hands the player small decisions one at a time, and two neighbours who have an opinion about everybody are met in the strip before any card appears. Their remarks then sign the card notes, on women and men, on the equal two and on everyone else, in equal measure. So the nudge never arrives alone: it is one remark in a chorus the player already knows, in a voice that grumbles about the weather too. Ties appear in most questions, not only around the equal two; the day's most stereotyped remark sits where the numbers already decide; one question a level is a near miss that punishes following the remarks either way. The strip itself never points: it talks only about the moment, never about anyone on the cards, and nothing reacts to a pick until the level's outcome, which comes from the numbers alone.

## 1. What a daily is

- **One scene a day, three levels, three moments.** Easy is the morning, Medium noon, Hard the evening of the same scene, with the same asker and the same two chorus voices. Each level has its own six people on the cards, and nobody sits on cards at two levels that day (stronger than rule 53, which only bars the equal two). No level ever mentions what happened at another level, because the player may play them in any order or not at all.
- **Questions per level** (**new**): Easy 5 questions with 4 people each; Medium 4 with 5 people; Hard 4 with 5 people. Easy cards read fast (clear numbers), so five fit in two minutes; Medium and Hard cards have more to weigh, so four.
- **What each question is for.** Every level has exactly two **measured** questions (a new twin's first half and a returning twin's second half), one **trap** (a near miss that looks like a measured question), and the rest **steady** (the numbers decide). In launch week there is nothing to return yet, so the returning slot holds a steady question and the first readings finish in week 2.
- **Where the measured questions sit** (**new**). Each weekday and level has its own two positions, set in the week plan and kept every week, so both halves of a twin sit at the same position. For the sample Tuesday: Easy 2 and 4, Medium 2 and 3, Hard 2 and 4. Across a week no level uses the same two positions more than twice, and question 1 is measured on at most two days.
- **A level's shape.** Day opener (first level played that day only) → the asker's handover → the circle and the targets → for each question, one strip beat then the decision → the outcome → the result.

## 4. The questions in the strip

- **Who asks.** One asker for the whole day at all three levels: the person in charge of the scene (Sarla, the lane committee's head, in the sample). Never a chorus voice, never anyone on the cards, never comic. Askers across a week: three women, three men. Because a twin's halves are a week apart in a different scene, rule 37's "same asker" becomes (**new**) an asker of the same gender and the same kind of role ("the one running things today"), with the same number and shape of bubbles and the question's words within 2.
- **Where the question sits.** Each question follows exactly one strip beat whose last bubble gives the goal of the moment ("Lineman's tea, the diesel. All from this box."). The strip then gives way to the existing decision screen: the question in the asker's bubble with her face chip at the fixed question place (top at 104 px), drawn so it cannot pass for a card or a button; on a hard-topic question it sits in the plain question box instead (rule 37).
- **How the cards appear.** All at once, in a new shuffled order on every load (`useShuffled`), over a people-free painting of the scene, with the key row. Easy: 4 cards (tiles for pick one, rows for team and rank). Medium and Hard: 5 rows. The first time a player meets a kind, a ghost hand demonstrates it on blank cards before the real ones appear, so the hand never moves towards a real person (F-25).
- **How the player answers.** Tap to pick, rank or spend a check; the yellow button confirms (yellow only for the pick and its button). The next strip beat then shows a settled plate naming only the job ("Cash box: settled"), the same for every pick. No names, no faces, no reaction until the outcome.
- **The outcome** comes from the numbers only, by one rule for every target (**new**): a placement counts unless someone left out was clearly ahead of the person placed (at least as high on both numbers and higher on one). The equal two are never clearly ahead of each other, so swapping them changes nothing (rules 44 and 45 hold by construction). Measured "matters" questions have a fixed outcome line.
- **The result** (owner, 28 Oct: "works") shows plain counts of the outcome only: "Power where needed: 5 of 5", "Nothing missing: 3 of 3", the day count, "Back to today". **Recommendation:** no count of matching pairs on a daily result. A line such as "treated 1 of 1 matching pairs alike", seconds after the strip, tells the player there was a pair today and sends them scrolling back to find it. Finished-twin counts belong on Sunday's week page, over the whole week, and in the First take's private reading.

## 5. Easy, Medium and Hard

| | Easy | Medium | Hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Questions, people | 5, 4 each | 4, 5 each | 4, 5 each |
| Kinds | pick one, pick a team, rank | adds spend checks (and later kinds) | rank, team, batting order, split, pick one |
| Numbers | clear: every card outside a tie is clearly above or below | the cut falls inside a tie between two women or two men | trade-offs: high on one number, low on the other |
| The measured question | equal two clearly best, one remark (the nudge) on one of them, a remark of the same kind on a decoy, a second tie between two women or two men below them | either a "matters" twin (a reason that truly decides sits on one of the two) or a plain twin with a second leaning remark of a different kind on a decoy, alternating by weekday | the two tied in the middle with the cut between them, each carrying a remark of the same weight, which swap sides in the twin; a heavy-sounding fact on a decoy |
| The trap | a woman and a man one apart, a remark pulling the wrong way | the same, mirrored | a tie between two women (or two men) differing only by a remark: the same shape as a measured question with no gender in it |

**Second and third level the same day.** The day opener (L-01 and the chorus panel) folds into a thin band at the top of the strip showing a strip of L-01 and the day's title; a tap unfolds it, otherwise the strip starts at that level's handover. Everything else is new: the asker in a new light, a new six on the circle beat, new moments, a chorus beat with new lines. The player never sees the same panel with the same words twice in a day.

## 6. How the equal two and the nudge stay hidden

The honest limit first: the equal two match exactly on displayed numbers, so a player who scans every card for a woman and a man with the same numbers will find them. The design does not rely on hiding them from a determined scanner. It relies on three things: a casual player meets the pair inside a field of ties and remarks where nothing marks it; finding the pair wins nothing, because the only steady way through is to treat the same remark the same whoever carries it, which is the habit the trainer exists to build; and "spot the sexist" is a losing rule.

**What the strip does to help (and never does to harm).**
1. **The chorus is met before any card.** The day opener shows the two commenters talking about the day only ("Third cut this month. Typical." / "Chai? My gas is fine."). Their faces then sign remarks on cards. A remark arrives as "that's Kishore again", in a voice that complains about everything, so its tone attaches to the speaker, not to the person it is about. Each chorus voice has one character (one grumbles, one fusses fondly) and both remark on women and men, positively and negatively, on the pair and on decoys.
2. **The circle beat equalises everyone.** The level's six appear together as identical face chips with names, shuffled, with no relation, job or quirk, before any question. Nobody arrives on a card as a stranger or as someone the strip has talked about.
3. **The strip talks about the moment, never about people.** No bubble, caption or sign names, points at, describes or uses a content word from the notes of anyone on any later card in that level (rule 36, checked against every earlier bubble, not only the lead-in). Card people never speak and are never painted.
4. **Lead-ins look the same before every kind of question.** One beat, a settled plate and the asker's goal of at most 10 words, whether the next question is measured, a trap or steady. The chorus speaks before measured and steady questions equally often.
5. **Nothing reacts to a pick** until the outcome, and the outcome cannot tell the pair apart.

**On the cards.**

6. **Ties everywhere** (**new**). In each number set of a level, most people have a number twin: in the sample's trust set, two women are tied, two men are tied and the equal two are tied, so "the tied ones" are half the cast. At least 3 of Easy's 5 questions and every measured question show an exact tie; every measured question shows a second tie (between two women or two men, or on one number at Hard).
7. **Same-sex contested ties look exactly like the measured shape** (**new**). In some steady questions the last place is fought over by two tied women or two tied men. A player cannot tell from the shape alone whether a tie is the one that counts.
8. **The lone-gender rule** (**new**). With 4 cards and a second tie, a measured question at Easy must be three of one sex and one of the other, and the lone one is in the pair. So steady questions also use three-and-one splits where the lone one is in no tie, and across a level the lone one is in the pair in at most half of such questions (the sample: 1 of 4).
9. **Notes are symmetric.** At Easy the equal two carry one remark and one plain fact, and so do the decoys; the decoy remark is of the nudge's kind (rule 20). All notes within 2 words, the pair's equal, no shared opening word or frame (rule 19). The nudge is never the only remark on the screen and never the longest note.
10. **Remarks balance by speaker, sex and warmth** (**new**, checked). In the sample's Easy level each chorus voice makes 4 remarks, 2 about women and 2 about men; women receive 2 kind and 2 unkind remarks, and so do men.
11. **Matched histories** (rule 17). Both of the pair appeared the same number of times before, both in the top half or both in the bottom, with notes of the same warmth, and neither was ever the weakest card. The sample's pair were the two picked in question 1, both with plain job notes.
12. **Names.** Both of a pair carry community-marked names or neither does; marked names sit in pairs, on decoys, at the top and at the bottom by their share; no heavy fact on a marked name (rule 21).

**Against "spot the sexist".**

13. **The nudge is an everyday remark, not a stereotype claim.** "Moody", "fussy", "spoilt", "always on the phone", "works so hard": things said of anyone. It is irrelevant to the task in a plain twin, so following it is a lean, not a judgement.
14. **The day's most stereotyped remark sits where the numbers already decide** (**new**). In the sample, "Bossy, that one. Always was." is on a woman who is clearly top for the keys. Spotting it and defying it changes nothing.
15. **Traps punish following the remarks in either direction.** A near miss (a woman and a man one apart) carries a remark that pulls towards the weaker one, sometimes praise on the weaker, sometimes a grumble on the stronger, on women and men alike across the week. Only the numbers get the outcome.
16. **Mirrored twins.** Half of each week's twins put the first nudge on the man; half of the nudges are kind remarks (a protective pull) and half unkind. A player whose rule is "defy remarks about women" picks the woman in both halves and shows exactly the lean the twin is built to see (rule 54).
17. **"Matters" questions** (Medium) break the rule "ignore every note in a woman-and-man tie": there the note truly decides, and the twin reads whether a woman's credential is followed as often as a man's.
18. **The same-side tie** (Hard's trap) is the same choice with no gender in it: two women, or two men, tied and differing only by a remark. It tells the research line how far a remark sways at all, so a lean is never confused with plain note-following. It is never shown on screen.

**Twins across weeks.** The second half returns next week, same weekday, same level, same position in the strip, same kind of question, in the next world, with new people, a new scene and task, the nudge of the same kind on the other one, an asker of the same gender and role, and the same bubble shape (rule 58 plus the position rule above). A ledger records every half so the build refuses a twin whose halves differ in anything but the people, the scene and the nudge's side.

**What the reading never shows.** No count of pairs on a daily result, no "test", "bias" or research words on screen (rule 64), nothing on the cover, the lock screen or a reminder about any card person.

## 8. Sample day: Lugdi week, Tuesday, "Lights out"

Launch week (week 1), a Tuesday: the second-easiest day, idea of the week loaded words, not a hard-topic day. Everyday scene: the lane's transformer burns out at 9 on a weekday morning and the electricity office says "by evening". No city, no festival, no brand. Predicted for the six players: a power cut is lived by all six; the lane committee asking the grown-up child of a house to run it for the day is the part the readers should test.

**The day's look** (for every painting): Lugdi pulp cover over a present-day residential lane in a power cut: bold black ink outlines, flat red, mustard and night blue, coarse halftone, hard sunlight or lamp light and deep shadow. Painter rules: no words, letters or numbers anywhere (signs, papers, screens and the transformer's warning plate painted plain or shut), unsigned, a plain wall, shutter or sky above the heads, heads 30 to 45% down, the first speaker in the left third, at most three people, never the player, never anyone from the cards, present-day plain clothes, no bindi, sindoor, religious symbol or brand (check every woman's face close up for painted bindis, memory flux-portrait-biases). New glossary rows before painting: transformer box on a pole, two-socket board, steel cash box, committee notebook, inverter, battery lantern, chest ice box.

**The speakers** (model sheets first, owner 26 Oct):
- **Sarla**, about 62, the lane committee's head, the asker. Cotton sari with a small present-day print, steel-rimmed glasses, grey hair in a low bun, a cloth bag on her wrist, her phone always at her ear on hold with the electricity office. Brisk, short sentences, never jokes, asks only for what is needed.
- **Kishore**, about 55, runs the corner shop with the small generator. Checked half-sleeve shirt, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, a pencil behind his ear, sits on a red plastic stool. Grumbles about everything and everyone, never loudly; his tic is "that one". His remarks are mostly unkind, sometimes backhanded praise.
- **Nusrat**, about 45, the tailor from number 7. Plain printed cotton salwar suit, dupatta over one shoulder, a cloth measuring tape round her neck, always carrying chai. Fond of everybody, says what she sees; her remarks are mostly kind.

**Easy's six** (names are placeholders, swapped for portrait-library names before writing): Meenal (woman, 38), Rekha (woman, 34), Farzana (woman, 29), Sunil (man, 36), Imran (man, 31), Deepak (man, 30). Farzana and Imran carry community-marked names.

## 9. Risks

1. **A determined scanner always finds the pair.** Exact ties on shown numbers cannot be hidden from someone hunting them. The design makes finding them useless, not impossible; if playtests show players hunting ties and alternating picks, the research line needs to know before launch.
2. **The lone-gender tell at Easy.** Four cards plus a second tie forces three-and-one splits with the lone one in the pair; the offsetting rule only dilutes it. Five cards at Easy would remove it but costs about 20 seconds a level and the "more" cue on small phones.
3. **The chorus may soften the nudge too much.** A remark in a voice that grumbles at everyone may sway nobody, and the twins would then show nothing. That is the subtle nudge asked for, but its strength is unknown until the research check (RSE-164) and a pilot with adults (never students, memory no-unvalidated-tools-on-children).
4. **Retention decides the reading.** Twins finish only for players who return the same weekday at the same level. Rule 58's "half of players meet the second half" may fail; the ledger must report it from week 2.
5. **Launch week reads nothing.** With no returns possible, the first finished twins arrive in week 2.
6. **Writing load.** 13 questions a day, about 78 a week, 156 for two weeks, each level a small deck that needs a solver for ties, matched histories, the lone-gender rule and chorus balance. The chapter 1 solver and checkers live in a scratchpad that a reboot wipes (memory scratchpad-wiped-on-reboot); they must move into the repo first.
7. **Painting and placement load.** 144 panels and about 5 hours of bubble placement for two weeks, plus three speakers' model sheets a day (about 18 a week). A week set in one neighbourhood with a standing cast would cut the sheets to about six, but would change rule 59's rotation of life areas; it is an option, not part of this design.
8. **Rule changes waiting on the owner:** 5 questions at Easy and 4 elsewhere; same-gender-and-role askers in place of "the same asker"; measured positions by weekday; the outcome rule; no pair count on daily results. Each is marked new above.
9. **Gain or load.** Keeping the cash, the keys and driving Sarla's car are read as gains; if the five players disagree, those questions leave the twins (rule 23).
10. **Time.** Hindi rows on a 360 x 640 phone show the "more" cue at five cards, which may push Medium and Hard past 2 minutes; time it in the real app.
11. **The matters twin's fixed outcome** may teach that reasons never change outcomes (the clash already noted under rule 45).
12. **Hindi.** Every Hindi line here is a draft for the five Hindi-first readers; the gender scan should pass (no line by or to the player uses a gendered future or past), but "क्रम लगाओ" and "जाकर कुछ खा लो" still need a native reader's eye.

## 11. Questions for the owner (plain words)

1. **Five questions at Easy and four at Medium and Hard?** Example: Easy's five are quick (four cards, clear numbers), Hard's four take longer (five cards, numbers that pull both ways). All three still come to about six minutes.
2. **May the day's result leave out "matching pairs"?** Example: after Easy, the result says "Power where needed: 5 of 5" but not "treated 1 of 1 matching pairs alike", because that line tells the player a pair was hidden today. The matched count would show on Sunday, for the whole week.
3. **Two neighbours who comment on everyone, every day?** Example: Kishore grumbles at the weather and at Rekha's phone calls alike; Nusrat praises everybody. Their remarks are the card notes. Yes makes each remark part of a voice you know; no keeps notes as unsigned facts.
4. **Should one neighbourhood carry a whole week?** Example: Lugdi week is five days on the same lane (the power cut, the water, the lift), with the same Sarla, Kishore and Nusrat. Fewer characters to draw and more story; fewer different places.
