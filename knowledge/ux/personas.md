# Equal Lens Daily: player personas

## How to read this

- **What these are.** Six fictional players who stand for the main groups who could play a free daily fairness puzzle on a phone in India, plus two people we design *against*. Each is built from published 2023 to 2026 data where it exists. Anything I could not confirm from a source's raw text is marked **ASSUMPTION** (my judgement) or **UNVERIFIED** (a claim from a source I could not check at first hand).
- **What these are not.** No real player has been interviewed for this file. Treat every persona as a hypothesis for the first playtests, and replace guesses with what real players do.
- **Why these six.** Together they cover:
  - women and men, ages 26 to 61;
  - a metro, two tier-2 cities, a metro suburb, a small town and a village;
  - English-first, Hindi-first and South Indian players;
  - phones from a ₹6,500 phone with 2 GB of memory to an iPhone;
  - one colour-blind player and two players with ageing eyes;
  - every stance from "I am already fair" to "this is false balance".
- **Population figures are context, not traits.** A survey figure such as "86% of Indian women agree a wife must obey" describes a population. It says nothing about any one person. Where a persona holds a view, that is marked **ASSUMPTION**, and the figure sits beside it only as context.
- **Source keys** like [S4] point to the source list at the end. [I1] and similar point to the research line's own files.
- **Product facts** come from the owner's brief [I2] and decision log [I3]: one daily puzzle of 5 to 7 minutes, consistency scored both ways, lean size shown but never direction, one forgiven missed day a week, one global board ranked by practice under road names, Lugdi and Station weeks, English and Hindi first, adults only.
- **Owner questions.** Where a persona tension needs an owner decision, it points to the single deduplicated list in [journeys.md](journeys.md), as OQ-1 to OQ-30.

**Three cautions on the evidence.**
1. The newest nationally representative survey of gender attitudes I found is Pew's, with fieldwork from November 2019 to March 2020 [S18]. The owner prefers evidence from 2024 on, so treat attitude claims as older data. Pew also found that younger adults are no less traditional than older ones on family roles [S18], so no persona assumes that young means progressive.
2. Data is cheap and plentiful [S12] [S13], but phones are getting dearer [S10] [S11]. The tight constraint for most players is an old, full, low-memory phone, often shared, not the data bill. **ASSUMPTION** drawn from those figures: people will keep their phones longer in 2026 and 2027.
3. Survey percentages describe populations. They are never a persona's trait (see "How to read this").

## Primary personas

### P1. Shruti, the puzzle collector
*A 27-year-old Bengaluru analyst who plays every daily puzzle going and posts her results.*

- **Who.**
  - 27, metro (Bengaluru), product analyst at a fintech firm.
  - Grew up in Mysuru, speaks Kannada at home, shares a flat with two friends.
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - iPhone 13 bought in 2023, unlimited 5G. **ASSUMPTION**; she stands for the 6% on iOS [S7] and the 22% buying above ₹30,000 [S9].
  - Reads English fast. Reads Hindi slowly, as many South Indian players do (**ASSUMPTION**). Reading comfort: high in English, low in Hindi.
- **When and where.** On the office bus at about 9 am (**ASSUMPTION**), and in bed after 11 pm, as most surveyed adults are [S20].
- **Plays now.**
  - LinkedIn Queens, Zip and Tango. India is second only to the US for LinkedIn game play [S21], and has 160 million+ members [S22].
  - NYT Connections and Wordle.
  - She posts results to the office chat. Wordle spread through its shareable emoji grid [S27].
- **Why she starts.** A colleague's spoiler-free result card, and the novelty of a puzzle about fairness rather than words.
- **Why she comes back.**
  - Streaks and friends.
  - A puzzle that fooled her yesterday. LinkedIn reports 84% next-day return for exactly this kind of daily habit [S21].
- **What makes her quit.**
  - Puzzles that are easy, or obviously "spot the sexist".
  - Copy that reads like corporate POSH training.
  - A result that feels like a form.
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - Calls herself a feminist and believes she is fair. That is the bias blind spot, which spans fields and cultures [I1, Pronin and Hazel 2023].
  - Low pushback on the topic, high pushback on preachiness.
  - May bristle when a lean shows up and the game will not say which way.
- **Accessibility.** None declared. She plays on a moving bus, so the type needs high contrast.
- **A good day.** "It got me. Same record, different words, and I only saw it on Sunday. Clean score after that, and my card looked great in the group."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost her.** Every day opened on the same settings-style Today hub. The result was a sum ("110 Score = 100 Consistency + 10 Practice") with no art and nothing worth sharing [I4, diag-screens].
- **The one design implication.** **Puzzle craft at the level of Queens and Connections.** Each puzzle needs one clever trap, difficulty that climbs fast, and a beautiful result card that gives nothing away.

### P2. Ravi, the shop-counter competitor
*A 26-year-old in Indore who runs the family phone shop by day, plays ranked games with his friends at night, and would play this for the road name and the board.*

P2 now merges the earlier P2 (Ravi, a 34-year-old shopkeeper) and P4 (Aman, a 20-year-old student), who overlapped: both were Hindi-belt men hooked by the road name and the board, with pushback on framing against men.

- **Who.**
  - 26, tier 2 (Indore). Runs the family's phone accessories and recharge shop with his father.
  - Lives with his parents and his sister, 22, who studies and rides home late from coaching classes.
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - ₹11,000 Android from 2023 with 4 GB RAM, prepaid data (**ASSUMPTION**). He stands for the roughly 78% of buyers below ₹30,000 [S9].
  - Reads Devanagari comfortably and skims English. Types Hinglish in Latin letters on WhatsApp (**ASSUMPTION**; the earlier copy audit cited research on this, not rechecked here [I6]).
  - Knows English mostly as tech words [S16]. Reading comfort: medium in Hindi, low to medium in English.
- **Colour vision.** Red-green colour blind (**ASSUMPTION** for him; about 1 in 12 men have some colour blindness [S32]). Red against yellow, and red against green, look almost the same to him.
- **When and where.** At the shop counter in the slow hours after lunch, and late at night, often past midnight (**ASSUMPTION**; most surveyed adults sleep after 11 pm [S20]).
- **Plays now.**
  - Ludo King with his cousins. It was the first Indian game to pass a billion downloads [S26].
  - A ranked mobile shooter with a squad of school friends (**ASSUMPTION**; midcore spending grew fastest [S6, secondary, UNVERIFIED]).
  - Matiks or a similar daily challenge game with a global leaderboard (**ASSUMPTION**; Matiks has daily challenges, global leaderboards and friends [S25]).
  - Forwards a lot on WhatsApp [S28].
- **Why he starts.**
  - A cousin's screenshot with a funny truck-style road name, or a friend's road name in the squad group.
  - The Lugdi pulp covers feel like railway bookstall novels (**ASSUMPTION**).
- **Why he comes back.**
  - Climbing above his cousins and friends on the Friends board, and a rank band on Everyone.
  - Puzzles set in places he knows: a shop hiring, a family wedding, a cricket team.
  - Gamers juggle 2 to 3 games at once [S4]. He drops the weakest.
- **What makes him quit.**
  - Anything that reads like an NGO lecture or moral science class.
  - Hindi that sounds translated or Sanskritised.
  - A board whose top looks like bots or fake players.
  - A message that seems one-sided against men.
  - Slow loading on his phone.
  - A result he would not want his cousins to see.
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - He thinks equality mostly exists already and sometimes forwards "men suffer too" memes (**ASSUMPTION**). Context only: 81% of Indian men told Pew that men have more right to jobs when jobs are scarce (2019, older data) [S19].
  - Protective of his sister, so the "kind bias" and safety levels hit close to home (**ASSUMPTION**).
  - High pushback on one-sided framing. Scoring both ways is his hook: favouring women costs as much as favouring men [I2]. Bias can run towards women too [I1, Williams and Ceci 2015].
  - The risk is that he optimises the score rather than the habit. He will try "always give twins the same answer", which false twins are meant to catch [I1].
- **Accessibility.**
  - Colour blindness: any state shown only by red, yellow or green is invisible to him.
  - Glare at the shop counter, and customers interrupting.
  - Late-night play in a dark room on low battery, one-handed.
- **A good day.** "I set my rule, I stuck to it every time, and Highway Ka Sher is three places above Pintu. Muddled level, missing records, still kept my rule."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost him.**
  - The first screen showed the brand twice, a slogan, a label and a dashed "The Hindi is a draft" notice. It told a Hindi player that his language came second [I4, diag-synthesis].
  - The board was an admin-style table under a dashed "Sample board: the other players are made up" notice, with five yellow "Add X" bars [I4, screenshot 057].
  - The forgiven streak day was marked by colour only, a red spine (the usability digest, finding on UA-4).
- **The one design implication.** **A board that looks alive and fair, Hindi that sounds the way people talk, and no meaning carried by colour alone.** The road name and the friends board are the way in, so they must work from a WhatsApp forward in two taps. The board rewards careful practice, not speed or volume, and needs per-player variations and pattern checks so copying answers and same-answer play do not pay [I1, section 4.5].

### P3. Sunita, the small-town tailor on a hand-me-down phone
*A 41-year-old in a small town in Uttar Pradesh who plays in a quiet hour after lunch and does not want anyone reading over her shoulder.*

- **Who.**
  - 41, small town in Sitapur district.
  - Takes in tailoring work at home. Her husband runs a small grocery shop.
  - Daughter of 17 and son of 14. Her mother-in-law lives with them.
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - Her husband's old ₹8,000 Android from 2021: Android 11, 3 GB RAM, storage nearly full (**ASSUMPTION**). She is among the 14% on Android 11 or 12 [S8].
  - Smartphone ownership in India is 36% for women against 58% for men [S3]. Among rural teenagers who can use a smartphone, 20% of girls own one against 44% of boys [S15]. So her daughter borrows this phone for school videos and messages (**ASSUMPTION**). The daughter is not a player: the game is for adults only. What happens when a child picks up a shared phone is a designed case in [journeys.md](journeys.md) (OQ-17).
  - Reads Devanagari slowly but surely, and reads very little English (**ASSUMPTION**). Reading comfort: low to medium.
- **When and where.**
  - A quiet hour after the midday work and before the children return, about 2 to 3 pm (**ASSUMPTION**), and again late at night.
  - Women in India spend 289 minutes a day on unpaid housework [S14]. Her play time is what is left over.
- **Plays now.**
  - Ludo King with her son.
  - Candy Crush (**ASSUMPTION**; women's top preferences are casual and midcore mobile games [S5]).
  - Recipe and devotional videos, WhatsApp family groups.
- **Why she starts.** A women's self-help group facilitator shows it at a meeting, or a cousin forwards it to the family group (**ASSUMPTION**).
- **Why she comes back.**
  - Scenarios from her own life: a wedding budget, a daughter's schooling, who inherits the shop.
  - A small daily ritual that is hers.
- **What makes her quit.**
  - Too much reading.
  - Anything that makes fun of family or religion.
  - A result that feels like a judgement.
  - A phone that stalls.
  - Anything private appearing where her husband, mother-in-law or children might see it.
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - The persona assumes she holds some traditional views on family roles and has seen dowry and son preference up close (**ASSUMPTION**). Context only: 86% of Indian women told Pew a wife must always obey her husband [S19].
  - Low pushback if the game respects her family. High if it seems to mock them.
  - Her bigger risk is feeling exposed, not pushing back.
- **Accessibility.**
  - Near-vision decline is likely at her age. Presbyopia is the world's main cause of near-vision loss [S17]; that it typically starts around 40 is general knowledge, UNVERIFIED here.
  - Reading glasses are not always to hand. She is unsure with unfamiliar gestures.
  - Typing Devanagari for the Sunday note is slow.
  - Safety and digital skills are Indian women's top barriers online [S3].
- **A good day.** "The wedding one: two brothers and a sister, same share of work, and I noticed the words. Nobody saw my note."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost her.**
  - Missing faces were letter squares ("J", "F") and thumbnails were 44 px.
  - The dashed line carried 8 meanings.
  - Long screens scrolled to 3,200 to 3,600 px [I4, diag-screens].
- **The one design implication.** **Every puzzle playable from pictures and numbers, with one short question read once,** on a 3 GB Android 11 phone. Nothing private shows on the home screen, in a notification or in a share.

### P4. Meena, the village banking agent on a 2 GB phone
*A 30-year-old in a Jharkhand village who handles her neighbours' cash withdrawals and plays at dusk on one bar of signal.*

This persona takes the place freed by merging the old P2 and P4, and fills the rural gap: 55% of India's internet users are rural [S1], and no earlier persona lived in a village.

- **Who.**
  - 30, a village in Gumla district, Jharkhand (rural).
  - Works as a bank's village agent (a "bank mitra"): she handles small cash withdrawals and deposits for neighbours on a fingerprint device (**ASSUMPTION** for this persona). Where she lives it is a job men usually hold (**ASSUMPTION**), so she breaks type.
  - Lives with her husband, who works away for part of the year, two sons of 9 and 6, and her mother-in-law (**ASSUMPTION**).
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - A ₹6,500 phone with 2 GB RAM and 32 GB of storage, mostly full of WhatsApp media, and a small 720p screen (**ASSUMPTION**). Phones under US$100 are getting scarcer [S10], so she will keep this one for years (**ASSUMPTION**).
  - A prepaid daily data pack, shared with her work apps. Signal is good at the weekly market and weak at home (**ASSUMPTION**; the network reports could not be read as raw text, see the usability digest UA-P33).
  - Reads Hindi in Devanagari well, speaks a local language at home, and reads English only as numbers and bank words (**ASSUMPTION**). Reading comfort: medium in Hindi.
- **When and where.** At dusk, after her last customer, at about 7 pm, and on Sunday mornings (**ASSUMPTION**).
- **Plays now.** Ludo King with her sons, and short videos (**ASSUMPTION**). No daily puzzle habit yet.
- **Why she starts.** A cousin studying in Ranchi forwards a card that asks "Who keeps the cash box?" (**ASSUMPTION**). It is about her job.
- **Why she comes back.**
  - Scenes from village life drawn with respect: a gram sabha, a ration queue, whose name goes on the land, who handles the cash.
  - Being good at it, quietly.
- **What makes her quit.**
  - A page that will not load on a weak signal, or loses her place when the signal drops.
  - An app that eats storage or data.
  - English.
  - Village life drawn as backward, or as a charity case.
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - She has met customers who would rather hand cash to a man (**ASSUMPTION**). Low pushback on the topic.
  - High pushback if the village, or her community, is mocked or pitied.
- **Accessibility.**
  - Situational: a 2 GB phone that throws background tabs out of memory, a weak and patchy network, a small screen, bright sun at the market.
  - She switches to her banking app mid-puzzle when a customer arrives, so the browser may reload the game (the usability digest, UA-P35).
- **A good day.** "It loaded on one bar, I finished it at the counter, and the result went when the signal came back. The cash-box one was exactly my work."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost her.**
  - Three screens before the puzzle on a slow network (001 to 003) [I4, diag-synthesis].
  - The build shipped four Hindi font weights of about 70 to 75 KB each, plus fonts for regional board scripts, and was never tried on a 2 GB phone or a weak signal (the usability digest, finding on UA-41).
  - Labels of 12 to 13 px on a small screen (the usability digest, finding on UA-13).
- **The one design implication.** **A game that loads light, plays on through a dropped signal, and draws village life with respect.**

### P5. Mr Natarajan, the retired rule-keeper
*A 61-year-old retired bank officer in Coimbatore who does the crossword with his morning coffee and believes he treats everyone the same.*

- **Who.**
  - 61, tier 2 (Coimbatore), retired bank officer.
  - Lives with his wife, Lakshmi. Their son works in Bengaluru and shares links in the family group.
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - ₹16,000 Samsung from 2022, with font size and display zoom set to large (**ASSUMPTION**). Home broadband plus a mobile plan.
  - Reads Tamil and formal English, and little Hindi (**ASSUMPTION**). He waits for Tamil, which comes later. Tamil is among the most popular Indic content languages [S1].
  - Reading comfort: high in English at a large size, low in Hindi.
- **When and where.** At about 6:30 am with filter coffee after his walk, and after lunch (**ASSUMPTION**).
- **Plays now.**
  - The newspaper crossword and Sudoku.
  - Wordle, since his son showed him.
  - Forwards results and good-morning messages to the family group [S28].
  - 28% of Indian gamers are over 45 [S6, secondary, UNVERIFIED]. Ludo King's growth is reported to have drawn in players aged 45 and older [S26, UNVERIFIED primary].
- **Why he starts.** His son's forward, and the idea of locking a rule first, which suits a man who applied loan rules for 35 years.
- **Why he comes back.** The streak, explaining a puzzle to Lakshmi, and proving to himself that his rule holds.
- **What makes him quit.**
  - Small or faint text, a timer, swipe-only controls.
  - Hindi he cannot read.
  - Any hint that he is being called biased.
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - Believes in merit and rules. The persona assumes he is still traditional on jobs and family (**ASSUMPTION**). Context only: southern respondents were less likely than those in the Hindi belt to say a wife must always obey [S18].
  - High pushback if accused. Low if he is treated as a fair man checking his own consistency.
  - Seeing the size of a lean without its direction may frustrate him ("tell me which way!").
- **Accessibility.**
  - Presbyopia [S17].
  - Text must still work at 200% with no loss of content [S30, WCAG 1.4.4].
  - Layout must reflow at narrow widths [S30, WCAG 1.4.10].
  - Large targets: at least 24 by 24 px is the WCAG floor [S30, WCAG 2.5.8]. The digests set 48 px for this product (the usability digest, section 2.0).
  - No time limits [S30, WCAG 2.2.1]. Never meaning by colour alone [S30, WCAG 1.4.1].
  - He reads best with dark text on a light surface (the perception digest, PH-35).
- **A good day.** "I locked 'most experience' and it held for all five. Lakshmi got the twins one wrong. Forty days now."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost him.**
  - The question and the status line were both 17 px, so nothing stood out.
  - Meaning was carried by dashed outlines.
  - The swipe card had no obvious tap alternative [I4, diag-screens].
- **The one design implication.** **Layouts that survive large text and a tone that treats the player as fair.** Posters must reflow rather than being fixed 340 by 720 frames [I4, diag-synthesis]. Every gesture needs a tap equivalent.

### P6. Farida, the nurse on the evening local
*A 32-year-old staff nurse who plays standing in the ladies' compartment, one hand on the pole.*

- **Who.**
  - 32, metro region (Mumbai). Staff nurse at a hospital in Thane, working rotating shifts; lives in Kalyan.
  - Lives in a joint family with her husband, son of 6 and mother-in-law.
- **Phone, data and reading.**
  - ₹18,000 Samsung A-series from 2023, prepaid (**ASSUMPTION**).
  - Speaks Hindi and Urdu. Reads Devanagari comfortably, English for work charts, and types Hinglish in Latin letters (**ASSUMPTION**).
  - Reading comfort: medium to high, but the train is noisy and she is often interrupted.
- **When and where.** On the train home, around 7:40 pm (**ASSUMPTION**). After a night shift, at odd hours.
- **Plays now.** Candy Crush and Instagram reels (**ASSUMPTION**; casual mobile games lead among women players [S5]).
- **Why she starts.** A nursing colleague's WhatsApp forward, and the scenarios about work and motherhood.
- **Why she comes back.**
  - Seeing real things named: a nurse passed over for shift lead "because she has a child", dowry, safety.
  - Short sessions that fit between stations.
- **What makes her quit.**
  - Losing her place when the train stops.
  - Needing two hands.
  - Content that turns her daily reality into a "both sides" exercise.
  - How her community is drawn once religion enters later levels [I2].
- **Stance and pushback risk.**
  - She lives the double shift [S14], is alert to bias and expects to agree with the game.
  - Her pushback comes from the other side: scoring that penalises favouring women can feel like false balance to someone who faces discrimination daily.
  - The brief has not settled whether legal corrective rules (reservations) appear [I2] (OQ-29).
- **Accessibility.**
  - Situational: one thumb, standing, glare, noise, interruptions. She cannot rely on sound.
  - The main button must sit within thumb reach.
- **A good day.** "Four minutes, one hand, done before Diva. The shift-lead one was exactly my ward, and the game took it seriously."
- **Where EVAL-13 lost her.**
  - The main button floated wherever the content ended.
  - Some screens asked for the same answer twice: tap a card, then a separate confirm bar.
  - Long scrolls [I4, diag-screens].
- **The one design implication.** **Play that survives interruption:** resume exactly where she left off, no timers, one-thumb reach, the main action in the same place on every screen. Scoring both ways must show as "the same rule for everyone" through play, never as "favouring women is bad".

## Who these personas leave out, and what the design must still hold

Six personas cannot cover everyone. These groups are not drawn as personas, but the design must not fail them, and the playtests should recruit them.

- **Dalit and Adivasi players.** Caste enters in later levels [I2], and some scenes will touch village and community life (P4).
  - The game never asks a player's caste, religion or community.
  - Scenes where caste appears are reviewed by people from the communities shown, as well as by the owner (**ASSUMPTION** about process, for the owner to confirm).
  - The cast includes Dalit and Adivasi characters in ordinary roles, not only in scenes about caste.
- **Non-binary and transgender players.** Road names are truck-driver style and many are gendered.
  - The name lists mix feminine, masculine and neutral names, and the player picks from 3 without being asked their gender (journeys, S4).
  - The app's own voice never assumes the player's gender, in English or Hindi (ux-writing digest, UXW-35).
  - Whether scenarios ever move beyond woman and man pairs is a content question for the owner, not settled here (**ASSUMPTION** that it is open).
- **Screen-reader users.** No persona uses TalkBack, although the usability digest designs for it (UA-23 to UA-29). Its testing rule (UA-44) asks for one or two sessions with a TalkBack user.
- **Players with motor or cognitive disabilities.** No persona has one. The step model (usability digest, section 2.0), the tap alternative to every gesture (UA-9) and the game accessibility checklist (UA-45) stand in for them until playtests include such players.
- **Players of other Indian languages.** P1 and P5 wait for Kannada and Tamil. Languages beyond English and Hindi come later [I2].

## Anti-personas (we design against these, not for them)

### A1. Kunal, the board farmer
*A 22-year-old who wants to be number one by any means.*

- **What he does.**
  - Runs several accounts and taps through as fast as possible.
  - Copies answers from a Telegram group. Tries scripts.
  - Hunts for any way to put a slur or his real handle into a public name.
- **Why it matters.** A board that he tops tells every real player (above all P2) that the board is fake, and they leave.
- **Design response, from the research line [I1, section 4.5].**
  - Road names are generated, never typed.
  - Names, twin assignment and order vary per player, so shared answers do not help.
  - The practice score rewards careful play, not speed or volume.
  - Pattern checks catch impossibly fast play and same-answer play.
  - Nothing of money value is ever awarded.
  - **Proposed, not decided:** the research proposes keeping new accounts off the Everyone board for their first 14 days. That changes "one global board, all players", so it is an owner question (OQ-23).
- **What not to do.** Do not punish fast, honest players. Never publish a bottom list or shame anyone in public [I1, section 4.3].

### A2. Sandeep, the hostile screenshotter
*A 38-year-old active in "men's rights" pages (or, equally, a page hostile to "Western feminism") who plays to find a clip.*

- **What he does.**
  - Plays a few days to screenshot a scenario, a word or a result that "proves" the NGO thinks men are bad, or that it attacks family values.
  - Posts it out of context.
  - May mass-report or review-bomb.
- **Why it matters.**
  - One bad clip reaches P2's and P5's WhatsApp groups before the game does.
  - Mandatory or preachy messages already drive denial [I1, Jin, Fitzsimons and Kay 2026].
- **Design response.**
  - Every screen has to make sense alone, out of context.
  - Twins and mirrored cases show that the rule cuts both ways.
  - No result or share card ever states a direction.
  - No moralising copy anywhere.
  - Every scenario passes the owner's content review [I2].
- **What not to do.** Do not soften the dowry, custody and safety content the owner chose to address head on [I3]. Make it fair and precise instead.

## Who is not a player but shapes play

- **Workplace HR or POSH lead.**
  - Employers must run regular awareness workshops under section 19(c) of the POSH Act 2013 [S29], so HR will want to send the game round and may want proof of completion.
  - Mandatory training raises pushback and denial [I1, Jin, Fitzsimons and Kay 2026; Legault et al. 2011], and real identities are never shown [I2]. So:
    - the game cannot become a compliance tool;
    - anything we give HR is a link and a poster, never a per-person report.
  - The colleague HR sends it to is P1 or P6 in a bad mood.
- **College gender cell, NSS or student-society coordinator.**
  - Shares it into class WhatsApp groups and may run a challenge week.
  - First-year groups include 17-year-olds, and children are out of scope until a lawyer advises on DPDP s.9 [I2]. Sharing kits must not aim at under-18 groups.
- **People who share the phone.**
  - 18% of Indian women internet users only borrow someone else's phone [S3].
  - Husbands, mothers-in-law and children (P3, P4, P6) will see whatever shows on the home screen, in a notification or in the Sunday note.
  - A child may pick up a phone that has the game on it. The game is for adults, and it must not treat a child's play as normal. The designed case is in [journeys.md](journeys.md) (OQ-17).
- **Equal Lens facilitators and partner NGOs.** May suggest the game after a workshop. They need one sentence about what it is, not a manual.
- **The owner, as content reviewer.** Reviews every scenario [I2]. Every persona's trust depends on that review holding.
- **Funders and judges (MIT Solve 2027).** Read the claims. The honest limit is change inside the game on material the player has never practised, not behaviour change outside it [I2].

## What all personas share

1. **Phone first, one thumb, short.** All play on Android or iOS phones between other things, in 5 to 7 minutes. Two-thirds of gamers are said to live outside large metros [S6, secondary, UNVERIFIED]. Women players mostly live outside metros too [S4].
2. **They judge a screen at a glance, and reading is a cost.** The owner's rule holds for all six: one question of 10 words or fewer, and 15 words on a puzzle screen [I5, minimal-text-ui]. Only P1 reads fast, and she still wants no clutter.
3. **Everyone believes they are already fair.** The blind spot is universal [I1, Pronin and Hazel 2023]. The player's own locked rules must be the answer key, never the game's opinion [I1].
4. **Nobody wants to be lectured, labelled or shamed.** The owner's rules are "never shame", "always show scores" and size only, never direction [I4, diag-feedback].
5. **Play is voluntary, and it competes with 2 to 3 other games** [S4]. Pushback rises the moment it feels assigned [I1].
6. **WhatsApp is how it spreads** [S28], and every share must be safe to land in a family group.
7. **They need to stop and resume.** Customers, stations, children, chores, a dropped signal. There are no timers anywhere [S30, WCAG 2.2.1].
8. **Privacy is the default.** Real identities never show [I2]. The lean is private. The Sunday note is private.
9. **None of them would have stayed past the first screens of EVAL-13.** The invented screens (language, road name, Today hub, result, board) had no approved look, and they were the first thing every persona met [I4, diag-synthesis].

## Where they conflict (design tensions)

Each tension is described in plain words, with no decision made here. Where an owner decision is needed, the tension points to the single list in [journeys.md](journeys.md).

1. **One shared puzzle against different starting points.** P1 and P2 want hard puzzles from day one. P3 and P5 need a gentle first week. The owner chose one daily puzzle on one track [I3]. What a newcomer plays on a hard day is OQ-19.
2. **Competition against reflection.** P2 lives for the board. P3, P4 and P6 want a private ritual. The board ranks practice [I2], so it must not reward simply playing more or faster, or P2 will tune it and A1 will farm it. The board's reset and who appears on it are OQ-22 and OQ-23.
3. **Which Hindi.** P2, P3 and P4 want everyday Hindi in Devanagari. P2 and P6 type Hinglish in Latin letters. P5 wants none of it. The owner's rule is to show only the chosen language [I4, diag-feedback]. Whether Hinglish in Latin letters is ever a language option is OQ-28.
4. **Scoring both ways.** P2 welcomes it, because it protects men too. P6 may read it as false balance. Whether legal corrective rules such as reservations appear in scenarios is OQ-29 [I2]. The way the game shows symmetry (twins, mirrored cases) decides which way this lands.
5. **Size without direction.** P1 and P5 will want to know which way they lean. The owner chose size only, so players reflect [I3]. The risk is frustration and quitting, so the Sunday "What was different?" moment has to carry the work the direction would have done. How the lean is drawn is OQ-1; how open the game is about what it hides is OQ-14.
6. **How directly to show dowry, custody and safety.** For P3, P4 and P6 directness is validating, and it may also be painful. For P2 and P5 it may feel accusatory. A2 is waiting for a clip. The owner chose to address these head on and to review all content [I3]. The usability digest sets trauma-informed handling (UA-46), and a help line on those days is OQ-30.
7. **Poster art against old phones and large text.** The approved Lugdi look is a full-screen painting with plates on top [I4, diag-screens]. P3's 3 GB phone, P4's 2 GB phone and P5's 200% text all push against heavy images and fixed frames. The art has to load light and the layout has to reflow, or the look will be cut again, as it was in EVAL-13. The tint over the painting is OQ-11.
8. **Sharing against exposure.** P1 and P2 want a card to post. P3, P4 and P6 share phones or homes. Share is always opt-in, and the card shows only the day, the puzzle object and the streak, never a lean.
9. **When a day ends.** P2 plays after midnight, P6 after night shifts, and 58% sleep after 11 pm [S20]. A midnight reset breaks their streaks even with one forgiven day a week [I2]. This is OQ-15.
10. **iPhone reminders and lost data.** On iOS, a web app can only ask for push permission once it has been added to the Home Screen [S31]. Daily reminders for P1 depend on that install step, which the first-run flow has to earn rather than demand. Keeping a player's data through a long gap or a new phone is OQ-18.
11. **Spreading through institutions against voluntary play.** HR and colleges are the fastest route to players, and also the surest way to make play feel mandatory [I1]. The share kit must keep the choice with the player.
12. **A weak signal against a fair, shared puzzle.** P4 needs the game to work offline. Saving future puzzles on the phone would also let them be read and shared early. This is OQ-27.
