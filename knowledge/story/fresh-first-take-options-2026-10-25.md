# Retake: a fresh First take, told as a webtoon
**These are options for you to choose from. Nothing here is decided, built or painted.** The questions only you can answer are at the end.

On 25 Oct you said Dadi's trunk is not working, and asked for three things: scenarios everyone goes through, like the shaadi demo, with ground rules for the First take and the dailies; a story where the characters talk to each other, you are one of them, and each chapter ends with what came of your decisions; and webtoon panels, painted with no letters, with every word laid on top by the app. Two research rounds followed. The first wrote five story directions and had three judges score each. The second wrote one sample chapter two ways (a scrolling strip and turned pages), had two judges score them, and checked every panel in the app today for painted letters.

Two words used throughout. Every decision has a **mirror decision** later in the same chapter: the same kind of choice with two different people who match exactly, and the loaded line sits on the other one, the woman in one half and the man in the other. That is how the game reads a lean. The matched woman and man are **the equal two**.

# Part A. The story
## A1. Why Dadi's trunk is not working
**You never have a place of your own**: you make a 1971 station master's calls, a 1977 truck owner's, a 1984 magician's, and Dadi made none of them herself. **You need to know the trade**: crewing trains over a ghat, hazardous-goods licences, a club's second eleven. **Too many strangers, nothing at stake**: 62 named people in about 20 minutes, 17 different pairs of numbers to decode, and each decision is a roster call, not a step towards fixing anything. **History lets players off**: unfairness set in 1971 is easy to file as "back then".

What the shaadi demo got right, and every option keeps: a present-day reason to decide ("Mummy has made YOU in charge"); people you get to know; each day one small crisis with a goal and a number ("We need 19 off six balls"); numbers anyone can read; a short outcome at each day's end; and the album, "Here's who you trusted with what", just before the reading. What it got wrong, and every option drops: the group chat; relatives as the equal two again and again, so liking a face shows up as a lean; jokes on people who sit in the equal two; outcomes that blame someone ("Beta... who counted this??").

Three judges, each out of 10: **players** (would our six players, from the village banking agent to the 61-year-old retired bank officer, recognise their own life in it in the first minute?), **measure** (does it still read a lean without giving the equal two away?) and **making** (can we paint and build it with what we have?).

The biggest weakness of each, as the judges saw it:
- **Dadi's Calendar** starts with its least familiar chapter (a colony match with women and men on one side, which village and small-town players have watched but never played) and ends with its heaviest (harassment inside your own family, at Dadi's party, just before the private reading). One young cousin says every doubting line, so her face becomes the clue.
- **Five Ordinary Days** makes every player a shop employee in the first minute; its unnamed festival will read as Diwali; and a player who reads only the first number gets no reading in a third of its sample decisions.

- **Naam Likh Diya** has two chapters nobody has lived (a colony exam help desk, running nine staff in your first week), and "your first job" makes every player young. Easiest to build, lowest with the players' judge.
- **Tum Dekh Lo** spends four of five chapters on one wedding, which you ruled out as the frame for a whole take, and puts the household into every world's look: up to seven paintings per face.

## A5. Ground rules in brief
The full draft rulebook is [ground-rules-draft-2026-10-25.md](ground-rules-draft-2026-10-25.md). These are the core, with every change to an earlier decision marked.

**First take**
1. Present day only: each chapter is something nearly every adult in India has lived or watched at home, checked by readers standing in for our six players. Chapter 1 is the most familiar of all.
2. One household's year: five chapters, five events, a wedding one chapter at most. Each chapter has one goal with numbers, set as a short list at the start, and every decision is a step towards it.
3. You are one of the characters: everyone talks to you, nobody paints you, and no word fixes your gender or age **(changes an earlier decision: today the player is an unnamed decider with no part in the story)**. Characters may say तुम to you, in forms that mark no gender; the game's own voice keeps आप. Every line works in Hinglish, Hindi and English.
4. A small household speaks, asks and jokes, is never the equal two, and is the one exception to "one person, one world, one role" **(changes an earlier decision)**. Everyone else appears in one chapter only. Jokes and quirks belong to the household; card notes are plain facts, the same length, in the chapter's own paperwork. Remarks about card people come from at least two voices a chapter, a woman's and a man's, spread across different mirrors and never swapping inside one, and nobody in the household ever doubts a report of being followed or a dowry demand.
5. One hard topic per chapter, growing out of the event, shown as one line on one card and never as the harm itself; never harm to a child; the heaviest never last **(changes an earlier decision: today the home chapter carries two)**.
6. The five worlds become the look of each chapter, not five places in five decades **(changes an earlier decision)**.
7. Chapters follow the story's own order **(changes an earlier decision: today work always comes first)**.
8. No festivals, rituals or religious symbols in the First take; a national day or a birthday instead **(changes an earlier decision: today a festival week may appear unnamed, as a full house and a big meal)**. No brands and no city-only details.
9. Ties between two women or two men appear too, so a tie alone never marks the equal two. Proposed (the measure judge wants it required); the research side checks it before it counts.

**Dailies**
1. Three a day (Easy, Medium, Hard): one painting of one everyday moment, three decisions in it.
2. Narrower moments are fine (the water tanker, hiring help), as long as every player meets at least two days a week from their own life. Festivals may be named if they rotate across communities.
3. Every daily still works for a player who knows how the game reads them **(an earlier decision; this corrects a draft Easy rule that broke it by making the only tie the answer)**. The proposed way to keep it is First take rule 9: ties between two women or two men too, so the equal two are not the only tie. Like rule 9, that counts only once the research side has checked it.
4. At most one hard-topic day a week, never on a Monday. A mirror's two halves come at the same level a week apart, in a new scene with new people.
5. Once a First take decision is used (who keeps the envelope box), that exact decision stays out of the dailies for a year, not the whole kind of day **(defines an earlier decision)**.
6. The household may ask a daily's question as a voice, but never sits on a daily card, and the direction of a lean never shows in a daily.

# Part B. The webtoon presentation
## B1. You are never drawn
Four ways were weighed. **Seen from behind, or as hands**: hair, sleeves, bangles, a watch and skin tone give away gender, age and class, and the painter's habits would choose them (you already caught three hands on one figure). **A look you choose**: every panel needs a version per look, and picking a look (usually a gender) just before a game that reads gender leans primes exactly what is measured. **A fixed named character**: players of another gender or age watch someone else decide, and you already ruled that Tara is never the player. **Never shown** works best: characters look out of the panel and talk to you, and things are held out towards you. The shaadi demo did this, the team found it the easiest to follow, and two of today's panels already have Dadi looking straight out at the viewer.

Three rules come with it:
1. You are "you" or your job ("in charge", इंचार्ज), never a word that fixes gender or age.
2. Every Hindi line to or from you marks no gender, checked by the export and by a native reader. One line in today's script (Dadi's "फ़ैसले तुम करोगे") and one in the longer Dadi's-trunk opening you asked for on 23 Oct ("आप तय करेंगे कौन", "you choose who") already make the player a man.
3. Your own line is one tap, never a choice of replies and never measured. Its tail runs off the bottom edge, and it is never yellow, since yellow means only your pick and the button that confirms it.

## B2. How a chapter reads: the judges tied, so the choice is yours
Both forms were written on the same sample chapter and mocked up in the app's own styles. **The scroll strip**: one long strip per chapter; you scroll, or tap Next to move one panel; at each decision the strip stops until you choose. **The paged form**: the story turns like comic pages, one or two panels a tap, and each decision gets its own full screen after a one-panel lead-in.

| | Clarity judge (can a first-time player follow it?) | Safety judge (does it hide the equal two, and is it safe to build?) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scroll strip | 7.5 | 6 | 13.5 |
| Paged form | 6.5 | 7 | 13.5 |

**The strip is clearer for players.** It reads most like the webtoon you described: you are "you" from the first picture, your own phone buzzing; the story keeps moving between decisions (the auto pulls away, "phat phat"); and you can scroll up to reread. What it costs: the bigger rewrite, since every decision screen moves into one long scroll (about 7 to 8 days of build after the parts sheet, 3 of them for the bubbles and their placement page); on a laptop, panels sit in a column over a full-screen painting, not full screen as you asked on 24 Oct; scroll snapping on cheap Android phones is untested; the trick of hanging a bubble into the gap above would cover the panel above at today's narrow gaps; how bubbles appear, and how a screen reader is kept from reading ahead into the cards, is not yet specified; its sample was at 85 Hindi words after two decisions, heading for about 145 against a cap of 120; it counted 11 to 13 story panels a chapter, nearer 20 once each decision has its own asking panel; and its outcome table was unfinished (one job fell after the lunch it was meant to feed). Its first outcome rule also let the equal two add up to the top score, which teaches players to add the numbers and turns the pick into a toss-up between a woman and a man. Taking the paged form's rule fixes that one; the safety judge's other two objections, the size of the build and the laptop pictures, stand.

**The paged form is safer to build.** It keeps today's one screen per decision (about 7 days of build counted from your yes, 2 of them for the parts sheet, so about 5 after it), pictures stay full screen on a laptop, every line is read in order, and its ending was the more earned: goals set at the start, checked at the end. What it costs: it feels like a visual novel (lead-in, cards, lead-in, cards, eight times over); on the smallest phones the bubbles drop under the picture, like a chat under a photo; and there is no "this is your phone" moment at the start. **Length**: today's First take is planned at about 20 minutes. The strip estimates about 23 to 24, on a panel count the safety judge found too low; the paged form about 27. They were timed differently (per panel against per page), so the gap is rough.

**A middle way the references suggested** (listed as an option to weigh; neither judge scored it): a single column that moves on by tap; bubbles appear one per tap, in reading order; the part already read stays scrollable above; and each decision is a stop in the strip, its question in the last panel's bubble with the cards under it. It keeps the strip's familiar scroll and its gaps that show time passing, and the paged form's promise that the asking line is read. Its cost is more taps per scene, so it needs a cap on bubbles per scene and a rule for what a tap does on a panel with three bubbles.

**Whichever you choose**, each form borrows the other's best parts: a list of targets at the start, handed over by the asker, that comes back stamped at the end (B3); a story clock in the top bar on every decision ("9:40 pm, the junction"); one lead-in per scene, not per decision, and after each pick an "it happened" panel that is the same whatever you picked; opening on your own phone; polite Hindi to elders ("लिस्ट दिखाइए", not "लिस्ट दो"); and the strip's people page (names only, everyone the same kind of person, a new order on every load).

**Bubbles**: speech, shout, a narration box, a sound word, a message on a painted blank phone, a sign on a painted blank surface, and your "You" bubble. All are live text, so one painting serves all three languages. Hindi bubbles come out taller, so the app sizes each one from its text. **Keeping the equal two hidden**: no bubble names, points at or describes anyone on that decision's cards; praise, doubt and gossip stay in card notes; both halves of a mirror have the same asker, the same number of bubbles and word counts within 2; no reaction to a pick until the chapter ends; card people are never painted into story panels, only laid on as small portraits of one size. The export refuses most breaks, and the rest are checked by eye on the contact sheet.

**Words** (a proposal): today speech is not counted, but in a webtoon nearly every word is speech. Count by tap: a story beat at most 20 counted words, a decision at most 15, the outcome at most 20, a chapter's story about 120, each language counted separately. Both judges found 120 tight: about 130 for the paged opening and lead-ins, about 145 Hindi words in the strip. The bubble spec caps a spoken line at 10 words and both forms used 12 (question 18).

**Must fix before the parts sheet**:
- From the clarity judge: Hinglish, the default, shows English sentences, so the language choice must come on or before the cover; a decision with a note on every card does not fit the smallest phones without scrolling; bubble text needs a 16 px floor for older eyes; every family voice needs a name tag in chapter 1, since faces drift between paintings.
- From the safety judge: someone who looked weak earlier in a chapter is never later one of the equal two; story lines are scanned for words from the next decision's notes (a "big car", "Pune"); the lead-in paintings of a mirror's two halves match; each saved bubble position carries a fingerprint of its picture, so a repaint cannot silently move heads under old bubbles; and someone labels which face is which speaker.

## B3. What a chapter's outcome looks like
At the start the asker hands you a list of targets in the same words as the card numbers. In blend chapter 1, Rakesh's list might read "Every berth to someone on their feet 9 hours or more" and "Every berth to someone who slept 5 hours or less", both worked out from the berth-holders' own card numbers. Anything the story's fixed ending decides stays off the list. At the end, after your last choice in the chapter (both halves of every mirror sit inside it):
1. The arrival painting, the same for everyone, with a live stamp in the chapter's look.
2. The same list, stamped: "On their feet 9 hours or more: 10 of 10 berths, ticked"; "Slept 5 hours or less: 8 of 10 berths". A missed target is never ticked.
3. One warm family reaction to how many targets were met, about the event, never a person.
4. The story's own ending, the same for everyone ("Ammaji had cooked for twenty. She slipped carrying the last plate."), which leads into chapter 2.

The figures come only from the numbers of the people you picked, never from notes. On a decision with the equal two, the bar sits at or below their own numbers, so either clears it. A new check makes sure swapping the equal two never changes a word, number, picture or sound, and that "always the woman" and "always the man" end the same; a second makes sure the cards that miss a bar are not mostly women or mostly men. Never shown: names, a row of your picks, who did well, right or wrong, or anything that proves a note true or false. The album with names comes only at the very end, opens with a tap, and leads straight into the private reading. Two open points for the research side: a decision where a real detail matters gets a fixed result by default, which may teach that details never matter; and outcomes that reward strong numbers could push players away from the equal two in later takes, where they sometimes tie for last place.
