# Retake ground rules (second draft, for your yes)

**Your answers, 25 Oct 2026** (they override anything below that says otherwise):
1. "Score the blend": the blend goes to the same three judges and the checks.
2. "22-30 year old from the house": the player is the grown-up child of the house, aged 22 to 30. This replaces rule 8's "no relation or stage of life". The player is still never painted, and still has no gender, caste or religion.
3. "Scroll strip": a chapter reads as one scrolling strip (question 1).
4. "Just the look": the five worlds become only the look of each chapter (question 2). This changes your 22 Oct call that every world has its own story.
5. "Give me the story to evaluate instead and mark whichever beats break the rules": you will judge the rules through the written story, with each beat that breaks a rule marked, rather than line by line here.

**Your answers, 26 Oct 2026**, after reading Saal Bhar draft 1 (they override anything below):
6. Characters as comics do it: "giving characters a unique visual identity that stays consistent across boards". The first job is "to define each character in depth, how they would talk, how they would type, the words they'll use, the clothes they'll wear, their postures everything". This answers the question of the household face-on in each look: characters are drawn, from their own model sheets.
7. The First take is the game's **story mode**, and the dailies are **daily challenges**. "If the story is good enough and experience fun enough people will play and they always have the option to do it in multiple sittings." No time cap: this replaces rule 50's "about 4 minutes" a chapter and its tap ceiling. Paint by scene, not by decision. "It should be entirely story driven. Right now, story is still our weakest aspect."
8. "There's no cap on number of characters... just make them real and memorable." This replaces rule 11's 8 to 10 people a chapter and "no quirks", and lifts the earlier worry about too many names.
9. "Bias is most visible in jobs that most can do. If we ask for a male nurse or a female truck driver, everyone gets a cop out by saying that's how world works." Cards carry jobs most people can do. This replaces rule 11's "against type at least as often as with it".

One rule per line, in nine sections, then the questions only you can answer, then how the clashes were settled. Nothing here is built. Tick, change or strike each line.

Every rule ends with where it comes from, in three kinds:
- **you decided**: you said so, with the date and, where we have them, your own words. Only this kind means you said so.
- **house rule**: set by the team in our design files or drafts, never put to you as a question. Some sit inside drafts you read or the demo you signed off on 22 Oct, but a yes to a draft or a demo is not counted as a yes to each rule in it.
- **new**: from the two research rounds of 25 Oct. It waits for your yes.

Where a rule would change something you decided, it says "this changes your ...", and your call stands until you answer.

Words used throughout:
- **The equal two**: the hidden woman and man on a decision's cards who match on every number.
- **The nudge**: the one note that might pull a player towards or away from one of the two. On a Hard daily it may instead be two notes, one on each of the two (rule 55).
- **A twin**: a later decision of the same kind with a different equal two and the nudge on the other person (a two-note nudge swaps sides). Picking the woman both times, or the man both times, is what shows a lean.
- **Plain, hard and "matters" twins**: in a plain twin the nudge should not matter (a loaded word); a hard twin carries a hard topic (a dowry hint); in a "matters" twin the nudge truly matters to the task (only one holds a driving licence), so following it both times is fair.
- **A beat**: one picture and the words on it, everything new between two taps. **A lead-in**: the story picture just before a decision.
- **The household**: the 4 to 8 people the story is about (rule 9). **The circle**: a chapter's people on the cards (rule 11).
- **Our six players**: the village banking agent, the small-town tailor, the shop-counter worker, the metro puzzle player, the 61-year-old and the nurse, from the product brief.

## 1. Everyday-ness

1. A First take event, and every decision in it, passes only if real people like each of our six players say "I have lived this, or watched it at home"; the writer's own guess does not count. A daily may be narrower (an office deadline, the water tanker, the family shop) if each of the six meets that kind of day at least twice a week in their own life. (you decided, 21 Oct: every rule works for all six players at once; new: the test, from your 25 Oct ask for scenarios everyone goes through)
   - Our predictions, for the readers to confirm or overturn: a night in hospital with someone from the household should pass for all six; crewing a 1971 ghat train should pass for none; "the parents' meeting" should fail anyone without a child in school; "the late shift home" should fail anyone without a job outside the home.
2. Present day and plain: no year on screen, no props from a past era unless the story owns them (the household's old TV), every painting prompt asks for present-day clothes and objects, and no trade words an outsider would not know ("learned the section", "second XI"). Each world keeps its own big moment whatever the answer to question 2, such as Rukavat's "sorry for the interruption" card. (you decided, 21 Oct: Rukavat is named after that card; new: the rest, from your 25 Oct note that the old story's scenarios came from several unfamiliar decades)
3. Write the shape everyone shares, not the ritual: guests, food for a crowd, money, travel, a room, a deadline, a helper who lets you down. No ritual, prayer, religious dress, religious or political symbol, real brand, famous person or community-marked place on screen or in a painting prompt. (you decided: the product brief you confirmed on 21 Oct brings religion in only at later levels, which this book reads as later weeks, rule 56; house rule: no religious symbols in paintings, the story bible; new: the rest)
4. Use details all six can picture: money as a share of pay ("two months' rent"), every city detail with a small-town version, no domestic help at the centre of a scene; a hired cook or caterer is fine, city prices and brands are not. (new)
5. Ask the player only what someone in their place would really be asked: "The household's Bua puts you in charge of the wedding week" works; a passenger choosing which strangers' complaints the ticket checker hears does not. (new)
6. A situation is four things together: the moment (the wedding week), the decision's role (who keeps the gift register), the place (the gift table) and the nudge. No situation, nudge wording or set of numbers is ever used twice, and a record of every one used lets the build refuse a repeat; a role may come back in a new place with a new nudge. First take decisions never become dailies, and their situations stay out of the dailies for a year; events such as weddings or train journeys stay open. (house rule: nothing used twice and the year's lock, the First take draft; new: what a situation is, and roles coming back, since 18 dailies a week would use up every role in about two months)

## 2. The player and the people

8. The player is the grown-up child of the house, aged 22 to 30, the one the household turns to, with no name, gender, caste or religion. Nobody calls the player by a word that fixes gender (bhaiya, didi, beta), only "you", the role ("in charge") or a word every family uses for a daughter or a son alike. Your 25 Oct ask that the player be one of the characters changes your 22 Oct call that the player is an unnamed decider with no part in the story. (you decided, 25 Oct: "22-30 year old from the house"; house rule: the player is never given a gender, the house writing rules)
9. The household: 4 to 8 people met on one screen in the opening, each with a plain relation label ("Bua, the family's aunt"); quirks and jokes belong to them alone and are shown in the panels, never written on that screen. While each member speaks in one chapter only (rule 10), the household needs at least five who can ask, 3 and 2 by gender, one for each chapter (rule 37), plus any comic one, and a joke stays inside its own chapter; jokes that run across chapters wait on question 11. (you decided, 23 Oct: a slightly wider cast; new: the numbers and drawn quirks)
10. The household are the voices who ask, gossip and joke, and are never one of the equal two. Being heard in every chapter changes your 23 Oct call that each person keeps one world and one role: they would keep one role and one present-day look (rule 33) but speak in every chapter's world. Until you answer question 11, your call stands: each household member speaks in one chapter's world only, the present-day opening and album aside, so the household is at least five (rule 9), each chapter has its own asker (rule 37), and in the dailies a member asks only in the week of their own world (rule 61). If you allow cards too, at most one household member in the whole take sits on one: not an elder, in a rank, team, batting order or split, in the same seat in both halves of one plain twin, with numbers between the strongest card's and the equal two's, and a neutral note. (you decided, 23 Oct: one person, one world, one role; house rule: the family-on-cards rules, still waiting on the research check; new: the exception, "the whole take" and "no elders"; waits on question 11)
11. Each chapter's circle is as many people as the story needs, half women and half men, all the same kind of relation to the household (all cousins of one side, or all neighbours), each real and memorable, met together as their card faces (faces at least 56 px, names at body size), in a new order each time. Their jobs are ones most people can do (shop counter, clerk, cook for a crowd, driving a car, teaching), never a job the world types by gender, so no pick can be explained away as "that's how the world works". (you decided, 26 Oct: "no cap on number of characters... make them real and memorable" and "Bias is most visible in jobs that most can do", which replaces the house rule of going against type; new: the rest)
12. Everyone on a card, speaking or choosing is 18 or over, in a role a woman or a man could hold in India today that means the same for both; children may be the reason for a choice but never sit on a card, choose or appear at risk. (house rule: adults only, the story rules; new: the "today" test)

## 3. The equal two, twins, notes and decoys

13. Each decision shows 3 to 5 people who look alike before a choice, with exactly one woman and one man matched on every number, portraits within 8 years of age and no second woman-and-man tie; the player picks, then confirms. (you decided, 21 Oct: 3 to 5 people, cards that look the same before a choice, pick then confirm; house rule: within 8 years and only one woman-and-man tie, the First take draft)
14. The equal two really compete for the same place: a player who goes only by the first number, or only by the second, still has to choose between them, and the build refuses any plain or hard decision that fails. (new, from the First take draft and the 25 Oct audit; on 25 Oct you asked to leave the audit's story findings aside for now)
15. The equal two are never the only tie: from chapter 2 on, at least half the decisions also show two women or two men tied, and in rank, team, batting order and split the equal two are the top two in at most half the decisions. (new)
16. Every decision has a twin, in the same chapter in the First take and a week later in the dailies (rule 58): the same kind of choice, a different equal two with no shared person, the nudge on the other one, a new place and task, never a near-copy (no one-word swap, no her-to-his swap). The nudge can be named in one or two words ("the dust") and shows on that kind's screen, never behind a tap. (new, from the First take draft and the 25 Oct audit; on 25 Oct you asked to leave the audit's story findings aside for now)
17. Nothing else that follows gender sits on the same side in both halves (age, who is older, who grew up in the house, who travels). Inside a pair, the two match on how they appeared earlier that chapter: on the same number of cards, as high on the numbers each time (both in the top half of those cards, or both in the bottom half), with notes of the same warmth; nobody who was weakest in an earlier decision sits in a later pair. No pair ever repeats, a person is in at most two equal pairs a chapter and never two decisions running, and a pair's exact numbers never return under the same number names that chapter. (house rule: age, seniority and no repeats, the First take draft; new: the rest)
18. Two everyday numbers per card, named after the task (meals cooked for a crowd, times late this month), the same number names in the same order with one key, on a range wide enough (say 0 to 20) to tie two people without a stray second tie. In the First take, at most two sets of number names a chapter plus one for decisions about a report, whose numbers bear on it (times proved right), and at most about 8 sets in the whole take, each with its icon and Hindi word drawn before it is used. (house rule: the same names and one key, the house number rules; new: the rest)
19. Every card carries one short note in the setting's own paperwork. A decision's notes are within 2 words of each other, the equal two's notes are the same length, and the longest note falls on the two in a quarter to a half of decisions. The two's notes never share an opening word, a sentence frame (the same words but for one or two slots: "Late twice this month", "Late once this week") or a word-for-word serious fact, unless a card outside them does too. (you decided, 23 Oct: a note on every card in each world's paperwork, ideas A and B, and "For quotes, we are falling in the trap that the longest quotes are the right one"; house rule: within 2 words, equal length, longest on the two at most half the time; new: the quarter floor and the frame rule)
20. Decoys are the same kind as the nudge: every decision has at least one card outside the equal two with a note of the nudge's kind (a family worry beside a family worry). Every chapter, and every hard-topic day, puts a heavy-sounding fact on a decoy ("lost a parent last winter"). A reason that truly matters (holds a driving licence, saw it happen) sits only on the equal two of a "matters" twin, one a chapter in the First take, never on a decoy. (you decided, 23 Oct: yes to decoy remarks; house rule: one "matters" twin a chapter, the First take draft; new: the rest)
21. Names are first names or what the family calls someone (Bua, Mama), never surnames or caste titles, mixed across communities in ordinary roles, and tagged in one list the build reads. Inside a pair both names mark the same community or neither does, and community-marked names sit in pairs, on decoys, at the top and at the bottom as often as their share. (house rule: first names, the mixed cast and no hard fact on a community-marked name, the people rules and the First take draft; new: the tags and the balance)
22. A judging note ("pushy", "scares easily") is said by a named person with a portrait, never by the game. Gossip about card people appears only as such notes, on at least two cards besides the equal two, and each chapter's judging notes come from at least two people, women and men. No note says anything about women or men in general, and family gossip never carries the doubt about a harassment report or a dowry hint. (house rule: a named speaker and gossip beyond the two, the house writing rules and the family-on-cards rules; new: the rest)
23. Every decision says whether being picked is a gain (the seat, the trip) or a load (the 2 am shift): a load is asked as a split or marked as a chore in the game, and unless all five players asked agree which it is, it stays out of the twins. (new; fixes the chores counted backwards that the 25 Oct audit found)
24. A script checks every rule here that it can count, and the build refuses on a failure; before any link goes to the team, someone plays the whole set. After chapters 1 and 3, five players are asked "What was this about?", and a chapter is rewritten if two or more name the equal two, the same case coming back with the people swapped, a character's line, or who was painted. (house rule: play it through before sending links; new: the playtest bar)

## 4. Hard topics

The hard twin's first half sits at the same decision number in at most two chapters; the chapter opens and closes warm without joking about it; a world's big moment (Rukavat's interruption card) never lands on it; and violence at home, the heaviest topic, never falls in the last chapter (the five Hindi-first players place any new topic against it). (you decided, 22 Oct: "Add hard topics in every chapter... these are adults", and dowry as the home chapter's second hard twin; new: the placement)
26. A hard topic is a situation, never the harm: the title card names the setting plainly before Start, the painting shows only the setting, no line tells violence in detail, there are no puns, the choice is never called harmful, and the topic never reaches a story panel, a bubble, a reminder, the lock screen or a share card. One help line for women and men alike, checked against the official source, sits in the "?" sheet, never on the cover or a card. (you decided, 21 Oct: one national help line in "?"; house rule: the line in every chapter, which followed your 22 Oct hard topics, the care rules, the title card naming the setting, and a line that serves women and men alike)
27. A hard topic arrives as someone else's case the player weighs (a neighbour's report, a cousin's complaint), never the player's own life or the household's, and the household is never the one doing the wrong. (new)
28. A hard fact means the same for a woman and a man: "bullying", not "harassment", at work, and "refused to give dowry" against "took no dowry" fails; every hard pair is read by the five Hindi-first players before it ships. (you decided, 22 Oct: "bullying" at the station, because it means the same for both; house rule: the five-player read; new: the rest)
29. Never harm or danger to a child, never a nudge asking whether an adult is fit to look after a child, no courts or legal claims in inheritance or custody, and the heaviest moments (a death, violence at home) only ever as one decision with the help line. The custody topic you chose on 22 Oct stays; this rule only shapes how it is told. (new)

## 5. Presentation: panels, bubbles and the player's own lines

30. Panels are painted with no words in them: signs, papers and screens are painted blank or shown shut, and every word is live text laid on top, so one painting serves all three languages; Rukavat's lettered slide and Hindi on signs become live text too, saying what they say now. A text check refuses any panel with stray letters unless you allow it with a reason, and your eye is the last check. (new, from your 25 Oct ask for paintings with no lettering in them)
31. Painter rules: a plain wall or sky above the heads, heads 30 to 45% down the picture, the first speaker on the left, no painter's mark, close-ups only for one short line, two or three people a panel at most, and each painting under 60 KB for 2 GB phones, reused under new words where it can. (house rule: 60 KB a painting; new: the rest)
32. Card people are never painted into panels; if the story needs them, small copies of their card pictures sit on top, all the same size. No painted face sits behind the cards, and outcome pictures show the event (the ball, the guests arriving), not the person picked. (house rule: card people never in the story pictures, the story rules; new: the rest)
33. The household is painted only in the present-day style, from reference pictures. Each member has one present-day portrait, used on every card and every note they carry. In a world's panels they are seen from behind or speak from off the panel, always with a name tag. Recurring objects (the list, the keys) are painted from one reference picture. Card faces come from the existing portrait library, each keeping one name for good, and every new face passes the close-up check for painted bindis and lightened skin. (new)
34. Speech bubbles are drawn by the app and sized from the text, never painted. Their text is at your 15 px body size with Hindi at line height 1.5, at 7:1 contrast, with Hindi given 1.5 times the room; bubbles never cover a face, tails never end on a card person, a name tag sits on every bubble in chapter 1 and on any voice from off the panel, and who is speaking is shown by tag, side and tail, never by colour alone. The research asked for 16 px and 1.6 in bubbles: 16 px would change your 22 Oct call on 15 px, and 1.6 would undo the 1.5 we set after your note (question 13). (you decided, 22 Oct: the type scale with 15 px body text, "go ahead"; house rule: Hindi at 1.5, which we set after your 22 Oct note "more top bottom spacing than required", 7:1 contrast and text that never shrinks; new: the rest)
35. Bubble kinds are speech, whisper, shout (once a chapter at most), thought (household only), a narration box for time and place only, the player's line, sound words and signs on blank surfaces. Bubbles look untappable, and a tap on one does what Next does. Each face on a panel is labelled by hand with its speaker, and each bubble is placed by hand on its painting, with a second set for laptops; a repaint stops the build until its faces are labelled and its bubbles placed again. (new)
36. Before and during a decision, no bubble, caption or sign names, points at, describes, or uses the note words of anyone on that decision's cards ("Pune", "the baby"); decoys stay as card notes, not bubbles. This parks your idea D (a fact told earlier in the story) until the research check, because any line about card people splits them into the mentioned and the unmentioned. (you decided, 23 Oct: "The message in the chat bubble and then on each card feels repetitive", and "I like A, B and D"; house rule: the speaker never names or repeats a card, which we wrote after your note; new: the note-word check and keeping decoys on the cards)
37. The ask: one asker per chapter, from the household, never on its cards, never the comic one, with askers 3 and 2 by gender across the five chapters; while question 11 is open, these are five different people. The question sits in the asker's bubble at the question's fixed place, drawn so it cannot pass for a card or a button; on a hard decision it sits in the plain question box instead, never in a bubble. It gives only the goal of the moment ("The mandi shuts at eleven"), never the number the two tie on. Both halves of a twin are asked by the same person, in the same number and shape of bubbles, words within 2, over a lead-in with the same figures and mood. (house rule: one speaker per chapter who is never a card, the First take draft, and the scene-only rule we wrote after your 23 Oct note; new: the rest)
38. The player's own lines are fixed, the same whatever they will pick: no rule for choosing ("experience first"), no card names, never agreeing or disagreeing with a remark. 40. (you decided, 22 Oct: 5:4 portraits, with the overflow the design file records as accepted; house rule: no scrolling on decisions at 100% text; new: the Hindi measure, the fallbacks and ending the overflow; waits on question 16)

(you decided, 23 Oct: results close each chapter, and on 25 Oct you asked for an outcome at each chapter's end; new: what the outcome shows, which waits on question 5)
43. (new)
44. The outcome comes from the numbers only: notes, nudges, decoys, a reason that matters and hard decisions add nothing, so swapping the equal two changes nothing visible. The build tries every answer and refuses on any difference, and "always the woman" and "always the man" must end the same. (you decided, 23 Oct: a result never depends on which of the equal two was picked; new: the check)
45. Across a chapter the cards that miss a bar are as often men as women, and the "matters" decision has a fixed outcome. (new)

It never blames anyone, never says right, wrong, fair, failed or well done, never praises one card person and never proves a note true or false. It names nobody: a numbers line carries the job and the number ("Station run: 12 years driving, on time"). (you decided, 21 Oct: no shaming; house rule: results never label the player, and feedback on choices is never funny; new: the rest)
