# Everyday realism for the Saal Bhar cast

Written for the step that defines each character in depth (owner, 26 Oct 2026: "how they would talk, how they would type, the words they'll use, the clothes they'll wear, their postures everything"). Nothing here is painted or built. The painter was not run.

## 1. How each generation talks at home

### 1.1 What the research says

**Code-switching changes with age.** A study recorded natural conversation among Hindi-English bilingual women in Dehradun in July 2012, three older speakers (54 to 63) and three younger (24 to 35) [1].
- The younger speakers built most clauses in English (75% English-frame clauses) and switched by whole clauses: a full English clause, then a full Hindi one.
- The older speakers built most clauses in Hindi (68% Hindi-frame) and dropped single English words into them.
- The English words older speakers drop in are mostly nouns (69% to 84% of their insertions), for things that came with English: technology, institutions, work.
- Both groups use the "English verb + kar/ho" frame ("start kar diya", "discuss kar lenge").
- **Weight:** six speakers, all upper middle class and English-medium at university. A small-town shop family will use less English overall than this sample, but the direction (older: Hindi frame plus English nouns; younger: whole English clauses) is the useful part.

**Setting, not just age, moves the language.** An older study of Hindi-English families found that which language is used, and how much switching, depends on domain, status, intimacy and topic [2]. In the Dehradun study, a retired teacher switched to English when the topic was school [1]. So Mummy talks shop in English nouns, Captain sahab talks cricket in English nouns, and nobody switches when scolding Tinku about lunch.

**The three Hindi "you"s.**
- आप is the honorific, the most respect, used for seniors [3]; one primer lists elders, parents, elder siblings and people of respect [4].
- तुम is the middle form: relatives, close friends [3], "normal or familiar" [4].
- तू is intimate and informal, and can also carry contempt; it is used to small children and to gods [3]. The BBC report quoted on Language Log says that in Mumbai's Hindi it takes its closer, warmer sense from Marathi and is not heard as rude, while a commenter adds that it is also how elders talk to children [5]. So the same तू is warm in one city and rude in another.
- जी after a word marks respect; one says "haan ji" or "ji" to a person of respect, not a bare "haan" [4].
- How strictly any family follows this varies. A Delhi woman in a 2023 pronoun row used आप for her parents, her elder brother and strangers, and तू only with her younger brother and classmates [5].

**Pronoun use is quietly gendered.** An essayist describes a pattern she sees in families across India: the wife uses the honorific to her husband, the husband the familiar form to her, and the children copy it, honorific to the father and familiar to the mother. She gives Tamil, Kannada, Telugu and Hindi as examples [6]. **Weight:** one essay, no data; it says so itself. It matters here because a fairness game should not encode that hierarchy in its own household by accident (see 1.3).

**Kinship words carry respect and distance.** Kinship terms go after the name ("Shankar chacha"), separate the mother's side from the father's and the older from the younger, and are used for non-relatives as respect or affection, as in "Kamala mausi" for a mother's friend; the custom has weakened in cities but is still common [8]. The same glossary confirms mama (mother's brother), mami (his wife) and mausi (mother's sister) [8].

**A 21-year-old talks two languages.** A 2025 magazine cover story on Indian Gen Z describes slang that shifts by app, Hinglish mixed with fandom and meme English, regional softeners (a Kannada speaker's "da", like "bro"), and a register with friends that one interviewee calls completely different from how they talk to parents [9]. It also notes that among them "bhai" is used for anyone, though most players would still hear it as male (see 3.2).

### 1.3 Rules of thumb for every speaking line (team judgement)

- **Symmetric respect inside the household.** The player speaks to Mummy and Papa in the same polite form (आप, -इए), and Mummy and Papa speak to each other in the same form (both तुम is the most natural for a couple of their age). This avoids the honorific-to-father, familiar-to-mother pattern [6] being written into the game's own family.
- **What the household calls one another, with no community mark.** Mummy and Mama ji call Nani "Maa" or "Amma"; Papa calls her "Mummy ji"; Mama ji calls his sister "Didi". Avoid "Ammi", "Khala" and "Mamu", which lean towards Muslim families, and "Mataji", which leans devotional. **No source found** for these leanings beyond the kinship glossary [8]; the South Indian and Hindi-first readers (rule 65) should confirm.
- **Fillers that belong to everyone:** arre, accha, haan, chalo, bas, theek hai, suno, dekho. They mark no region, religion or gender. **Team judgement.**
- **Regional speakers show region through words, never spelled accents.** A chapter voice from the south or east may use one real word of their own language if a reader from there signs it off; their Hindi is never spelled to sound funny (see section 7).
- **No line from anyone in the household or the chapter voices doubts a report of harassment or a dowry demand**, jokes about it, or says it was surely a misunderstanding (rule 22). This binds Mausi's talk-over-everyone energy most.

## 2. How each generation types

### 2.1 What the research says

**Roman script dominates informal Hindi online.** On public Facebook pages in 2014, Hindi was written mostly in Roman letters; only a Hindi news page drew more Devanagari words than Roman ones [10]. Romanised Hindi has no fixed spelling (the same word appears several ways) [10]. **Weight:** public posts, 2014, not private chat. **No source found** for script choice in private messages by age.

**Many people do not type at all, and send voice notes.**
- A 2025 long read describes a "non-typing majority", mostly in smaller towns and villages, who talk to their phones; it reports that Indic keyboards still feel clunky and autocorrect gets them wrong [16].
- A 2025 trade piece on a WhatsApp campaign opens with a writer's mother who cannot really type (stiff fingers, small letters, an alphabet that feels foreign) and sends voice notes about recipes and family news instead; the campaign itself pushed voice notes in villages [15].
- **Weight:** journalism and marketing. **No source found** with survey figures on voice notes by age.

**Older adults use messaging every day.** In a 2025 interview study of 19 urban adults aged 55 and over, 17 used WhatsApp daily, alongside YouTube, Facebook and Instagram, for socialising, health information and motivational or spiritual content; one man needed his son's help to set up a call, and one woman said failing eyesight shuts the very old out [17]. A 2018 university release says WhatsApp is very popular among older adults in India [18].

**What older relatives send.**
- A 2021 report describes an aunt of 60 posting motivational quotes, prayer emoji, home remedies, and "good morning", "good afternoon" and "good evening" greetings with pictures of babies and roses [11]. It calls WhatsApp groups the main way relatives stay in touch, and a forum for gossip and forwards [11].
- A 2018 report, citing the Wall Street Journal and Google, says good-morning greetings start before sunrise and peak before 8 am, carry flowers, toddlers and birds, and that one in three Indian smartphone users ran out of storage daily [13].
- A 2018 essay calls the family group the modern annual family gathering, where news of illness, promotions and new babies travels, and says older members expect deference there [12].
- A 2025 piece on sticker culture says families swap glittery good-morning roses instead of texts, and quotes a student whose father sends only stickers of an old film singer while her friends spam memes at 2 am [14].

**How a 21-year-old types.** All caps reads as shouting, proper punctuation as uptight, lowercase as relaxed; stretched letters ("loool", "brooo") soften and warm a message; abbreviations exist because replies have to be quick [9].

### 2.2 What the game can and cannot show (team judgement)

- **No group chat** (owner, 23 Oct). Messages are single messages on a painted blank phone or a message card under the panel (rule 40). The family group can be mentioned, never shown.
- **No brand.** No app name, app colours or "forwarded" label, and no stickers of real singers or actors [14] (rule 3). A sticker is a generic drawn object: a cup of tea, a flower, a thumbs up.
- **Good-morning images: flowers, a sunrise, a cup of tea, a bird** [13]. Many real ones carry gods; ours never do (rule 3).
- **A forward is a small loving habit, never misinformation and never politics.** The research on forwards is mostly about false news [11][18]; the game is not.
- **Folded-hands emoji 🙏.** Real and common from elders [11], and most use it to say thanks or please. It sits close to rule 3's "prayer". Owner's call; until then, let elders use it only after a thank-you.

### 3.2 Saying anything without a gender (Hindi grammar, with sources)

Hindi verbs and adjectives agree in gender with the person or thing they refer to [4], which is why "main aa gaya / aa gayi" gives the speaker away. These shapes do not:
- **Requests and commands**, already in the bible: लिस्ट दिखाइए, चलिए.
- **Offers in the subjunctive**: मैं देखूँ? (bible, rule 66).
- **Transitive past with ने.** In the transitive perfective the verb agrees with the object, not the speaker [7]: मैंने टिकट बुक कर दिया is said the same way by anyone. Keep मैंने explicit so readers see why the verb ends as it does. Rule 66's checker will flag the sentence-final -ा; a native reader clears it.
- **Possessives agree with the thing owned** [4]: तुम्हारा फ़ैसला, तेरी लिस्ट say nothing about the owner.
- **English verbs and adjectives in Hinglish**: "Reached." "Coming." "Done." "Booked." "Main ready hoon." Real Indian texting habit (**no source found**; team observation), and fully gender-free. The checker will flag रेडी हूँ for its -ी; clear it.
- **Impersonal lines**: हो जाएगा, हो गया (bible).
- **Never**: आ रहा/रही हूँ, पहुँच गया/गई, थक गया/गई, करूँगा/करूँगी (rule 66).
- **Address words from Tinku.** Real younger siblings in north India call an older one didi or bhaiya, which the game cannot use (rule 8). Tinku uses no address word, or "oye" and "sun". Not "bro" or "bhai", even though Gen Z uses "bhai" for anyone [9], because most players will hear a boy.
- **Emoji from the player**: faces and objects only (👍 🙂 😂 ✅), never a person emoji, which carries a gender and a skin tone.

## 4. What they wear

### 4.1 What the research says

**Saris and suits.**
- The sari has many drapes; the commonest is the Nivi [23]. In Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Gujarat and Rajasthan the end is worn over the front (sidha pallu), which reads regional [23].
- The salwar kameez, once a Punjab and north-west style, is now worn by women throughout India and across the subcontinent [24].
- A 2024 survey of 270 urban women, most aged 41 to 55 and working, found that most treated the sari as formal or traditional wear and fewer than a fifth as daily wear; younger women saw it as conservative and kept it for occasions [25]. It also notes the sari's link to Hindu wedding trousseaus [25].
- A 2005 report from Delhi: city women were swapping the sari for jeans or the easier salwar kameez, wearing saris for formal occasions, while in the countryside women of all classes still wore them daily [26]. Old (21 years), but it shows the city and small-town split.

**At home.** In Kerala, the long loose nightie is daily home wear for women of all ages, sometimes worn out to the shop; one woman got her 88-year-old mother into nighties [27]. Men in Kerala live in the lungi at home [29]. In Odisha, West Bengal, Bihar and Haryana the lungi is mostly home or night wear for men, and Hindu men generally avoid wearing it on the street [28], so out of doors in the north and east it can read as a community marker.

**Winter.** Chennai sells mufflers, shawls and monkey caps for its mild "winter" [31]; in Bengal the monkey cap is a middle-class winter staple and also a standing joke, worn by comedians in Hindi and Bengali films [30].

**Men's work clothes.** The safari suit was default office wear in government and small business into the late 1990s and is now tied to that era [32]: wrong for the present day.

**Footwear.** Chappals are the most typical foot covering in India, worn by men, women and children of all religions [34]; the rubber hawai chappal is common enough to have its own national standard [33].

**Glasses.** In the national ageing survey (2017 to 18), 48% of adults aged 45 and over reported vision impairment [35]. Most of this cast is over 45, so many wear glasses, some only for reading.

### 4.2 Pan-India defaults (team judgement, from 4.1)

- Women: Nivi-draped cotton or synthetic saris, or salwar suits and kurtas; the dupatta lies across one shoulder or both, never over the head (see 5).
- Men: shirts and trousers, kurtas, T-shirts, track pants at home; no lungi out of doors.
- Young people: T-shirts, jeans, hoodies, a kurti over jeans.
- Feet: chappals, plain sandals, sports shoes.
- Everything plain or simply printed: no letters, no logos (rules 3 and 30; FLUX paints logos onto T-shirts unless told to keep them plain).
- Colours: muted and mixed. Not all-white on an older woman, not saffron and green together, not black from head to foot (see 5 and 7).

## 5. Jewellery and accessories

### 5.1 What carries religious, caste or marital meaning (sourced; keep all of it off screen and out of prompts)

From Pew's national survey of Indian adults, fieldwork 2019 to 2020 [19]:
- **Bindi:** worn by 84% of Hindu women and 78% of Buddhist women, and by some Muslim, Christian and Sikh women; read as faith, adornment or marriage. Hindu women in the North-east wear it less (59%).
- **Head covering outside the home:** Muslim women 89%, Sikh 86%, Hindu 59%; Hindu women in the North (82%) and Central region (84%) against the South (22%).
- **Religious pendants** (amulet, cross, god's image): about half of all adults; Christians 61%.
- **Tilak** (forehead mark on men): 53% of Hindu men, 76% of Brahmin men. **Janeu** (sacred thread across the chest): mostly Brahmin men.
- **Kara** (metal bracelet): 84% of Sikhs. A kara is a steel or iron bangle [22], so any plain steel bangle on anyone reads Sikh.
- **Skullcap:** 84% of Muslim men. **Turban:** 69% of Sikh men. **Beards:** Sikh men 83%, Muslim men 64%, Hindu men 42%. A beard alone marks nothing; a long beard, or a beard with a shaved upper lip, leans towards a community.

Marital and community markers [20][21]:
- in much of north India, sindoor in the hair parting, toe rings and glass bangles mark a married woman;
- Punjabi chooda and Bengali shell-and-coral bangles do the same;
- the mangalsutra or thali (black-bead or thread necklace) is a married woman's necklace in many communities, with Christian (a cross pendant) and Muslim versions too;
- a sindoor-marked parting is applied by the groom at a Hindu wedding.

Add from team knowledge (**no source opened**, readers to confirm): red or orange threads tied on the wrist, black threads on the ankle, rudraksha or tulsi beads, mehendi, a wedding safa (turban) on men.

### 5.2 What is safe (team judgement, within the above)

- A wristwatch with a steel or leather strap (one wrist only).
- Spectacles: reading glasses, half-moon or full frames, on a cord or not.
- Small plain studs or small plain hoops for women.
- A thin plain chain with **no pendant** [19].
- Hair clips, claw clips, rubber bands, a plait or a bun.
- A cardigan, a shawl, a muffler, a handkerchief, a cloth purse, a sling bag, a phone pouch, an umbrella, a pen in a shirt pocket.
- For men, no earrings.
- No bangles on Mummy, Nani, Mausi or Mami: almost every bangle carries marital meaning somewhere [20].

## 6. How they hold themselves

### 6.1 What the research says

- **The head bobble** (side-to-side tilt) most often means yes or "I understand", and can stand in for thank you or a polite hello [38]. It is real and everyday, and it is also the most worn-out caricature of Indians abroad (see 7).
- **Older knees.** A 2024 study in Maharashtra names squatting and floor sitting as cultural practices that may raise the risk of knee osteoarthritis in older women [37]. An 80-year-old sits on a chair with arms, not on the floor, and rises in stages.
- **Eyes.** Vision impairment is self-reported by 48% of adults 45 and over [35], and older adults in one study said failing eyesight gets in the way of using their phones [17].
- **A tailor's body.** Among 100 sewing-machine operators in Gujarat, 70% had work-related muscle and joint problems, low back pain was the commonest complaint, and the neck carried a very high exposure risk [36].
- **Folded hands and touching feet** are real greetings, but a primer files them under Hindu greetings [4]; in a painting, folded hands read as prayer. Keep both out of panels (rule 3).
- **No source found** for gestures of shopkeepers, coaches or young people. The lines below are team judgement.

## 7. What turns each of these into a caricature

**Accents and the funny-shopkeeper voice.** A 2017 documentary traced how an Indian shopkeeper cartoon voiced in a put-on accent fed the bullying of South Asian children; actors described being asked to deliver lines in that accent [39]. Never spell anyone's Hindi or English to sound foreign or comic.

**The north-south joke.** A 2004 essay says Hindi cinema has long ridiculed south Indians' dress, food and speech, starting with a comedian's "madrasi" in the 1960s [40]. No "Aiyyo", no coconut-oil or idli jokes, no lungi as a gag.

**Community stock characters.** A secondary report of a study of Hindi films from 1960 to 2010 says most corrupt businessmen carried trader-caste surnames, most Sikh characters were comic relief, and Christian women were often cast as loose [41]. **Weight:** the study itself was not found; the figures are unverified. Separately, a reference work notes the long-standing slur of the trader caste as greedy and exploiting customers [42].
- So Mummy's shop is never the cliché trader: no cushion on the floor, no ledger and pot belly, no haggling for laughs.
- No Sikh-coded comic, no beard-and-turban coach.
- The circle's names that mark a community never carry the stereotype either.

**Soap-opera women.** A university article on Indian soaps lists the stock types: the ideal daughter-in-law veiled, in heavy shiny clothes and jewellery from morning, feeding everyone before herself; the controlling, tradition-bound mother-in-law; women set against women [43].
- Mummy and Mami are never veiled or dripping with jewellery.
- Nani's authority is warmth and wit, never control over the women of the house.
- Mausi and Mami are allies, not rivals.
- Men pour the tea as often as women.

**Older people as props.** An Indian advertising study found older people in fewer than 4% of ads, and the article on it lists the clichés [44]:
- the elder as background filler;
- the silent patriarch;
- the dutiful grandmother as a symbol of tradition;
- the unfit uncle;
- the couple who cannot work technology;
- the elder as moral compass.

What that means for this cast:
- Mausa ji's few words must be kind and active (he holds the file, he stands when the doctor comes), never the silent patriarch.
- Nani runs things; she is not a symbol.
- Papa's phone habits are his own way, not a joke about old people.
- Mama ji's worry is not a pot-belly gag.
- Nobody old preaches.

**The aunty, and the WhatsApp uncle.**
- An essay describes how middle-aged women get boxed into "aunty" tropes [45].
- One report calls the most loyal users "WhatsApp aunties" [11].
- A Gen Z interviewee calls her all-caps brother "aggro WhatsApp uncle energy" [9].

What that means for this cast:
- Mausi talking over everyone is energy and care, never nosy gossip about people's characters.
- Masterji's views on passers-by are about cloth, cut and punctuality, never women's morals or clothes.
- Papa's good-morning flower is affection, never spam.
- No uncle forwards politics.

**The 21-year-old as a slang machine.** Tinku's quickness is her scoring and teasing, not a mouthful of trending words; she drops into plain Hindi with Nani [9].

**Visual tells that mark religion, caste or marriage** (section 5). Two more:
- an elder woman all in white with a bare forehead reads as a widow [21];
- a lungi or a long beard on the street in the north or east reads as a community [28][19].

**The painter's own caricatures** (house memory `flux-portrait-biases`, from 300 portraits on 22 Oct 2026):
- FLUX.2 klein paints a bindi on nearly every Indian woman, whatever the prompt says. Check every face close up and paint it out by hand (`DOTS` in `portraits.py`).
- It lightens brown skin in soft styles; "South Asian features and warm brown skin" fixed the family-magazine look.
- A repaint needs a new seed (`SEED_BUMP`).

## 8. For the model sheets and the painter (team judgement)

- **One fixed look line per person**, word for word every time, as `LOOKS` in `cutscenes.py` does: skin tone, face, hair, glasses or not, one silhouette, one palette. Names never reach the painter; each goes in as a role (`ROLE`).
- **Words that need a glossary row before painting** (`painter-glossary-rule`; each has more than one meaning):
  - suit (salwar suit, or a Western suit);
  - vest (undershirt, or waistcoat);
  - cap (sun hat, or a skullcap);
  - glasses (spectacles, or tumblers);
  - chain (necklace, or a metal chain);
  - tape (measuring tape);
  - jumper and sweater;
  - shawl, and where it lies;
  - dupatta, and where it lies;
  - chappal;
  - track pants.
- **Recurring objects** painted from one reference (`paint-refs`): the bible's list sleeve, cane, key ring, scorebook and umbrella, plus Masterji's tape and Mami's list.
- **Skin tones across the household should differ**, as real families do, and none should drift pale in the Rukavat look [flux-portrait-biases].
- **Painting stays in the heavy-job queue only** (`heavyq`); nothing was run for these notes.
