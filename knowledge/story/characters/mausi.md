# Mausi (मौसी), about 55, chapter 1 voice

**Role.** Chapter 1's voice (May, the night train to Nani's, painted in the Lugdi look). Mummy's elder sister and Nani's eldest child. She speaks the chapter's one shout (rule 35) and gives half its judging notes, six in draft 1, each with her face chip; Papa, the asker, gives the rest, so chapter 1's remarks come from a woman and a man (rule 22). She is never the asker (rule 37), never on a card (rule 10), and never names a card person in a bubble (rule 36). Two things set her voice apart from the rest of the cast: in chapter 1 she is the voice with no numbers (Papa owns the figures, so the speaking pair never sound alike), and in a family that shows love only by deeds she is the one who says what she feels out loud. First-time label: 'Mausi, Mummy's elder sister' | 'मौसी, मम्मी की बड़ी बहन' | Hinglish 'Mausi, Mummy ki badi behen' (rule 65); plain 'Mausi' after that, with a tag on every bubble (rule 34). Painter's role word: 'the copper-haired woman', taken from her look. Never her name (names get painted as lettering), and never 'the aunt', because a relation word can paint a second person, the same reason Mausa ji's file gave up 'the aunt's husband' and Mummy's gave up 'the mother'. Flat by design, as a chapter voice: one want, one fear, one contradiction that shows the same way every time, and one turn in December. **Essence.** Nani's eldest, who has taught the junction town's smallest children in her front room for thirty years and talks to every room as if it were Class 3: the loudest, fastest person in the family, a basket bag on her arm, who never lets you finish a sentence and remembers every word of it a year later. The type in five words: the loud aunt who feeds. What makes her not the type: talking over you is how she listens, her memory is exact, and she is the only one in a family of quiet deeds who says what she feels out loud, her hurt included ('Amma calls your Mummy first!'), so loudly and so often that nobody takes it in.

## Inner life

Born 1971, the eldest of Nani's three (Mummy 1974, Mama ji 1979). While Nani ran the grocery from her front room, Mausi did the younger two's homework at the back of the shop: she taught Mummy to read the clock and Mama ji his tables, and has not stopped teaching since. She wanted to be a schoolteacher; she married at 21 instead (1992) and moved to the junction town, where Mausa ji was posted as a lineman with the electricity board. In 1996 she started a tuition class for Classes 1 to 5 in her front room: a blackboard painted on the wall, a mat on the floor, two long benches Mama ji paid for with his first city salary, and a ceiling fan that Mausa ji swaps for a bigger one every summer (his file). That year she bought her basket bag at the junction's Saturday market to carry her pupils' exercise books home to mark, and she still carries a stack of them everywhere. She says she has taught a thousand children; Tinku has counted 612. You and Tinku both did your sums on her mat, so half the town calls her 'Madam ji' and the rest 'Mausi'. In 1995 she noticed the young railway parcel clerk lodging in her lane, the only bachelor who paid the milkman on the first of the month, and carried his proposal to Nani for her younger sister; Mummy and Papa married in 1997, and she mentions it at every wedding. Her two grown children (about 32 and 29) live elsewhere and come to family events by road. Her right knee aches from years on the floor mat with her youngest pupils (the realism notes record squatting and floor sitting as possible knee risks for older women); she now teaches from a chair and accepts the lower berth she pretends not to need. She is a little short-sighted and has never needed reading glasses: she reads with the page close to her face, and nags Mausa ji about the reading glasses he will not wear (his file). She wakes at four, as Nani does, which is why Nani rings her, not Mummy, on nights she cannot sleep; Mausi has never thought that counts for anything. The morning Nani's 'Everyone comes' arrived in January, she cut limes and set a jar of lime pickle in the sun on her roof, made to Nani's own recipe without asking Nani for it, for the first time in her life; it has been maturing up there ever since, and in May it rides to Nani's in her basket bag.

WANTS THIS YEAR: to be first through Nani's door for the eightieth with her basket bag, the January jar of lime pickle inside, and be the first one Nani thanks. The fall that evening (1.40) cancels the party and the jar goes onto Nani's kitchen shelf unopened, so her want waits until December. WANTS FROM YOU: your news first-hand. She lives in the same town and still hears it second-hand on her daily call with Mummy; on the night train she asks for it straight ('Now tell me. Straight to me.') and then talks over the answer. FEARS: that she, the eldest, is the daughter Nani puts up with while Mummy is the one Nani relies on; and that she talks so much that nobody hears her. Unlike everyone else in the family she says the first fear out loud, more than once ('Amma calls your Mummy first!'), and that is exactly why nobody hears it: it sounds like the rest of her noise. CONTRADICTION ('X, but Y'): she never lets you finish a sentence, but remembers every word of it a year later. It shows the same way every time: her bubble starts over the end of yours (your line trails off, 'Mausi, wo…'), and later she quotes your exact words back ('Last year you said…'). ITS COST: whatever you were about to say on the carriage step never gets said, and she never learns what it was. HABIT OF SPEECH: says her first word three times. LINE PEOPLE QUOTE BACK: 'Suno, suno, suno!' | 'सुनो, सुनो, सुनो!' | 'Listen, listen, listen!' (the tripled 'suno' is hers alone in the cast; she calls Mausa ji 'Aji', not 'suno ji'). I KNOW THIS PERSON (a prediction for real readers to confirm): Sunita, the small-town tailor of 41, whose lane will have a woman who runs tuition from her front room and feeds whoever sits down, and whose family she would not want mocked; so Mausi is never the butt of the joke. FLAT OR ROUND: flat by design, with one contradiction and one turn: in December, when Nani thanks her first in front of the whole kitchen, the woman who says everything out loud says nothing at all, because for once she has been heard.

## Voice

**Register.** Hindi-first; the loudest and fastest voice in the cast, warm and bossy like a front-room tuition teacher with her Class 3. She talks in bursts: two to four bubbles of short closed sentences, most ending on ! or ?, the first bubble starting over the end of yours. Her shape: she says a word twice to push the room along ('Chalo, chalo!', 'Na, na!'), asks a question and answers it herself before anyone can, and tags a statement 'hai na?' and moves on before anyone replies. A triple is kept for a peak, at most once a scene, and 'Suno, suno, suno!' is the one the family quotes back. Her long run is a string of short loud sentences, never a comma chain (Mama ji's run-on) and never a list (Mummy's jobs, Mami's statuses). The common fillers come and go in her lines as in anyone's (arre, achha, haan, chalo, dekho, suno, bas, theek hai); what marks her is the shape and the classroom and kitchen her words come from, never a word she owns. Pace quick and rising. When cross she gets louder and faster, the only one in the family who does; when moved she goes silent, the only time she does. No numbers at all, not even ordinals. One shout bubble a chapter at most (rule 35, which is 1.09), and never a whisper, because she cannot whisper. Each bubble is at most 10 words (rule 62), so a longer thought becomes more bubbles. She is never the asker (rule 37) and never reacts to who was picked (rule 39).

**Hindi and English.** A Hindi frame with single English words in it, the older speakers' pattern in the realism notes (1.1). Her English carries feeling and school, not trade: sorry, tension, first class, topper, homework, copy (exercise book), class, teacher, fail, late mark; in chapter 1 also the travel nouns berth and chart. The everyday English verb with kar or ho comes to her as it does to any schoolteacher, with school verbs: 'top kiya', 'check karo', 'fail ho gayi', 'pass ho gaya' (of you only as a command, since a past said of you would mark a gender). Mummy is told apart by what she turns into counter English, not by owning the pattern. She never switches a whole clause into English (Mama ji's and Mami's way). Her Hindi is everyday small-town Hindustani with a teacher's words in it (inaam, bujho, copiyan, 'kaan pakdo', 'khaali pet'), and the town's ordinary words fikr, waqt and zaroor come to her plainly too; never spelled to sound funny. The tag 'hai na?' is a habit of hers, not a word she owns; in English mode it becomes the Indian English 'right?'. In Hinglish mode she reads as Roman Hindi with those English words (how a Hinglish bubble looks waits on rulebook Q6). A Hindi-first reader reads every line aloud (rule 65).

**Habits**

- Says a word twice to hurry, refuse or soothe, the way real Hindi does: 'Chalo, chalo!', 'Na, na!', 'Aao, aao.', 'Sorry, sorry!', or a name twice ('Munna, Munna!', 'Bhabhi, Bhabhi!'); to elders 'Chaliye, chaliye!'. The triple is saved for a peak, at most once a scene: 'Suno, suno, suno!' to call the room (the one the family quotes back), or 'Na, na, na!' at news she will not have. 'Haay' is never doubled or tripled, because हाय-हाय is a lament and a protest chant.
- Asks a question and answers it herself, like the riddle-setter she is every morning, blurting the answer before anyone can guess: 'Kiski? Hamari!', 'Main padosan hoon? Nahin!', 'Andhera? Koi baat nahin!', 'Chai ya halwa? Halwa!'. The one question in the year she cannot answer herself is 'Kisne karwaayi?' at the lower berth, and then she goes silent.
- Ends a statement with 'hai na?' and is gone before anyone answers; in English mode, 'right?'. Not on every line: a few a scene at most.
- Quotes people back word for word: 'Pichhle saal tumne kaha tha…'. Her memory is exact; she never misquotes.
- Says what she feels, out loud, to the whole room: love ('Bahut yaad aayi'), hurt ('Amma pehle Chhoti ko bulaati hain!'), sorry. Silent only when moved, palm to her chest.
- Runs the room like her class: marks the late ('Late mark!'), sets the classroom's punishment in fun ('Kaan pakdo!', to Munna or Tinku), grades a favour ('Ekdum first class!', at most once a chapter), now and then fails herself when she is wrong ('Aaj teacher fail!'), and measures a loss by her pupils ('meri class ka inaam'). Her teaching is in her mouth, not only in one wordless picture, so she is more than the loud aunt who feeds. But the classroom is one of her two sources, never her stamp: about one line in four reaches for it at most, and the kitchen and her exact memory of what people said carry the rest (the flood is 'Achaar ke martbaan upar!', not her pupils' exercise books; being wrong is 'Galat kaun nikla? Main!').
- Worries in quick questions about the body and the belly, and answers for you if you are slow: 'Kuch khaya bhi? Nahin?', 'Chot toh nahin lagi?'.
- Food as punctuation: 'Pehle khao' (the only 'Pehle' that is hers; 'Pehle ___' as a courtesy is yours), 'Khaali pet baat nahin!', 'Muh meetha karo!', and something held out as she says it, her face in the frame. 'Haay' never comes just before she holds out food.
- Report-card verdicts on habits, never on character, in all her judging notes ('Never sits still for long.').
- 'Hamara, hamari' (ours) for anyone she is proud of ('hamari topper'), but never of you, since it would mark your gender.
- She always has an answer and almost never says 'pata nahin', which is why 'Kisne karwaayi?' silences her.
- When cross: louder and faster, hand on hip, the basket bag set down hard, and her own catchphrase fails ('Koi sunta bhi hai? Nahin!').

## Posture

**Stance.** Feet planted wide, weight on her left leg to spare the right knee, chest forward, chin up, the whole body leaning a little towards whoever she is talking to (towards the viewer when she talks to you); the basket bag hooked in one elbow, that hand resting on its cloth; the other hand up at shoulder height, stirring the air. Stance goes in the scene words of each panel, never in her fixed look (rules review).

**Sitting.** She drops down all at once with a thump (sound word 'dhamm' | 'धम्म') and sits deep and wide, right back against the berth wall, never perched on the front edge (eight files had the front edge; this is hers): right leg stretched straight out along the berth for the knee, left foot planted, the basket bag beside her, one arm along the berth back, taking up space. She pats the place beside her with her palm, the invitation she promised you a year ago. She gets up by rocking forward twice and pushing off with one hand, not as Nani does, in stages with a hand on her knee.

**Walk.** Quick short steps, hips rolling, the basket bag swinging on her forearm, talking over her shoulder as she goes; first through every door and first up every step, a hand on the rail, left leg leading. She never walks in silence.

**Gestures**

- THE STIR: one hand turns small circles in the air while she talks, faster as she gets louder, the palm open and the fingers spread when she wins an argument. Her one constant.
- THE TUCK: when she is about to take charge she pulls the loose end of her sari round and tucks it into her waist at the front with both hands. It means 'Now listen'.
- THE OFFERING ARM: a foil parcel held out at full stretch towards the viewer (always with her face in the frame, so the hand is plainly hers, rule 7), to Papa or to Mausa ji; never towards the bay, which is the card people and stays out of the frame (rule 32).
- THE CLOSE READ: anything written (her phone, an exercise book) held a hand's width from her nose, eyes narrowed, no glasses; papers and screens painted blank. It replaces the arm's-length read, which six files shared.
- THE STILL HAND: her palm pressed to her chest when she is moved, the only time her hands stop.
- She touches the sleeve of whoever she is talking to (Papa's, Mausa ji's), never yours. She never greets with folded hands, which read as prayer (realism 6, rule 3): her greeting is both arms opened wide; with Nani, she bends and Nani lays a palm on her head.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: mouth a little open as if about to speak, eyebrows up, body turned to the nearest person, one hand ready.
- PLEASED: both arms flung wide, chin up, a wide open grin, the basket bag swinging.
- WORRIED: mouth shut tight (the only time it is, apart from when she is moved), a hand pressed on the basket cloth, eyes going from face to face.
- CROSS: louder, not quieter, the only one in the family whose anger is loud: a hand on her hip, the basket bag set down hard, chin out, forefinger wagging as at Class 3, mouth wide.
- LAUGHING: head thrown back, eyes shut, a hand slapping her own knee, shoulders shaking.
- TEASING: one eyebrow up, chin down, a sideways look, the stirring hand slowing.
- TIRED: sunk deep into the berth, a hand rubbing her right knee, hair flattened on one side, eyes half shut, mouth still going.
- MOVED: silent, palm pressed to her chest, lips pressed together, eyes wet, her hands still for once. Her one bubble-less panel a chapter.
