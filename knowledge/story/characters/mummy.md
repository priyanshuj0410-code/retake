**Essence.** She runs the electrical shop on the station road, across from the station's parcel gate, and she runs the family the same way: every question gets a price, every account is settled before nine at night, and she has stood behind her glass counter for 27 years so that nobody can say she sits behind one like her mother.

## Inner life

Born 1974 in Nani's town, the middle of Nani's three children: Mausi is three years older, Mama ji five years younger. From the age of eight she counted change at the counter of Nani's front-room grocery, and she half raised her little brother while Nani served the lane. She swore she would never run a counter. What she wanted was a bank's glass window, where nobody haggles. She took a commerce degree at the town college, married Papa, a railway parcel clerk, in 1997, and moved to the junction town. In 1999 she opened an electrical shop on the station road, across from the station's parcel gate, because 'a fuse is never a luxury: when the lights go, people pay'. Nani lent her the first stock money. Mausa ji, then a lineman, gave her her first wholesaler's name. Masterji the tailor has shared her wall ever since. She built the counter of glass, fixed her prices, and paid Nani back in instalments; the last receipt is taped inside the lid of her cash drawer. She lends but never borrows: the town's electricians and contractors, her regulars, buy on credit and settle at the month's end in a plain blue exercise book she keeps in the drawer, and only strangers hear 'Udhaar? Kal.'. What she will not do is owe anyone herself, and that pride is the thing the flood costs her. She wears her late father's man-sized wristwatch (the default answer to the owner's question about Nana; if he is alive, it was his gift when the shop opened). It runs two minutes fast, she has never corrected it, and she winds it every night at nine while Nani's call runs. Her reading glasses live in the cash drawer; she will not wear spectacles like her mother's big round ones in front of the shop. Today: fans, wires, switches, bulbs and fuses in plain boxes. The shutter goes up at nine by that watch, six days a week, Saturday the busiest, and comes down at half past eight. Papa cooks on weekdays and she cooks on Sundays. On the shop roof she grows green chillies and tomatoes in a row of old unmarked tins, the one thing she does slowly, and every Sunday a handful goes to Nani. She taught both her children to count change at that counter at eight, the age she learned, and has not noticed. She knows everyone on the station road by how they stand at a counter, which is where her judging notes come from. When a wholesaler's man asks for the 'malik' (the owner, assumed a man), she has one answer and has given it for 27 years: 'Main hi hoon. Boliye.'

WANTS THIS YEAR: the shutter up at nine every Saturday, flood or no flood, without owing anyone. WANTS FROM YOU: to take half the phone calls, which she will never ask for in words. Laying the red pen down for you in January is how she asks, and laying the spare shop key down for you in the album is how she asks for more. FEARS: the shutter staying down, and the shop that Nani's money started going under. Under that, owing anyone anything: the one time she needed help, in 1999, she paid it back to the last rupee and kept the receipt. She lends to her regulars every month; she will not let anyone lend to her. THE COST (the India review: every capable woman pays for something on the page): her pride. On the first flood night she sends Masterji back to his own doorstep and bails alone until two, and the bottom shelf's cartons of wire, which his offer would have saved, are lost. That loss is why she hands you the hiring list next morning. CONTRADICTION (X, but Y): she swore she would never sit behind a counter like her mother, but she runs hers exactly like her, down to counting heads with one raised forefinger. She squares it by never sitting down: she has stood behind her counter for 27 years. It shows the same way every time: offered a chair, she perches on its front edge, back off the chair back, keys in her lap. In December, beside Nani in the front row, she sits back for the first time. HABIT OF SPEECH: answers any feeling with a price ('How are you, Mummy?' 'Copper's up again.'). QUOTED LINE: 'Nau baje. Shutter up.' | 'नौ बजे। शटर ऊपर।' | 'Nine o'clock. Shutter up.' ## Voice

**Register.** Brisk, mid-fast and level: the pace of a shop counter on a Saturday. Her sentence shape is the list: items joined by commas, each with its price or its person, the figure last ('Tum bill, Tinku stock, shutter nau baje.'), never three full-stopped fragments (Captain sahab's calls) or numbered points (Mami's). Warm underneath, never cold, never loud: when she is angry she gets quieter and more polite, not louder, and that rising politeness is hers to keep. Her shop voice ('Haan ji, boliye') and her home voice are the same voice, which is the family's joke and not hers. She is not the comic one: her dry lines are rare, and in chapter 3 her asks carry only times and needs (rule 37). Her numbers are rupees; minutes belong to Papa and scores to Tinku.

**Hindi and English.** A Hindi frame (the everyday Hindi of a small Hindi-speaking town) with English trade and money nouns dropped in whole: stock, bill, delivery, shutter, wire, switch, fuse, rate, counter, cash, godown, wholesale, advance, copper. English verbs come only as 'English verb + kar/ho': 'check karo', 'delivery ho gayi', 'bill banao', 'count karo'. No whole English clauses; that is Mama ji. This follows two patterns the realism notes report: older speakers build on Hindi and drop in single English nouns (the Dehradun study), and people switch language on the topic of their work. In English mode the shape stays the same: a short list, the figure last. Figures in her bubbles are digits, as in her typing ('saath' is both 60 and 'with').

- Answers every phone call with the shop greeting, 'Haan ji, boliye.' | 'हाँ जी, बोलिए।' | 'Yes, go ahead.', even when it is Nani or you, and carries on without correcting herself. This is her first tell.

## Typing

- Device: a plain black Android phone in a plain black case, the screen painted blank with every word laid on as live text (rule 30). It lives in the side pocket of her kameez and comes out wedged between her left ear and shoulder.
- Script: Roman Hinglish (Hindi in Latin letters), all lowercase, no punctuation at all (no full stops, no question marks); figures always as digits (2 balti, 6 baje, 1840) and never wrong, though her spelling of words drifts (chabi, chaabi).
- HER TELL, which nobody else in the cast has: every message ends on a figure, a price, a count or the hour ('counter 9', 'ye wala check karo 1840').
- Acknowledges by typing the figure back: you send 'Done, Mummy. 1840 ka bill bhej diya.' and she replies '1840'.
- Length: two to six words, one message per thought (a second thought is a second message, never a full stop), never a paragraph, never a greeting or a sign-off.
- Emoji: none, ever (the thumbs-up is Mausa ji's, and your own reply to her). No stickers, no good-morning pictures (those are Papa's), and only one kind of forward: the electricity board's power-cut times, sent the moment she hears them.
- Photos: of bills and stock cartons, painted blank, with one line under them that ends on the figure.
- Voice notes when her hands are busy in the shop. They start with her shop greeting, shown as live text: 'haan ji boliye, arre tum, shutter 9 baje'.
- Speed: reads within a minute between nine in the morning and half past eight at night, and replies only when she has the answer. Nothing between 8:30 and 9:15 pm (the shutter, then Nani's call), and never after nine, so a late message from her means trouble. The flood night is the one time all year.

- Types fast with her left thumb, phone in her left hand, because her right hand is usually holding the keys or the pen.

Against the cast: square like Mausa ji, but she stands where he sits forward, and has a dark bun where he has a white brush-cut. The keys, the watch and the studs do not count at this size.

**Build.** Height band: middle, with Mausi, Mama ji and Tinku (the comics review's four bands; the old ranks of ten were finer than the painter can hold). **Face.** A square face with a strong jaw. ## Posture

**Stance.** On her feet behind a glass-topped counter, feet planted apart, weight even, shoulders square to the customer. Her left palm lies on the glass; her right hand writes or holds the keys; while she writes a bill, the phone is wedged between her left ear and shoulder. She stands all day: the plastic stool behind her counter is for the hired hand, and the strip lets Tinku say so once ('Sit down, Mummy. The stool won't bite.'), so players see that the family has noticed. When she must read small print she turns her back to the shop, puts on the reading glasses from the drawer and takes them straight off (off the panel; the arm's-length squint goes to whoever the trait table gives it). In painter words, stance goes in the scene words, never the fixed look, and the verb is 'is behind the counter', never 'stands', which pulls in the old spectator-stands row.

**Sitting.** Rarely, and only on the front edge: back straight and clear of the chair back, knees apart, both feet on the ground and one pointed at the door, the keys in her lap under her right hand, ready to stand in one movement (Nani rises in stages; Mummy never needs to). This is her claim in the cast's trait table, because her vow and her arc rest on it; the other seven files that give their person the front edge should give it up. The one exception is her arc's turn: in December, beside Nani in the front row, she sits back in her plastic chair and her hands lie still in her lap, in a panel of her own (Mami's file needs a different release on that night, the comics review).

**Walk.** Fast, short, flat-footed steps, chest first, arms close, keys jingling on her right forefinger. She never runs and never looks hurried; she simply arrives early. She takes a stool or the roof ladder like stairs, without a hand on the rail.

**Gestures**

- THE PALM ON THE COUNTER: her left palm laid on a counter, a table or a carton, meaning 'this is settled, and I stand behind it'. At the lane cup it goes on Tinku's scorer's table, in place of the folded arms that are Captain sahab's. A strip-size gesture: the arm's angle reads in black.
- THE ONE-FINGER COUNT: heads counted with her raised left forefinger, lips moving, exactly as Nani counts. It is her contradiction made visible; she has not noticed it, and Tinku has. Painted as a raised arm and pointing hand, big enough to read.
- THE FIST ON THE KEYS: when a decision is made she closes her fist on the key ring and drops her hand to her side. The spin before it is movement, so it lives in the writing and in a close-up, never as a still's anchor.
- ALSO, CLOSE-UPS AND OFF-PANEL ONLY: counting a bundle of notes twice with a fast flick of the right thumb (off the panel: banknotes carry a famous face and numerals); winding her father's watch at nine with the phone on her shoulder (an insert).

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: square stance, brows level and low, mouth a straight line, eyes already on the next thing, one hand busy.
- PLEASED: one short nod, the fist closed on the keys, one corner of her mouth lifting; she says a price.
- WORRIED: she tidies. She squares a stack of cartons that is already square, counts the same shelf twice, checks her wrist twice in a minute, and starts talking in minutes instead of hours.
- CROSS: very still and very polite: both hands come off the counter and clasp in front of her at the waist (never folded across the chest), chin up, eyebrows lifted, a 'ji' on every name; the voice drops and never rises.
- LAUGHING: rare and sudden: her head goes back, her mouth opens wide, her left palm slaps the counter once, then she goes straight back to work.
- TIRED: both palms on the counter and her head down between her shoulders for one breath; or, at nine, sitting on the edge of a chair winding the watch with her eyes shut while Nani talks.
- MOVED: she looks away at the shelves and counts something under her breath, and her right hand covers her left wrist.

## In the story

**Best scenes**

- THE OPENING, the January kitchen (present day): she presses the free paper calendar onto a nail, makes the joke about the nail, rings three dates in red and lays the red pen down for you. The family's whole year passes through her hands, and her request for help is a gesture.
- 3.02, THE FLOOD: water over her sandals at the half-raised shutter, sleeves up, keys on her finger, 'Saturday, 9:00. Shutter up.', while Masterji asks 'All this mud, by Saturday?' from his doorway. Two shopkeepers of one wall: the dark square block and the light stoop.
- THE FIRST FLOOD NIGHT (a proposal, off the asks): she sends Masterji back, bails alone, and at two the bottom shelf is gone; 'Had hai!'. Her pride, paid for.
- 3.11, THE ROOF in the first sun: wet cartons laid out in rows among her chilli tins; she checks the chillies first ('Mirchi bach gayi.'), then counts the cartons twice with one finger, exactly as Nani counts heads.
- 3.26, SATURDAY MORNING: the shutter goes up by her father's fast watch; across the station road Papa stands at the parcel-office window, and she lifts her arm with the keys above her head without turning round.
- THE NIGHT TRAIN (chapter 1, silent): Papa's phone is dead and she lifts her left wrist to him; two people who speak in numbers, his minutes and her watch, in one insert.
- THE NINE O'CLOCK CALL (any present-day night page): Nani's call comes, Mummy on the front edge of a chair winding the watch, eyes shut.
- THE LANE CUP (chapter 4, silent): at the back of the boundary, her palm pressed on Tinku's scorer's table from the first ball.
- THE WEDDING'S LAST NIGHT (chapter 5, silent), in its own panel after the cake: she sits back in her chair beside Nani for the first time all year and lets others serve.
- THE ALBUM (present day): beside Nani, sitting back; the spare shop key laid on the table, turned towards you.

**Never does**

- Sits behind her own counter. She stands; the stool is for the hired hand.
- Asks for help in words. She assigns, or lays something down for you (the album line is the owner's call).
- Says 'don't worry', gives a vague time, or says she is tired.
- Haggles for laughs, cheats a customer, charges city prices, or plays the greedy trader.
- Gives credit to strangers, until the flood week, when she gives it to the whole lane. Her regulars always have credit.
- Borrows, or lets anyone pay for her.
- Hands anyone her own key ring; the spare set from the nail is the one that travels, and the one she lays down for you in the album. She would hand a new hand the broom, the counter or the scooter, but the cash drawer and the 6 am shutter only if she cannot be there herself, and then the strip should show why. This is a note for the chapter 3 writers: the review asks whether any shop would do it (rule 5).
- Raises her voice. She gets politer instead.
- Folds her arms (Captain sahab's), wears red in Cricket (his), or claims the cast's last-night sit-back for anyone but herself.
- Covers her head, wears bangles, a chain or a forehead mark, wears a sari, or wears spectacles in front of anyone.
- Doubts a report of harassment or bullying, or a dowry demand, or calls one a misunderstanding; she never hints that someone who complained about a boss is trouble (rules 22, 27).
- Judges anyone's character beyond counter habits, or says anything about women or men in general (rule 22).
- Sits on a card, is one of the equal two, or is the comic one in her own chapter (rules 10, 37).
- Touches the player, or reaches towards the viewer; she lays things down instead (rule 7, bible line 78); calls the player by a gendered word or a pet name (rule 8).
- Sets herself against Mausi or Mami as women against women: the rivalry is concern, and the alliance is real.
- Feeds everyone before herself as a soap-opera sacrifice. She eats standing at the counter at two, and Papa cooks on weekdays.
- Messages anyone after nine at night, except on the flood night; texts Nani, ever; or uses an emoji.
