**Essence.** The lane's old grocer. For 44 years she weighed out rice and dal in her own front room and never once put a thumb on the scale; now she weighs everyone's plans from the wicker armchair that stands where her counter used to be. She runs everyone's life and lets no one run hers, and what she fears most is becoming a head that somebody else counts. She is not always right: she cheats at carrom, she is the only person alive who says the 1979 umpire was right, and her pride put her on a wet step in the wrong shoes. The type in five words: the grocer who weighs everyone.

## Inner life

Born in 1946, the eldest of four, in the older small town one night by train from the household's junction. She has lived there all her life. She studied to Class 5: she reads Hindi slowly in big print and does sums in her head faster than anyone with a phone. From 1972 to 2016 she ran a grocery from the front room of her house. Her one rule at the counter was the grocer's oldest, 'डंडी मत मारना' (no thumb on the scale), and the lane trusted her weights for 44 years. She also carried half the lane on credit in an account book. In 2016 her bad left knee closed the counter, and that day she tore up the credit book in front of the lane. That is why the whole lane still calls her Nani, and why it comes to the ward in May. The counter is gone. Her wicker armchair stands where it stood, facing the lane door; the shop's old brass pan scale hangs on a nail on the wall, never used; Mama ji's school cricket bat leans behind her door. Her children: Mausi (b. 1971), Mummy (b. 1974) and Mama ji (b. 1979). Nana is the owner's question. The default: he died before the story begins, off screen. He is never a plot device and nothing on screen marks it (no white sari, no garlanded photograph). His wristwatch is on Mummy's wrist, so Nani wears none and tells the time by the lane. A younger sister and a younger brother live in other towns; their grandchildren are among the cousins on the night train. The headcount is the writers' to fix (Papa's file has sixteen people and twelve berths, the draft bible fourteen), so none of Nani's lines carries the number. Her day now: up at five and tea at her window by half past, while the lane passes and reports in. Mama ji phones at eight. A neighbour's grandson fetches the milk and the medicines. In the afternoons the lane still sits in her front room and she settles its arguments; on Sundays she loses at carrom to Tinku and disputes the score. At nine she calls Mummy: BP, the day's takings at Mummy's shop, then a silence neither of them ends, until Nani hangs up first. Lights out at ten. Health: a bad left knee, so a walking stick since 2016; BP tablets; cataract operations on both eyes in 2019, hence the big round spectacles. In March Mummy brought her strapped sandals with a ridged sole; Nani called them 'the doctor's shoes', left them in their box under the bed and kept her smooth old chappals, and in May she slipped in those on the wet back step. Nobody ever says so; the shoes say it. Her phone is a large-screen one Mama ji gave her, and Tinku set it up for voice typing so she never has to ask anyone to read or write for her. All of this is team judgement built on the cast map, for the owner to approve. None of it appears as a caption; it surfaces only in lines, objects and rooms.

WANT this year: everyone under her roof at once for her eightieth, every head counted by her own finger. WANT from you: the plain truth, unsoftened, and someone to hold her list when she cannot. You have read her bills aloud since you could read. FEAR: being moved out of her own house into a child's city flat, a guest in someone else's kitchen; underneath that, becoming a head that somebody else counts. The fall in May makes the fear real, and Mama ji's 8 am call says it out loud. CONTRADICTION: she runs everyone's life, but will not let anyone run hers. It shows the same way every time: when someone helps her, she turns the help into an order she gave ('The doctor said one night. I said yes.'; 'My whole lane's here. Put them to work.'; 'Someone holds it.'). She can ask for help only in the third person ('Who rides home with Nani?'). THE COST, so that she can be wrong: the refusal is never free. She refused the doctor's shoes and fell; on the ward night, too proud to sleep, she sends Tinku a voice note disputing a carrom board she lost, and Tinku's book is right. WHO WOULD SAY 'I KNOW THIS PERSON': Mr Natarajan (his own mother); Sunita (the woman behind the counter in her own lane, since Sunita's husband runs a small grocery, and she will know 'dandi mat maarna' at once); Farida (every ward she has worked on has one patient who runs it from the bed).

## Voice

**Register.** Low, slow and final. Her sentence shape, hers alone in the cast: fragments of one to three words, each closed with a stop, and the stop is part of the line; a verb only when she gives an order. Never hurried and never raised, except for one 'बस!' a year. She names no clock times and few figures: she tells time by the lane (when the milk comes, when the lamps come on, after Mummy's call), leaving minutes to Papa, prices to Mummy and scores to Tinku. Her questions are 'who should' asks. She calls herself 'Nani' only when she needs help, because she cannot ask for it in the first person. Dry, and never comic in her own chapter. Like several elders she shows love in a deed, not a word (tea); her one overt moment is the album's 'And you.'

**Hindi and English.** The most Hindi in the cast: the everyday spoken Hindi of a small Hindi-speaking town, with no English verbs and no English clauses. English comes only as single nouns the world handed her: doctor, BP, ward, X-ray, ticket, cake, flat, list, scorer. Her Hinglish (rulebook Q6 still open) is her Hindi in Roman letters: 'Sab aayein.' Her images come from the grocery's scale, not its account book: तौल (weighing), डंडी (the scale's beam, as in डंडी मारना, to short-weigh), तराज़ू (scales), उधार (credit), छुट्टा (change). हिसाब belongs to Mummy, so the two never share a word.

## Posture

**Stance.** When she stands, which is rarely: small and upright, feet together, her right hand on the stick's T-handle with the stick a little forward and out to her right, her left hand free, its forefinger ready to count. She never leans on a wall or a person. She does not walk and talk at once: to say something she stops and plants the stick first.

**Sitting.** Upright and right back in her wicker armchair, her straight spine against its back, the wide chair round her small frame like a throne, feet resting on a low wooden stool, the stick upright at her right knee, the purse in her lap under her left hand, the chair facing the door so she sees who comes in. She is the one person in the cast who sits back: the front-edge perch is shared by eight files and is Mummy's December beat (sitting back at last), so Nani claims the opposite. When she laughs she rocks forward and slaps her knee, the only time she leaves the chair back. She rises in stages: left hand on her bad knee, right hand on the stick, then up, refusing help. In a hospital bed she sits up against the head rail rather than lie down whenever anyone is in the ward. Never on the ground (floor sitting and squatting are knee risks at her age).

**Walk.** Slow, short, even steps, the stick going first, never hurried, chin up. She stops to look at whoever she is passing. After the fall she is slower and the stick takes real weight for the first time, which she hates. By December she walks again, slowly, and points with the stick once more.

**Gestures**

- COUNTING, hers alone in the cast: her left forefinger taps the air once for each head, lips moving, no sound, eyes going from face to face. She counts only the household (and the steel tumblers on the ward locker), never a card person, and counts you only in the album.
- POINTING WITH THE STICK: from the chair she lifts the walking stick level and points it at the thing she means (the file, the door, the cot), never at a person, and never at anyone on the coming cards (rule 36). She directs; she does not hand over.
- 'BAS': an open palm pressed down once in the air to end an argument, then she turns her face to the window.
- GREETING, the gesture that stands in for touching her feet (used by families of many communities): her palm laid on the bent head of a grown child who arrives (Mama ji in December), a hand on the shoulder for the younger ones (Tinku). Only between painted people, since the player is never painted.
- IDLE HANDS: her right thumb rubs the T-handle of the stick; her left hand smooths the edge of her sari end on her knee, the way she smoothed banknotes at the counter.
- YES: one slow downward nod with the eyes closed for a moment (never the side-to-side head bobble, a stock caricature).

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: upright, mouth in a firm straight line, eyes steady behind the lenses, one hand on the stick, one on the purse, both still.
- PLEASED: the chin lifts, a small closed-mouth smile deepens every line, one slow nod.
- WORRIED: she counts again, faster, lips moving silently; she sits even straighter, because worry makes her more upright, not less.
- CROSS: one tap of the stick's rubber foot, the 'bas' palm, then she turns her face to the window and will not look at the person. Her anger is a tap and a turned face, not stillness or volume; in chapter 2 Mausa ji's tell is going still and standing up, so the two never share one.
- LAUGHING: a sudden wide open laugh, rocking forward out of the chair back, shoulders shaking under the shawl, one hand slapping her knee.
- TIRED (only on the ward night, after the X-ray): the shoulders drop under the shawl, both hands stacked on the T-handle, chin down on her hands, eyes shut; the one time her posture is small too.
- MOVED: she cannot say it. She looks hard at the thing (the cake, the full table), pushes her spectacles up with one finger, then busies her hands smoothing her sari end and counts heads again. Her eyes are wet behind the lenses.

## In the story

**Best scenes**

- The ward night (chapter 2): running a district hospital ward from the bed, face-on, sitting up against the head rail, the stick hung upright beside her, counting the steel tumblers on the locker to see who came. Her whole contradiction in one room.
- Past midnight on the ward: her one message ever sent after ten, a voice note disputing a carrom board she lost. Petty, wrong and entirely her.
- 7:30 am outside X-ray: small in a steel wheelchair, the stick upright in her hand, the purse in her lap, having to let 'someone' hold it. The turn of her year, told by her hands in a close-up insert.

- Home in her wicker armchair the morning after, tea in a steel tumbler, cancelling her own party, Mausi's shut box of candles on the stool and the doctor's shoes on her feet.
- Her lit doorway at dawn in May, a small dark shape counting the household through the gate and stopping before you.

- The album: face-on at last, counting heads round the table and counting you last.
- Any scene where someone tries to help her, and she turns it into her own order.
