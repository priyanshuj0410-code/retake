# Captain sahab (कप्तान साहब), about 65

**Role.** The lane's old club captain and the lane cup's only umpire, about 65, and chapter 4's voice (15 August, the lane cup, painted in the Cricket look). Not family: a neighbour on the household's lane in the junction town, in the house where the two lanes meet, beside the cracked drain. One of chapter 4's two judging voices, with Tinku (rule 22: a man and a woman). He sets the stakes, calls the tosses (the toss is his word alone in the cast; Tinku's file bars her from it) and umpires the final. He never asks a decision (Tinku asks all eight, rule 37), never sits on a card, and is met in chapter 4 only while rulebook Q11 is open (a silent presence in chapter 3 is proposed below; the bible allows silent presences). HIS COMPETENCE ON SCREEN, which the plot needs (India review: every man gets one): he sets the stakes that get the drain mended, umpires the final straight, and spreads a tarpaulin over Masterji's cloth on the flood night. Flat by design, with one contradiction held the same way every time and one turn, painted on the page, on cup day. FIRST-USE LABEL, kept word for word: 'Captain sahab, the lane's old cricketer' | 'कप्तान साहब, गली के पुराने क्रिकेटर' (rule 65). In much of north India 'Captain sahab' first means a retired army man, so the label never shortens and the ball is in his hand in his first panel (India review). Plain 'Captain sahab' after that, with a tag on every bubble. His first name is never said: the lane has called him Captain sahab since his first season as captain in 1990, his wife included. ROLE WORD for the painter: 'the old man in the white sun hat' (comics review: a role word from his look), never the name, which the painter letters, and never 'cricketer' or 'captain', which bring kit, a scoreboard, a peaked cap or epaulettes into the flood and the drain (rules review).

**Essence.** The strictest umpire on the lane, who has given every ball by the book for thirty cups and has still not forgiven one decision given against him at his only state trial. A retired bus-depot mechanic with big knuckled hands, he stands side-on as if waiting for the next ball, tosses an old leather ball a hand above his open palm while he thinks, and answers most questions with an umpire's call: 'Out.' 'Not out.' 'No ball.' 'Wait.' The type in five words: the strict old cricket uncle. What makes him not the type: the specific past he carries in his hand and on every lane bat (the match ball the town side gave him at his last game in 2002; bat handles regripped with strips of bus inner tube from the depot), and a failing he cannot see: his verdicts on balls are exact, but his verdicts on people are old grudges kept like scores (he still says one lane player 'plays to the crowd' because of one innings years ago), the trial's failing turned the other way. On cup day, at dusk, in a painted panel and not off it, he lets a call against himself stand: Tinku's book, which once scored a no-ball against his own lane, holds every ball of ten cups, and he tells her to show it to the district.

## Inner life

Born in 1961 in the junction town, the son of a state bus driver; grew up in the depot quarters, batting with a taped tennis ball against the bus-yard wall. In 1979, aged 18, he went to his one state under-19 trial and was given out leg before wicket first ball by an umpire he still calls blind; he came home on the night train and never got another trial. From 1979 to 1985 he worked at a scooter repair shop by the station; in 1985 he joined the state bus depot as a mechanic and stayed 36 years, retiring in 2021 at 60. He captained the town's club side from 1990 to 2002; the lane has called him Captain sahab since that first season. In a club game in 1987 a fast ball broke his nose, which still bends a little to one side. His last club game was in 2002, aged 41, and the side gave him the match ball, the old leather ball he has carried every day since. In 1994 the lane road was raised and the drain where his lane meets the next one began to flood every July; he and Masterji (who lives on the far lane) have argued every monsoon since about whose lane's drain floods the station road (Masterji's file). On 15 August 1996 he started the lane cup to give the two lanes one day a year on the same side of something. It has one rule, said once and never explained: 'Live on the lane? Then play.' | 'गली में घर है? तो खेलो।' | 'Gali mein ghar hai? Toh khelo.' (India and rules reviews: the draft's रहते हो is masculine and, said to the lane with you in it, gave you a gender; खेलो is a command and clears rule 66.) He made the rule because two lanes never had twenty-two players otherwise, which is why the cup has always been everyone from 18 to 60, women and men alike; he has never called it a rule about anything but numbers. In 2010 a jack slipped at the depot and caught his right knee; he stopped playing even lane cricket and became the cup's only umpire. In 2017 the old scorer could no longer see the far end of the lane, and he handed the cup's book to twelve-year-old Tinku, who had been keeping her own tally on the wall in chalk (Tinku's file). He regrips every lane bat with strips of old bus inner tube. He lives with his wife in the house by the drain; their daughter teaches at a school in the district town and their son works in the city, and the grandchildren come for the cup every year. He walks with Papa at six every morning (Papa's file) and polishes his black shoes when he gets back. His reading glasses stay in a drawer at home. This year: more scooters park on the lane ground than last year, the July flood has cracked the drain, and his knee tells him the cup needs someone after him.

WANTS THIS YEAR: one more cup on 15 August as it has been since 1996, both lanes out at nine, and someone to hand the cup's book and the toss to before his knee decides for him. WANTS FROM YOU: a straight bat and a fair pick. On cup day he watches whether you pick a side the way he umpires, by the book; he never says so, and nothing he says before a pick hints at one (rules 36 and 39). FEARS: that the cup dies with him and the lane ground becomes parking; under it, that he will be remembered for one ball at his trial and not for thirty cups. CONTRADICTION, held the same way every time: the strictest umpire on the lane, but still sore about one decision given against him at his only trial. He gives any ball by the book without a flicker, and any talk of umpires, luck or fairness brings him straight back to the trial ('My one trial. Not out. Ask anyone.'; no year on screen, rule 2). His judging notes on lane players are the same failing off the field: one innings remembered for years. HABIT OF SPEECH: he answers with umpire's calls (Out, Not out, No ball, Wait, Over), and when he says more, the call comes first and one short reason after it ('No ball. A rule's a rule.'). THE LINE PEOPLE QUOTE BACK: 'Straight bat. Front foot.' | 'सीधा बैट। फ़्रंट फ़ुट।' | 'Seedha bat. Front foot.' Every child of the lane, you included, heard it at seven and still hears it passing his door. WHO SAYS 'I KNOW THIS PERSON': Ravi (P2), the 26-year-old shopkeeper for whom puzzles set in a cricket team are a reason to come back, knows the uncle who ran the gully team; Mr Natarajan (P5), the 61-year-old rule-keeper who believes he treats everyone the same, is Captain sahab, blind spot included. ## Voice

**Register.** Crisp and low: an umpire's carrying voice that never needs raising. HIS SHAPE IS TWO BEATS: the verdict first, short, then at most one short reason or instruction, never the reason first, 2 to 8 words in a bubble. At the ground and on any ruling the verdict is an umpire's call ('No ball. Line ke peeche se daalo.', 'Not out, Nani ji. Bat laga tha.'). Everywhere else, at the gate, on the six o'clock walk, across the drain, the verdict is plain lane Hindi and the reason comes from the bus depot ('Zyada meethi hai, Master. Bilkul depot canteen wali.', 'Theek hoon. Bas ghutna servicing mein.'). A call shows up in at most one bubble in two, so cricket is his trade, not his whole tongue: a retired depot mechanic talks cricket at the ground and plain Hindi with depot words at the gate. The calls he keeps: 'Out.', 'Not out.', 'No ball.', 'Over.', 'Next ball.', 'Well played.', 'Well fielded.', 'Front foot.', 'Retired hurt.' for his knee, and 'Ruko' ('Rukiye' when an elder can hear). A second thought is a second bubble after a second toss of the ball, never a longer line, and he never adds a third beat: Masterji's verdict wanders on after 'waise', Captain sahab's stops. Short, but not like the cast's other short voices: Nani closes bare fragments and orders, Mausa ji drops the subject, Tinku gives a score and 'haan?', Mami reads things back with a status, you name the listener. Beside Tinku in chapter 4 he gives rulings and she keeps the score: he never describes play as it happens (her commentary) and never says a score. FEELING: in plain words most of the time, said short and once ('Mujhse chook ho gayi.', 'Badi meherbani.', 'Koi baat nahin, khiladi. Hota hai.'). A cricket code for a feeling ('Next ball' as comfort, 'Well fielded' as thanks, 'Front foot' as love) comes at most once a chapter, beside its plain form. 'Well played.' comes once a year, and then he looks away. HUMOUR: sardonic and bone-dry, about his knee, his age and the game ('Purana model hoon, Scorer. Parts nahin milte.'), never about a person's hard fact; while rulebook Q5 is open his jokes stay out of the chapter outcome (rule 46). NUMBERS: rare and marked, only the toss countdown in the two lead-ins (a story deadline, rule 37) and 'Nau matlab nau' once a year to Papa; everything else in words. When he is not sure he waits ('Ruko.') rather than guess out loud. CROSS: he goes still. A whisper bubble, the words further apart, the verdict finger up and kept up; never a shout, never an exclamation mark. Tinku, his chapter's other voice, goes fast when cross, so their tells never match. TALKS MOST TO the lane's players, and on the page to Tinku at the scorer's table, Papa on the six o'clock walk and Masterji across the drain. He is never the asker (rule 37).

**Hindi and English.** A Hindi frame: plain lane Hindi with a few older everyday words in it (meherbani, zaroor, waqt, chook, mol-bhaav). English comes in for two things only, the topic pulling it in, as the realism notes have the older speakers. Cricket: the calls and coaching words, said the way the lane says them (out, not out, no ball, over, next ball, front foot, straight bat, well played, well fielded, clean catch, catch, toss, pitch, crease). Machines: depot nouns (servicing, jack, clutch, tube, grip, tarpaulin, canteen, depot, model, parts), and for a machine job the everyday English verb with karo ('Grip change karo', 'Brake check karo'), the way every depot mechanic talks. His knee stays 'Retired hurt', a call, never 'retire hua'. He never switches a whole clause into English (Mama ji and Mami do). The common fillers are his as much as anyone's, in the middle of a line (achha, bas, arre, haan, chalo, dekho, theek hai). आप and a title to elders and equals; तुम to anyone he has coached or seen drop a catch. HINDI MODE, written fresh for Hindi-first readers: the cricket words every lane fan knows stay (आउट, नॉट आउट, नो बॉल, कैच, ओवर, टॉस, पिच, फ़्रंट फ़ुट, सीधा बैट), and the rest go into plain Hindi: Next ball अगली गेंद, Well played बढ़िया पारी, Well fielded अच्छा पकड़ा (बढ़िया फ़ील्डिंग when it is said of a job off the field), Clean catch साफ़ कैच, Dead ball ये गेंद नहीं गिनी जाएगी, Retired hurt घुटना छुट्टी पर, Last over आख़िरी ओवर; machine verbs in plain Hindi (ग्रिप बदलो). HINGLISH keeps the English calls. ENGLISH MODE: plain, clipped British English with the calls unchanged; 'Arre waah' stays as it is, the way Mami keeps 'Ish'.

**Exclamation.** 'Arre waah.' | 'अरे वाह।' | 'Arre waah.' Said quietly, to nobody, at a clean catch, a shot off the middle of the bat, or good news he can see as one ('Arre waah. ## Posture

**Stance.** Side-on to whoever he is talking to, as a batter stands at the crease: weight on the good leg, the other knee soft and a little forward, chin up, looking along his nose from under the brim. One hand on his hip; the other loose with the ball, or tossing it. At the boundary he stays side-on and tossing; his arms fold high across his chest only at the umpire's spot (comics review: Mummy's chapter 4 pose must not match his; her file gives her a palm on Tinku's scorer's table and no folded arms). Standing, he never leans on a wall.

**Sitting.** Well back in a plain plastic chair, never on its front edge (comics review: eight files had the front-edge sit), the stiff leg stretched straight out, the other foot drawn in, the ball on his thigh, his thumb turning it along the seam. Never on the ground, a durrie or a step (the knee). At lunch he eats with the steel plate on his knee.

**Walk.** Brisk, short, even steps, upright, arms swinging from the shoulder, with a small stiffness in one leg that he hides by never slowing down. On the six o'clock walk beside Papa, Papa takes long leaning strides and Captain sahab short upright ones, and they are never in step. He walks round the cracked drain, never over it.

**Gestures**

- THE TOSS (his default and his front image): tosses the old ball a hand's height above his open palm and catches it without looking, again and again while he decides; when he has decided, he catches it and closes his fist on it. The catch is his full stop.
- THE SHADOW STROKE: when he says 'Front foot.' he slips the ball into a trouser pocket and plays a straight-bat forward stroke with empty hands, front elbow high, front foot forward, even at 65, even in a doorway.
- THE VERDICT FINGER: when something is final he lifts his forefinger slowly to shoulder height, as an umpire gives a batter out, and holds it there. He uses it for 'no' at home as well as 'out' on the lane, and it is his anger tell. In a painting the finger points up, never at the viewer.
- THE TWO-FINGER POINT: he marks a place (the crack, the crease, where someone should stand) with two fingers of one hand pointing down, the ball staying in the other.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: side-on, chin up, eyes narrowed under the brim, the ball going up and down a hand's height, mouth a straight line.
- PLEASED: one short nod; the ball caught and held still; one corner of his mouth goes up; he says 'Arre waah.' at a shot, or 'Well played.' once a year, and looks away along the lane as if he had said nothing.
- WORRIED: looks up at the sky over the depot end and pushes the brim up with one knuckle; the ball held still in his palm; his weight shifts off the good leg and back.
- CROSS: goes very still and quiet; the verdict finger rises and stays up; lips pressed thin; eyes fixed on the thing (a scooter on the pitch); his calls come further apart. He never shouts and never waves his arms. Tinku, beside him in chapter 4, goes fast instead (her file), so the two never share an anger tell.
- LAUGHING: a short bark of a laugh, head back, and one slap of his palm on his thigh; deep creases everywhere. The hat stays on (comics review).
- TIRED: slumped back in a plastic chair, the stiff leg straight out, rubbing the knee with the heel of his hand, the ball in his lap, the hat tipped forward over his eyes.
- MOVED: turns side-on away from whoever moved him and looks along the lane; presses the ball hard between both hands; clears his throat once. No tears on screen. Then 'Next ball.'
