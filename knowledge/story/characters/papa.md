**Essence.** The railway parcel clerk who loves in timetables: he cannot say he missed you, so he books you the lower berth, texts 'Reached?' and, once this year, lays his list down for you. Every feeling he has comes out as a time, and every time is exact. The type in five words: the punctual railway dad. What makes him not the type, and it is on the page, not only in his past: he is a goods-train guard's son who waited at a railway-colony gate for his father's late train, and he still lifts a hand to the last van of every goods train that goes by; he still counts under his breath in Tamil, learned from the colony children he played with (proposal); and the one thing he never times is the dal he cooks every weekday, by taste.

## Inner life

Born in 1970 in a railway colony, the son of a goods-train guard who was away every other night. As a boy he waited at the colony gate at the minute his father's train was due, and could not sleep when it was late; that boy is where 'Reached?' comes from. When the train came, his father lifted a hand from the last van and the boy waved back. The family moved every few years across several states, so he grew up with no home town, a new school each time, careful Hindi that belongs to no one town, and one constant: the timetable. From eight to twelve his father was posted to a Tamil-speaking division; he learned to count in hide-and-seek with the colony children and still counts under his breath in Tamil (proposal; a Tamil reader signs it off). His father also taught him the guard's 'steady' signal, a flat hand lowered palm down, and he still uses it. He joined the railway at 19 as a clerk and has worked the parcel office on the junction's platform since 1995: bicycles, crates of fruit, a wedding's sweets, each weighed, entered in the register against the minute and sent on time. Mausi brought his marriage proposal in 1996; he married Mummy in 1997, and Nani's town became the home town he never had. His parents have died; his one brother lives far away and rings on his birthday, so nobody from his side appears in the story. Today he is four years from retiring. He walks to work in eleven minutes, walks with Captain sahab at six every morning for half an hour, and at 6:35, back home, sends the family one good-morning picture. On weekdays he is home at six and Mummy at nine, so the weekday dal is his, cooked by taste and never the same twice; nobody in the house remarks on it. On Saturdays he looks across the platform at nine and lifts a hand as Mummy's shutter goes up, the same hand he lifts to a guard's van. He travels on his privilege pass with the family members its rules allow, and everyone else goes on tickets; one booking holds at most six people, so a family trip is always several PNRs, which he enjoys. The story never says whether you are on his pass: the pass rules treat a grown son and a grown daughter differently, so saying it would give you a gender (rule 8). He writes a fresh travel list for every trip, one plain page folded once lengthwise into a long pale-blue plastic folder, and keeps every old one since his first job in a cardboard file box under the bed. He never reads his list in front of anyone: he knows it by heart. He wears no watch, because the phone keeps railway time to the second, and no spectacles, because his eyes are good: he reads the platform clock from the far end of the junction.

WANTS THIS YEAR: all sixteen of them at Nani's door at 6:00 on the morning of her eightieth, and, under it, to be counted as one of hers, not only as the family's railway. WANTS FROM YOU: to be the one who never needs reminding. FEARS: being late when it matters; and under that, that in the family he married into he is only as welcome as he is useful. CONTRADICTION, held the same way all year: exact to the minute about time, but unable to say one plain thing he feels; every feeling comes out as a time, a berth or a ticket (he books Mausi's lower berth for her knee and never says so; he texts 'Reached?' and means 'I missed you'). The one thing he does not time is the dal. HABIT OF SPEECH: whole, careful sentences that keep their verbs, like a platform announcement, with any time corrected to the minute. ## Voice

**Register.** Measured, even and quiet; never raised and never an exclamation mark (chapter 1's one shout is Mausi's). He talks like a platform announcement: an ordered run of whole sentences, each three to eight words with its verb and its full stop, one fact to a sentence, across one to three bubbles, and the last one often names what comes next ('Ab TT aayega.'). That is his long shape, and it is unlike the other long talkers: Mama ji runs on with commas and 'aur haan', Mausi triples and overlaps, Masterji gives a verdict and wanders into cloth and weather; Papa closes each sentence before he starts the next. The everyday fillers of spoken Hindi come into his lines the way they come into anyone's, softly at the head of a sentence ('Suno,' to Mummy, 'Achha?' at a piece of news) or in the middle ('aap bas time bataiye', 'aayenge zaroor'), never as a tag at the end, so the closed announcement stays his. Slow and exact, but not a machine: a time stands where others put a feeling, in the words railway people really say ('do ghante der se', 'baarah minute late'), never a robot count, and good news goes straight to Nani ('Mummy ji ko abhi batata hoon.'). His only verbless lines are the logged ones, the time correction ('9:40, aaspaas nahin.') and the deed logged in his thanks to the young ('Raat bhar, list ke saath.'), the repeated word when cross ('Koi nahin.'), 'Thank you, ji.' to strangers, and the fixed replies he types. With Nani, who lets him finish, the run is longest (three bubbles); with everyone else it is usually two sentences. Numbers: one of the two people in the cast who lean on them (with Tinku), and only clock times, durations and the count before a noun; never prices, scores or ordinals. Budget: at most one figure a bubble, and at least one bubble in three with none (in the test set below, 3 lines of 12 carry a clock figure and one more says 'do ghante'). Humour is deadpan, about himself and clocks, in the same flat announcement voice.

**Hindi and English.** Careful standard Hindi of the railway colonies, belonging to no one town: full verbs, आप and -इए to elders, no regional colour, and the town's common Hindustani used the way anyone uses it ('fikr mat kijiye', 'zaroor'; cast-wide 8: these words belong to the town, not to Masterji). English comes as single words: the railway nouns every Indian traveller knows (coach, berth, platform, PNR, chart, waiting list, booking, ticket, running status, TT, board; railway people say TT, and 'checker' is an outsider's word) and the everyday nouns of a colony boy grown up (time, battery, alarm, gate, colony, joining, walk); clock times stay figures in all three modes ('9:40'). His verbs are mostly Hindi, many of them the railway's own (gaadi lagna, chhootna, chalna, chart banna, berth milna). The everyday English verb with kar or ho now comes too, where it suits him, and what he turns into one is the booking counter's own work: 'book kiya', 'book kar deta hoon', 'confirm ho gaya', 'cancel karna' (cast-wide 2; Mummy's mark is home things in counter English, 'MCB check karo', so the two still sort apart). He switches by single words, as older speakers do in the Dehradun study the realism notes cite, never by whole English clauses (Mama ji and Mami do that). टिकट is masculine in his mouth ('का टिकट', the standard booking-counter form); if Mama ji's file keeps 'की टिकट' it is Mama ji's colloquial mark and both files say so, otherwise both say 'का'. Hindi mode uses plain Hindi where a low-English reader would stall: 'गाड़ी कहाँ है' for 'running status', 'सैर' for 'walk'. Tamil only in the coach count under his breath, kept in Tamil in every mode (a Tamil reader signs off the words and the script). When cross the English goes altogether: 'late' becomes 'der', 'halt' goes, and only the time stays. ## Typing

- DEVICE: a plain black smartphone in a plain black case, in his trouser pocket, charged every night at ten; normal text size, since his eyes are good. On the night train it dies by 10 pm because he has refreshed the running status since noon (the worry line's reason, shown plainly, never as an age joke).
- SCRIPT: English in Latin letters for everything he types, in all three language modes, because that is how he types and because 'Reached?' marks no gender while 'पहुँच गए?' does (rule 66). Devanagari versions are given in case the owner applies rule 65 to typed messages too.
- SHAPE: he types the way he speaks. Each line is one short whole English sentence with its verb, a capital and a full stop, one fact to a line, in order. Times in figures with a colon (9:40, never 9.40 or 'nine forty').
- NUMBERS: the speech budget carried onto the screen: at most one figure a line, and at least one line in three with none ('Charge your phone tonight.'). No prices, no scores, no numbered lists (no '1.', no ticks: Mami's), no ordinals.
- FOUR FIXED LINES, his only ones with no full sentence: 'Reached?' (after any journey you make without him; it means 'I missed you'; his alone in the cast), 'Noted.' (his reply to a fact you send, a PNR, a platform, 'Reached'; never to a thank-you), 'No need.' (his reply to a thank-you, followed by the next job), 'Good morning.' (under the 6:35 picture).
- FULL STOPS: the family reads them as cross; they are not. The method notes cite NPR's report of a study where a one-word text ending in a full stop read as less sincere. The story can use the misreading once, with Tinku explaining it.
- SPEED: one slow forefinger, the phone in his other palm; he rereads before sending; no typos; autocorrect off.
- REPLIES: within a minute, always, distinct from Mausa ji's 'Ok.' an hour later. Distinct too from Captain sahab's telegram style with no punctuation, Mummy's and Tinku's lowercase, and Mama ji's and Mausi's doubled marks.
- EMOJI AND STICKERS: none. VOICE NOTES: never sends one (he says they have no time in them); he listens to Mausi's in full and replies in text.
- FORWARDS: none, except one good-morning picture to the family at 6:35, after the walk with Captain sahab (a sunflower, or a glass of tea on a sunny windowsill; never a lotus, which is a party symbol, never a marigold, which leans to ritual, never a god or a famous face, rule 3) with 'Good morning.' under it. Never politics, never false news, never a forward he has not read. The readers marked the 6:35 flower true.
- CALLS: anything that matters is a call, made at the minute he said he would call. Nani gets calls, never texts, and good news reaches her by a call before anyone else.
- TIMING: 'Reached?' goes out within ten minutes of your train's due time, by the running status, not the timetable.

## Posture

**Stance.** Straight back, feet close together (Mummy's are planted apart), the whole body tipped clearly forward from the ankles, like a man walking into a wind, even standing still. The folder hangs in one hand at his side; the other hand hangs free or holds the phone low. Head up, chin a little lifted, eyes on the nearest clock or the far end of the platform. Under an upper berth he ducks his head and keeps his back straight. (Stance lives in the scene words, never in the fixed look: the rules review.)

**Sitting.** Sits angled towards the nearest door, on the aisle end of a berth or the end of a row of chairs, one foot already pointing at the door, back straight, the folder across his knees: always ready to stand for a train. Not 'the front edge of the seat', which eight files claim. He sits back only when tired (see expressions). Never forearms on knees (Mausa ji's shape).

**Walk.** Long, quick strides, a step ahead of everyone, upper body leaning forward, the folder swinging at his side in step; he turns his head back to check the group without breaking stride, and stops dead at the exact door or the exact coach. He never runs: he is never late enough to need to.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: upright, the clear forward lean, mouth a straight line, eyes on the nearest clock, the folder at his side.
- PLEASED: one short nod and two taps on the folder; only one corner of his mouth lifts; he looks at the clock, not at the person he is pleased with.
- WORRIED: shoulders lift a little, his eyes narrow at the clock, the lean goes further forward; phone, clock, phone again.

- LAUGHING: silent; his shoulders shake, he looks down and to the side, a hand over his mouth for a second. Only Tinku gets this.

- MOVED: he looks up at the clock and keeps looking, swallows, then lays the folder down without a word, his face in the frame. He never holds anything out towards the viewer (bible section 4).

## Twelve everyday moments (the voice test)

- 1. The lights go out in the middle of dinner.: "Kamaal hai. Aaj bijli bhi time par gayi." | "कमाल है। आज बिजली भी टाइम पर गई।" | "Remarkable. Even the power cut is on time tonight."
- 2. Someone everyone was waiting for arrives two hours late. (to Mama ji, the latecomer; cross: the time in the words people say, then one word twice; the English goes, so 'late' becomes 'der'): "Tum do ghante der se aaye. Khana thanda hai. Thanda." | "तुम दो घंटे देर से आए। खाना ठंडा है। ठंडा।" | "You are two hours late. Dinner is cold. Cold."
- 3. A cousin phones to say they have got their first job. (to the cousin; no verb agrees with the cousin; the congratulation words are Mummy's 'Badhai ho!' and Mami's 'Congratulations.', so his is the plain 'good news' and the train booked ahead): "Ye toh bahut achhi baat hai. / Kis din pahunchna hai? Ticket main book kar deta hoon." | "ये तो बहुत अच्छी बात है। / किस दिन पहुँचना है? टिकट मैं बुक कर देता हूँ।" | "That is very good news. / Which day do you report? I'll book the train."
- 4. The tea someone has just made has far too much sugar in it. (to Tinku, who made it; the loudspeaker joke, in place of the sugar joke Nani owns): "Kripya dhyan dein. Ye chai nahin, sharbat hai." | "कृपया ध्यान दें। ये चाय नहीं, शरबत है।" | "Attention, please. This is not tea. It is sherbet."
- 5. In front of the family, someone shows that they had a fact wrong. (he is the one who was wrong): "Maine 9:40 kaha tha. Galat nikla. Board sahi hai." | "मैंने 9:40 कहा था। ग़लत निकला। बोर्ड सही है।" | "I said 9:40. I was wrong. The board is right."
- 6. Someone they love is leaving, and they say goodbye on a railway platform. (to you, as the train starts): "7:25 wali chal padi. Aakhri dibbe tak main yahin hoon." | "7:25 वाली चल पड़ी। आख़िरी डिब्बे तक मैं यहीं हूँ।" | "The 7:25 is moving. I'm here till the last coach goes."
- 7. Someone thanks them for something they did. (the everyday brush-off, then the next job): "Roz ka kaam hai. / Ab wapsi ka ticket dekhta hoon." | "रोज़ का काम है। / अब वापसी का टिकट देखता हूँ।" | "It's nothing, I do it every day. / Now for the return tickets."
- 8. A neighbour passes on a rumour that the corner sweet shop is closing. (to Captain sahab, who brought it; he asks when, then makes a plan for their walk; no proof demanded): "Achha? Kab se band ho rahi hai, Captain sahab? / Kal subah walk mein udhar se chalenge." | "अच्छा? कब से बंद हो रही है, कप्तान साहब? / कल सुबह की सैर में उधर से चलेंगे।" | "Really? When does it close, Captain sahab? / We'll go that way on tomorrow's walk."
- 9. Heavy rain floods the lane outside the house.: "Patri par bhi paani hoga. 8:15 wali gaadi dekhta hoon." | "पटरी पर भी पानी होगा। 8:15 वाली गाड़ी देखता हूँ।" | "The tracks will be under water too. I'll check the 8:15."
- 10. Someone asks them how they are.: "Sab gaadiyan time par hain. Matlab main bhi." | "सब गाड़ियाँ टाइम पर हैं। मतलब मैं भी।" | "All trains are running on time. So am I."

- 12. A phone call brings good news. (the news goes to Nani first, not into a register): "Kamaal hai. Mummy ji ko abhi batata hoon." | "कमाल है। मम्मी जी को अभी बताता हूँ।" | "Remarkable. I'll tell Mummy ji right away."
