**Essence.** The city hospital's billing-desk manager who runs her daughter's wedding off the clipboard she was handed on her first day at work in 1999: every envelope, key and plate an item, and every item given a status, done, pending or not known. The type in five words: the organised city sister-in-law. What makes her not the type is on the clipboard itself. Under every list lies one soft, yellowed sheet from her own wedding week in this same house, written the night Nani found her alone in the kitchen and showed her where everything was kept. And at the top of every list since the evening the date was fixed sits one line with no number and no status: her daughter's bag. It is the only item she cannot tick. The one thing that catches her out all week is the one thing never on any list: Nani hands her, the one from outside, the first slice of the cake, and she laughs.

## Inner life

Born in 1981 in the big city where she still lives (unnamed, rule 3). Her father retired from a clerk's desk in the city's water department; her mother's family is Bengali, settled in the city two generations back, so she grew up in Hindi outside the house and a little Bengali with her mother's sisters. That side is chapter 5's circle (the continuity fix), which gives its three Bengali names a home; nothing on screen marks it. At 18 she joined the billing counter of a big city hospital as a trainee and did her commerce degree in the evenings; it took five years and she is still proud of it. The counter taught her two habits she has never dropped: she says 'Thank you' to every stranger who stands in front of her, and she asks every family at the counter about their children, because families wait there at their worst hour. In 2000 a young man from a small town came to the counter to pay a friend's bill and disputed every line; she made him read it line by line, and he came back the next day with the right change and a box of sweets. That was Mama ji. They married in 2001 at Nani's house, and her family travelled a night by train to a town they had never seen (Mama ji's file). She spent her own wedding week not knowing where anything was kept, which room was whose, or whom to ask. On the last night Nani found her standing alone in the kitchen, and without a word walked her round it, tin by tin and shelf by shelf. Mami wrote it all down on the back of a billing form on her clipboard; that sheet, soft and yellowed now, has lain under every list she has written since. Their one daughter was born in 2002. She has run the billing desk since 2017: a shift of six, every insurance form, every family at the counter at its worst hour, which is why she is calm when others are not. A two-bedroom flat in the city; at the desk by eight, the day's list at eleven at night. Since Nani's fall in May she phones Nani at eight every evening for the BP reading Nani speaks into the phone, and sends the doctor a numbered log each month. This December her daughter marries from Nani's house, the same house, and this time she knows where everything is kept, because she wrote it down in 2001.

WANTS THIS YEAR: a wedding with no surprises: every envelope, key, room and plate accounted for, and nothing going wrong in her husband's family's house. ## Voice

**Register.** Medium and even, in two voices, and the switch between them is her tell. THE DESK VOICE is for jobs, the list, vendors and strangers. Its lead shape is the readback: 'So.' | 'तो।' | 'Toh.', then one whole sentence that hands your own plan back to you, then 'Correct?' | 'सही?' | 'Sahi?', and she waits for the answer. Its second shape is the status pair: a thing, its status, a full stop, then the next pair ('Keys, pending. Envelopes, done.'; in Hindi mode with no comma, 'टेंट लग गया। रसोइया अभी बाक़ी।'). The item is named, never numbered: no ordinals, no counts, no prices, no clock times. THE HOME VOICE takes over the moment a family feeling arrives (a goodbye, a cousin's news, her husband worried, a report): the statuses stop, and she speaks short whole sentences of plain, warm Hindi with their hai or hoon ('Fikr mat karo. Mummy ji ka BP normal hai.', 'Mujhe yakeen hai.', 'Ab kya karna hai?'). A city manager talks in statuses at the desk and stops at home, and so does she, which is why the desk voice is funny when she turns it on her husband and used in no tender moment. A bubble runs 4 to 10 words, most often 6 to 9; usually one bubble, at most two. Crisp, unhurried and never raised: no exclamation mark in any bubble, 'Congratulations.' and 'Ish.' included, and never the shout (the chapter's shout is Mama ji's). The same pace in a crisis as at tea, which is why people calm down near her. Firm with vendors, polite with elders, sly with her husband. Talked over, she raises the pen, point up, says nothing, and finishes when the room is quiet. When cross she goes slower, turns the clipboard round to face the person, reads the line aloud from it ('Yahan likha hai.') and closes with an icy 'Thank you' to a vendor, or a flat 'Correct?' to family. Apart from the cast: not Mummy's comma lists of jobs and people with no status ('Aur kuch?') or her home things in counter English; not Mausa ji's subjectless verdicts on things; not Captain sahab's call then reason; not Papa's whole sentences with a time in them; not Nani's jobs handed to 'koi'; not Mama ji's run-ons and 'Oho!'. A script can count it: about half her lines carry a status word or end on 'Correct?', and every one of those is about a job, a list or a vendor; her family feeling lines carry none, and most of them have hai or hoon; no line carries an ordinal, a figure or '!'.

**Hindi and English.** City whole-clause switching, the younger urban pattern in the realism notes (the Dehradun code-switching study): she switches at the full stop, a whole Hindi clause and then a whole English one, or the other way round ('Meri shaadi ki bhi list thi. Still on file.'). Inside a Hindi clause the desk crosses over in two ways: its nouns (list, item, form, file, claim, receipt, balance, payment) and, as with most city people under fifty, an office English verb with kar or ho ('List ab tum handle karo.', 'Claim main file karti hoon.', 'Receipt pe sign kar dijiye.', 'Bahut help ho gayi.'). Her English verbs come from the office (file, sign, check, handle, note), never from the home: Mummy turns home things into counter English ('MCB check karo', 'mains off karo'), and Mama ji talks logistics. Her English statuses in Hinglish: done, pending, not known, safe, approved, in progress, closed, still on file, all clear, correct. In Hindi mode a status becomes the everyday Hindi a city office speaks (हो गया, लग गया, बाक़ी, पता नहीं, मंज़ूर, चालू, बंद, ठीक, फ़ाइल में), with पेंडिंग and स्टेटस kept only for her two dry jokes, and with no comma between a Hindi subject and its verb, because spoken Hindi does not pause there; a colon comes only before a label with no verb ('स्टेटस: मेहमान।', 'तुम्हारे मामा जी: अब भी पेंडिंग।'). The commas stay in Hinglish and English pairs. Her Hindi carries people, food, rooms and elders, and the town's everyday Urdu-rooted words sit in it as they do in anyone's (fikr, zaroor, yakeen). 'Thank you' and 'Sorry' are the counter's words, English in every mode (थैंक यू, सॉरी), for vendors and strangers only; with family her sorry is Hindi ('मुझसे छूट गया') and her thanks is plain ('बहुत मदद हो गई'). Polite Hindi to every elder and every vendor (आप, -इए). One Bengali word, her mother's: the exclamation 'Ish.' Nothing else from Bengali, and never a spelled accent. Apart from Mama ji, who leads with fast English and runs his clauses together on 'aur haan', she is clause by clause, each one closed, and her English is the desk's, never logistics talk, slang or 'Don't worry'.

## Posture

**Stance.** Bolt upright, weight even on both feet, feet a hand apart and parallel, chin level, shoulders down. The clipboard braced along her left forearm and tipped out from her chest, her right hand at it with the pen, as if mid-line on a round; when she is only listening, the pen goes into the clip and her right hand drops to her side. She never leans on a wall, a pole or a person, and never puts a hand on her hip. Half a head taller than her husband, she does not stoop to hide it.

**Sitting.** She does not take a chair while there are guests to come: chairs are on the list, and they are for guests. She perches on the nearest hard thing that is not a guest's chair, the gate step, the edge of a table, a stack of plastic chairs, one knee crossed over the other, the clipboard on her top knee and the pen moving. (The draft's front-edge perch is gone: eight files shared it, comics review.) On Friday at eleven at night, on the low step of the blue gate with one sandal off, still writing. At the first slice she does not sit at all: she bends from the hips to Nani seated, the clipboard face down on the table beside her. Sitting back in a chair at last is Mummy's December beat, not hers.

**Walk.** Quick, short, even steps in a straight line down the middle of the lane, her head turning left and right as she passes things on the list, the way a ward round moves; she stops dead, writes one line and walks on. She never runs and never hurries (Mama ji hurries). The clipboard stays tipped out on her forearm; the bag stays still at her side.

**Gestures**

- THE CLICK: before she speaks, her right thumb clicks the top of her pen once. (Tinku taps her pencil twice and Papa taps twice, so Mami never taps.)
- THE TICK: one short, hard downward stroke of the pen on the list, her chin dipping with it; it is her 'Tick.' with or without the word.
- THE CLIP: when she stops to listen or takes a call, she slides the pen into the clipboard's steel clip, so there is only ever one pen in the picture and one hand free.
- THE RAISED PEN: pen held up, point to the sky, at shoulder height, when someone talks over her; she says only the item's place ('Three.') and waits.
- DOWN THE LIST: she counts by running the pen's point down the card, line by line, never on her fingers (Nani counts with one finger, Tinku shows totals with an open hand).
- THE TURN: when cross, she turns the clipboard round to face the other person and reads from it.
- THE TOP LINE: before she writes anything new, the pen's point rests a moment on the top line, the one with no status, then moves down. Nobody notices but the player.
- IDLE HANDS: the left forearm stays under the clipboard; the right hand turns the pen end over end once, then stops.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: level gaze, the mouth a straight line, the clipboard tipped out on her forearm, the pen at it, weight even.
- PLEASED: one short nod and the tick; the corners of her mouth press in rather than up; no grin.
- WORRIED: the arched eyebrows lift, she reads the list again from the top, the pen's point moving faster down the lines; her feet do not move.
- CROSS: very still; the pen stops; she turns the clipboard round to face the other person and reads from it in a slow even voice ('It's written here.'); her eyebrows go level. Never a raised voice, never hands on hips.
- LAUGHING (rare, at your Mama ji, at herself, and once at the first slice): a short surprised laugh with her head tipped back, the clipboard lowered to her waist for one beat, then back to her forearm.
- TIRED (Friday, eleven at night): on the gate step, one sandal off, her shoulders down for once, the clipboard on her knee, smoothing the yellowed sheet before she writes.
- GRIEF (the suitcase, Saturday evening): the clipboard lowered to her waist in her left hand, the pen held still above its top line in her right, her eyes shut and her mouth pressed into a line. Alone; nobody comforts her; over in one panel; never played as a joke or as weakness.
