**Essence.** A retired lineman, 34 years on the electricity board's poles and night shutdowns, with big scarred hands, who says two blunt words and does twenty kind things. He keeps the steel chairs outside the ward door all night so you can sleep, peels a sweet lime with his bare thumb and sets half of it down beside you, stands up when the doctor goes by, and leaves the room if anyone thanks him. The type in five words: the quiet uncle who fixes. What makes him not the type, the same way every time: blunt in words, but soft in deeds; and two things only a lineman carries, the night-gang habit of handing out halves of whatever fruit there is, and a scar from the day a dead line came back on under his hand, which is why nobody near him touches a switch until he nods. The line people quote back: 'Hmm. Ho jayega.' | 'हम्म। हो जाएगा।' | 'Hmm. It'll get done.' Who would say 'I know this person': Farida, the staff nurse, who has seen him outside her ward on a hundred nights (cast map); and Mr Natarajan, 61 and retired, who knows what the first year with no duty to go to feels like.

## Inner life

Born in 1968 in a small town (never named, rule 3), the eldest of four. After Class 12 he took the state electricity board's lineman training; the job letter came in January 1992 and he married Mausi that November (married 34 years, cast map). For twenty years he climbed poles; for fourteen more he ran a line gang out of the sub-station near the railway junction, the man the night calls went to. On night shutdowns the gang shared whatever fruit someone had brought, and whoever peeled it handed out the halves; he still cannot eat a whole one, and he is the only one in the family who can take the thick peel off a sweet lime with his thumb. In 1998 a line he had shut down was switched back on while his hand was on it: a pale, shiny burn scar the size of a coin sits on the back of his right hand, and since that day nobody touches a wire near him with the switch on. He retired last December at 58 on the board's voluntary scheme, two years early, because his knees would no longer take the poles (team judgement: we assume a retirement age of 60 for his board and have opened no source; readers confirm). This is his first year with no duty roster, and he still wakes at five. He and Mausi live in a two-room house near the junction, fifteen minutes from Mummy's shop; the front room is Mausi's tuition class, whose fans he rewires every summer. Since he retired Mausi has hidden the stepladder, because of the knees; he borrows the neighbour's. Their two grown children (about 32 and 29) work in other cities, ring on Sundays and want their parents to move nearer them; he answers 'Hmm', which means no. What fills his day now: neighbours who call 'ek minute' for a switch, a fan or a meter; the customers with wiring trouble whom Mummy sends him, so he is never idle; twenty minutes asleep sitting up after lunch; cricket with the sound off. He takes a blood-pressure tablet, which Mausi tells everyone about. For thirty years he has fixed Nani's house wiring without being asked and held her spare house key (Nani's file), and every winter he fits a new rubber foot on her cane. Her lane knows him as the son-in-law who fixes things; a few still call him 'Lineman sahab'. In May, the evening of Nani's fall, he carried her to the auto at seven. The fall has no cause on screen or in writing (the default; see role, question 6). What it did to him: it frightened him that eighty is not a number that waits, and he has not said 'kal' (tomorrow) about a job since. Before she came home he had lit her back step and laid a rubber mat on it, and nobody has mentioned it. FEARS: that his tongue undoes what his hands do, so he keeps it to two words; that retirement makes him a man who sits; and, after May, that Amma ji's time is shorter than any 'kal'. CONTRADICTION, shown the same way every time: blunt in words, but soft in deeds. He calls a man lazy, then peels a sweet lime and leaves you half. Every bubble of his is followed, in the same panel or the next, by something his hands do. He is never the silent patriarch: his few words always lead to an act, and he never has the last say on a family decision.

## Voice

**Register.** Low, slow and plain: the quietest voice in the cast, with long gaps his hands fill. Most bubbles are two to four counted words and none is more than six (names and kin words not counted, as in rule 62; his own cap, well inside rule 62's ten), usually in two short pieces: an opener ('Hmm.', 'Hain?', 'Lo.' or the thing itself), then the state of a thing. His usual sentence is about a thing, not a person: the wire, the bench, the board, the food, the job, given a state or a verdict. Verdicts on things are often bare ('Taar dheela.', 'Board theek.') and sometimes keep their verb ('Bench theek hai.'); comfort and kind lines always keep their 'hai' ('Achha hai.', 'Amma ji mazboot hain.'), so he never talks in telegrams. His own jobs come with the pronoun dropped ('Aata hoon.', 'Garam karta hoon.', 'Ho gaya.'). His orders are small and practical: rest ('So jao.'), hands off live current ('Switch mat chhoona.'), and the next little job as he hands something over ('Lo, Mausi se baat karo.', 'Haath dho lo.'). He answers more than he asks; his few questions are about sleep, water or a drip. Nearly every bubble is followed by something his hands do, and when a beat needs more he speaks again after a deed, so a run is two bubbles with an action between them. He talks most to whoever should be resting (you, at the ward). His few words are kind and busy, never the silent head of the house. Told apart from the other short voices by shape and source, not by owned words: Nani gives people orders and counter proverbs; Captain sahab makes a cricket call, then gives the reason; Tinku gives a score with a tag; Mummy lists jobs; Mami reads things back; you name the listener. He reports on things, names almost nobody, counts nothing and leaves himself out.

**Hindi and English.** Bare, plain Hindi, the plainest in the cast after Nani's, with no proverb or image of his own (that is Nani's and Masterji's ground). The common fillers are his as much as anyone's: achha, haan, chalo, arre and theek hai come in mid-line, sparingly, as they would from any man of 58; so do everyday Urdu-rooted words such as waqt, fikr and zaroor ('Waqt lagega.'). English comes in from his work. First, the lineman's nouns, used as Hindi nouns: current, line, fuse, load, meter, earthing, board, switch, wiring, joint. Second, now and then, the everyday Hinglish English verb with ho or kar, and his is always about what the line or the current did, with the thing as the subject ('Line trip ho gayi.', 'Short hua hai.', 'Wiring check ho gayi.'). That is how a lineman really talks, and it keeps his thing-first shape; Mummy's English verbs are counter jobs she hands to people ('MCB check karo'), his are what the current did. The hospital words everyone uses: BP, drip, ward, X-ray. Where everyday Hindi has its own word he takes it: 'bijli' for power, 'taar' for wire, जोड़ for a joint in Hindi mode. No whole English clause (Mama ji's and Mami's), and no English feeling words such as tension, relax or chill. Never 'main switch' or 'mains' in Hinglish, because 'main' reads as मैं. Hinglish mode is Roman Hindi with those nouns. English mode is plain, natural British English, as short as the Hindi: the thing first and himself left out wherever English allows it ('Board's fine. Open the shutter.', 'Lights on, then.'), with the full everyday phrase wherever a clipped one would sound translated ('Get any sleep?', 'It'll take time.', 'No harm done.'). One English word for each work verdict, so the English sort still hears him: mazboot is 'solid' of work and 'strong' of a person, dheela is 'loose', theek is 'fine'.

- THE KNOCK: after he has looked at something (a switch, a bulb holder, a latch, a bag's zip) he gives it one knock with the knuckle of his right forefinger, a lineman's rap, then one nod.
- STANDING FOR THE DOCTOR: up in one movement, arms at his sides, one small nod, and he stays standing until the round has gone by.
- YES AND NO: one slow dip of the chin for yes, one slow turn of the head for no; never the head bobble, which the realism notes call a stock caricature.
- WORRY: his left thumb rubs the back of his right hand, where the old scar is.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: still as a post, bent forward, forearms on his knees, mouth a flat line under the beard, eyes on the door.
- PLEASED: the creases at his eyes deepen and the beard lifts at the cheeks; he looks down at his hands, not at you, and peels a little faster.
- WORRIED: the left thumb on the back of his right hand; he stands and goes to look for himself; his jaw works under the beard.
- CROSS: silent, brows down, jaw set; he picks up the nearest job and does it twice as hard; never louder.
- LAUGHING (once a year at most): silent; his shoulders shake once or twice, eyes shut, mouth closed, head dipped.
- TIRED: head back against the wall, eyes shut, hands still on his knees; he never slumps sideways, and his eyes open at any sound.
- MOVED: looks away to the window, clears his throat, and busies his hands, peeling far too carefully.
- ALERT (the doctor, a spark, a cry): on his feet before anyone else, arms at his sides, chin up.
