# Tinku (टिंकू), 21

**Role.** Your younger sister. On first sight she is labelled 'Tinku, your sister' | 'टिंकू, आपकी बहन'. This replaces the draft's 'Tinku, the youngest', which is not a relation (chapter 1 check, rule 65), and it still gives you no gender. She is in the household. She asks all of chapter 4, the lane cup, in the Cricket look, and she is one of its two judging voices, with Captain sahab (rule 22). She is one of the three women askers (rule 37). A round character. She is never on a card, never the comic one, and never doubts a report. Painter's role word: 'the ponytailed young woman'. It names her strongest anchor and no trade, place, title or relation. 'The young scorer' would pull cricket kit or a scoreboard into the flood and the drain (rules review), and 'the young woman' alone is already Tara's in ROLE in cutscenes.py.

**Essence.** The family's scorer. She is a final-year commerce student who works Mummy's Saturday counter and has kept the lane cup's book since she was twelve. She keeps a tally of everything: runs, promises, Papa's corrections, who said what and when. Ask her about herself and she answers with somebody else's score. She is the third woman of Nani's line to sit at a table keeping count (Nani at her grocery counter, Mummy at the shop, Tinku at the scorer's table), and the only one keeping it for a game rather than a till. The picture a reader keeps: a wiry young woman on a plastic chair with her knees drawn up and a wide book across them. Quoted line: 'Third time. Three-nil.' | 'तीसरी बार। तीन-ज़ीरो।' | 'Teesri baar. 3-0.' Players who would say 'I know this person': Meena, for the quiet skill with numbers, and Ravi, the younger one at the counter of a family business.

## Inner life

Tinku was born in 2005 in the junction town, a month early and small, six years after Mummy opened the shop. Nani held her and said she was as light as a tinka, a straw, and that a straw bends and does not break. The family turned tinka into Tinku. It is Nani's pet name, about smallness, so it carries no 'tomboy' story (Hindi-first readers still check how it reads to players; owner question 1). The name has stuck to her the way 'the youngest' has, and she minds both. At eight she sat on the shop counter counting fuses into tens. Every summer until the grocery shut in 2016, Nani taught her to keep accounts in the grocery's old cloth-bound ledger, and Tinku still crosses her sevens the way Nani does. When she was twelve, the lane cup's old scorer could no longer see the far end of the lane. Captain sahab handed the book to the girl who had been keeping her own tally on the wall in chalk. She has scored every cup since, and this August is her tenth. She buys a new scorebook every season. The nine old ones stand in a row on the shelf above her bed, and the back page of every one is blank: the page a scorer keeps for her own notes. She is in the final year of a BCom at the town college. Every Saturday she works the shop counter for a day's pay. Her first three months of that pay bought a cheap plain pair of black headphones from a mobile shop on the station road, so she can follow the commentary on her phone while she scores (the radio is Nani's). In March she found the form for the district cricket association's scorers' course. She printed it, folded it into the back of the scorebook, and has not filled it in. Its last date is 20 August. Everyone calls her Tinku. Her given name is used only at college and is never shown, because the household marks no community (rule 21).

WANT, THIS YEAR: to count as more than 'the youngest', with a place on the district association's scorers' course (owner question 2). WANT FROM YOU: to be treated as an equal. On cup day she hands you the decisions ('Today, your call') to see what you do with them, and she is scoring you while she asks. FEAR: being 'the youngest' for ever, left in the junction town keeping everyone else's numbers while everyone calls you. Under that: that she is only useful for counting other people's things. CONTRADICTION: she keeps exact score of everyone else's game, but never writes down her own. It shows the same way every time. Asked about herself (her marks, her plans, the form), she answers with somebody else's score ('My marks? Papa's corrected four times today.'), and the back page of her scorebook stays blank. This contradiction is hers alone in the cast; the player's file keeps only 'everyone calls you, nobody asks' (comics review). THE COST, ON SCREEN (India review: every capable woman pays one real price): her silence nearly loses her the course. The form has sat in the back of the book since March, and its last date is 20 August. She sends it only on cup night, after Captain sahab says the district should see her book (chapter 4, proposed). Her year turns there, and it pays off in December, when the letter comes and she writes on the back page for the first time (chapter 5).

## Relationships

- NANI: Nani taught her accounts on the grocery's old ledger, every summer from eight to eleven, and gave her her name. Tinku keeps Nani's carrom scores and medicine times (8, 2, 8) inside the scorebook's front cover. She is the only grandchild who drops every English word for Nani: plain polite Hindi, आप, every word in full. Nani is the only person she switches her phone to the Hindi keyboard for. Nani calls her 'scorer' | 'स्कोरर', the one English word Nani took up for her, and never 'the youngest'. Friction: Nani cheats at carrom, and Tinku writes it down. Tenderness: after the fall, Tinku knows every dose without looking, and when Nani comes home from the ward Tinku is at the door: 'Eighty, not out, Nani.' (proposed; see year_arc). Nani greets her with a hand on her shoulder, never on her head (rules review: an elder's hand on a head reads as a blessing).
- MUMMY: Tinku works her Saturday counter and keeps the stock tally on her phone. Mummy is hardest on her because Tinku is most like her: both answer every question with a number, Mummy with a time or a price, Tinku with a score. Friction: both want to hold the count. In the July flood they argue over whose tally of wet stock is right, and both are. Tenderness: Mummy pays her a full day's rate every Saturday, the same as any hand, and Tinku knows that is Mummy's way of saying she is good at it. Tinku grew past her at fifteen, by a clear half head, and Mummy pretends not to notice (Mummy's file). She says आप to Mummy, exactly as to Papa.
- PAPA: she is the only one who can make him laugh, by scoring his corrections ('Papa, that's the fourth not-9:45 today.'). He taught her to read a timetable at seven, and she taught him to read a scorebook at fifty. She reads his messages with full stops correctly and explains them to the family: 'He's not cross. That's just his full stop.' Friction: he wants her to sit the railway's recruitment exam for a safe job, and she has not told him about the course (the same exam as Papa's file; the draft's bank exams did not match). Tenderness: on the night train he counts the family, she counts them again behind him, and they nod to each other. In December he books her train to the course without being asked (Papa's file, proposal). She says आप and 'Papa'.
- MAMA JI: the uncle whose gifts are too expensive to use; one is still in its box. She scores his promises: 'You said March, Mama ji. Nil for one.' Friction: he calls her 'the youngest' at every family function, and it is the one thing that makes her lose her temper (she reads him his whole record, fast). Tenderness: in December, when his numbers keep growing, she quietly keeps the real count for him, and it matches Mami's. She says आप and 'Mama ji'.
- MAUSI: Tinku did her Class 3 sums in Mausi's tuition class in her front room. Mausi still checks her sums, tells everyone her marks ('Sau mein sau, hai na?' is Mausi's tease, so Tinku no longer uses it) and asks about her plans three questions at a time. Tinku answers with a score. Friction: Mausi talks over her. Tenderness: Mausi remembers that at nine Tinku wanted to be a cricket commentator, and she is the only one who still brings it up. आप, 'Mausi'.
- MAUSA JI: they watch matches side by side without talking. She scores, and he says 'Hmm' at every wicket. He is the only adult who never calls her 'the youngest', and the only person she lets turn the pages of her scorebook. आप, 'Mausa ji'.
- MASTERJI: he stitched her first school uniform, and he still says she is too thin for any pattern. She brings him his eleven o'clock tea on Saturdays, and he checks the time by it. Friction: he says she slouches over that book. Tenderness: in the flood week she counts his wet bolts of cloth too, without being asked. आप, 'Masterji'.
- CAPTAIN SAHAB: he taught her to keep the lane cup's book at twelve, and in August he says the district should see it. She teases him about his one state trial ('Your one trial again, Captain sahab?'; no year on screen, rule 2), always with आप. She is the successor he has not named. Friction: she once scored a no-ball against his own lane in front of everyone, and he has never said she was right. Tenderness: he gives her his chair in the shade. Their voices never cross: he makes the calls (the toss, 'Straight bat', the umpire's verdicts) and she keeps the score; the toss line is only ever his (comics review). In a panel the painter tells them apart by shape: her seated angles, ponytail and wide book against his tall, standing, side-on figure, hat and ball, both against one mid-grey plastered wall.
- MAMI: Mami thinks Tinku should do a proper accounts course, and says so at every function. Tinku keeps a tally of how often: 'That's four, Mami.' Friction: Mami asks her to take the headphones off for the wedding week, and she does not. Tenderness: the two list-keepers end the wedding checking each other's counts, and Mami's sixty matches Tinku's sixty. आप, 'Mami'.
- YOU: her older sibling, and her rival for being the one everyone calls. She keeps one tally nobody knows about: how many times the family called you this year. She uses no address word for you, or 'oye' (you answer 'oye, scorer', in the You file). Never bhai, bro, didi or bhaiya (rule 8), and no longer 'suno', which is Mausi's and Mummy's (comics review). In Hindi her तुम lines to you are only commands, 'who should' questions and lines where you are not the doer (rule 66). Never तू, which can carry contempt (realism notes 1.1, sources [3] and [5]). You say तुम to her. Friction: Mama ji calls you, not her. Tenderness: in August she hands you the calls, and in December you are the first person she tells about the letter, before Mummy. You are the only one who names her habit out loud ('Scorer, write your own score too.', in the You file).
- THE LANE SIDE (chapter 4's circle): she knows all ten from ten years of scoring. In her own bubbles she never names, points at or describes anyone on a decision's cards before or during that decision (rule 36). Her judging notes stay in game words, fall as often on men as on women (the chapter 4 check counted 2 and 2), and never touch the job being asked. ## Voice

**Register.** Quick, light, dry and teasing. Her sentence shape is scoreboard shorthand, then a tag question: the result as a score ('Three-nil', 'Nil for one', 'Not out'), then 'haan?' or a question back ('Third time, haan?'). In chapter 4 her questions are the decisions themselves ('Who goes in?'), asked plainly with no tag (rule 37 wants crisp asker lines). Her lines run two to six words, except to Nani, to whom she speaks in whole, careful, polite Hindi. She teases with scores, never with insults. When cross she does not go quiet like most of the house: she speeds up and reads out the whole record in one breath, flat and exact ('March, nil. May, nil. August, nil.'), then shuts the book and pulls the ear cups over her ears. In chapter 4 she is the asker: crisp and warm, never the comic one (rule 37). Of chapter 4's speaking pair she is the one who speaks in numbers; Captain sahab speaks in calls, so the two stay apart with the names covered (comics review).

**Hindi and English.** A Hindi frame with English nouns, the way a BCom student in a junction town talks at home (India review). The draft's 'English clauses with Hindi tags' leaned on the Dehradun speakers, who were upper middle class and English-medium; the realism notes themselves say a small-town shop family uses less English (realism notes 1.1). With you: Hindi sentences carrying English cricket and commerce nouns and a few English tags ('Third time, haan?', 'Your call.'), as in 'Aaj faisla tumhara.' Her English shows more in her typing than in her speech. Her English nouns are only ones everyone knows: run, over, wicket, out, not out, nil, final, keeper, tally, total, extras, score, and toss only when she reports Captain sahab's. Words the players' judges found hard, such as slips and off-spin, stay out of her bubbles (rule 2). With Nani: plain polite Hindi, every word in full, आप, and no English except Nani's own nouns (doctor, BP) and 'scorer', Nani's word for her. A young person's talk with family differs from talk with friends (realism notes 1.1, source [9]). With Mummy and Papa: the same Hindi frame, with आप. Her teasing rides on numbers, so it never needs a familiar 'you' to an elder. With the chapter voices: polite, with ji. In Hindi mode she says तीन-ज़ीरो, not the formal तीन-शून्य (rule 65 asks for everyday spoken words; Hindi-first readers confirm). In romanised Hinglish bubbles every number is a figure ('3-0', '8, 2, 8'), so a number is never misread as a word (India review: 'saath' is both sixty and 'with').

**Habits**

- Gives the result as a score: 'Three-nil.' 'Nil for one.' 'Not out.' (This replaces the draft's 'number first, then the thing', which is Papa's tic.)
- Ends a tease with 'haan?' to soften it: 'Third time, haan?'
- Repeats a number back as a check: 'Fourteen? Fourteen.'
- Keeps a running tally of people's habits, out loud: 'That's four, Mami.'
- Says 'In the book.' when anyone makes a promise; she means it is written down. (This replaces 'Noted.', which is Papa's reply.)
- Answers any question about herself with somebody else's score. This is her contradiction, and it shows the same way every time.
- Hands a choice over with 'your call'. It is a test, not a shrug.
- When cross, speeds up and reads the whole record in one breath, instead of going quiet.

**Walk.** Quick short steps, ponytail swinging. She often walks backwards for a few steps while she finishes a sentence to someone behind her. The book is under one arm, so her free hand can hold up a score.

**Gestures**

- Spins the pencil once round her thumb before she says a score. It replaces the draft's double tap, which Papa and Mausa ji also had (comics review). A close-up gesture.
- Holds up fingers to show a score, three for 'third time', with the other hand closed. Nani counts heads with one pointing finger; Tinku shows totals with an open hand.
- Slides one ear cup up off her ear when she is really listening, and back down when she is done with the conversation.
- Holds the pencil between her teeth while she thinks, and then it is in no hand.
- Lifts the pencil once when the umpire signals, as a scorer does to acknowledge it (team knowledge, not from an opened source).

**Expressions**

- Neutral: chin down over the book, ponytail still, the pencil turning in her fingers, eyes narrowed a little.
- Pleased: a quick grin that shows the gap in her teeth; up off the chair onto her toes, a fist round the pencil.
- Worried: the shut book pressed to her chest with both arms, shoulders up, the pencil between her teeth.
- Cross: fast, not quiet (comics review: most of the house goes quiet when cross). She reads out the whole record in one breath, snaps the book shut one-handed and pulls both ear cups up over her ears, the only time the headphones go on her head.
- Laughing: head back, ponytail swinging behind the chair, one hand over the gap in her teeth, knees pulled up.
- Tired: slumped sideways on the plastic chair, the book open face down on her knees, headphones round her neck with nothing playing.
- Moved: looks down at the book and runs her thumb along its edge, and does not look up. The one time she cannot keep score.
