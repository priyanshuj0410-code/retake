**Essence.** The youngest, who left Nani's town for the city at twenty and has loved his family by phone ever since. He sells water pumps to builders, and his real stock is his phone book: every tent man, cook and bandmaster he has ever met, known by first name and by how their mothers are. Generous with money, with numbers and with feeling; sure that more is safer; worried about everyone but himself. In five words: the worrier who rings first. He is one of the two elders who say feelings out loud (Mausi says her hurt; he says his love: 'Amma, I missed you'), and he hugs everyone he can reach. His contradiction: he worries about everything, but plans nothing. He makes every number bigger and leaves the how to others, because doing the how would mean being there. The other half of it is his gift: nobody plans less, and nobody else can make the whole district turn up.

## Inner life

Born in 1979 in Nani's town, the youngest of three. His sisters half raised him while Nani ran the grocery in her front room; Mausi still calls him 'Munna', and Nani and Mummy call him 'Chhote'. He was the one expected to take the grocery on. Instead, in the summer of 1999, the year Mummy opened her shop, he took the night train to the city for a sales interview, in a shirt Masterji stitched free 'for luck'. He reached the city in the first monsoon without an umbrella and was soaked through at the interview. He got the job anyway, still says it was the shirt, bought a big black umbrella that evening, and has carried one like it every day since, rain or not. This one is the third. Nani ran the grocery alone until 2016. He has never said he minds; he sends money to everyone instead. In 2000 he disputed a friend's bill line by line at a city hospital's billing counter; the young woman behind it made him read it line by line, and he came back the next day with the right change and a box of sweets (Mami's file). They married in 2001 at Nani's house, in Nani's lane, and Mami's family travelled a night by train to a town they had never seen to fill it. He has owed them a loud welcome ever since. Today he is area sales manager for a water-pump dealer, out at building sites by auto from nine till dark, phone at his ear. He lives with Mami, who manages a hospital billing desk, and their daughter, 24, in a two-bedroom flat high up in a city housing society whose lift breaks every monsoon. He rings Nani at eight every morning before his first site; she says 'बस, सब ठीक' and hangs up first (Nani's file). He sends Mummy stock leads, and money she never spends: it sits in its own account (Mummy's file). He books his train the night before and rings Papa to fix it; Papa fixes it, says nothing, and meets the train himself at 5 am (Papa's file). He forwards every weather warning for Nani's district. His daughter works in the city, chose her own match, and marries in December. She is never drawn, named or put on a card, and her wedding carries no talk of gifts to anyone, because the household is never the one doing the wrong (rule 27). Chapter 5's 'talk of a dowry' belongs only to the relatives' own past, on their cards. In June, after Nani's fall, he moved the wedding from a city hall to Nani's house, as his own had been, and lost the hall's advance. Mami mentions the advance; he never does.

WANT THIS YEAR: his daughter married from Nani's house, with every one of them there and the lane too, more people than anyone expects. In his own words to Mami: 'This time Amma doesn't catch a train.' WANT FROM YOU: to hear 'Sorted, Mama ji' so he can stop worrying. You are his eyes in Nani's town, the one he rings instead of his sister. FEAR: that Nani grew old while he was on the phone in the city; that he is the son who was not there. In May he is not there. He is on a waitlisted ticket, booked the night before, when she slips on her back step, and he reaches her the day after she is home. CONTRADICTION ('X, but Y'): he worries about everything, but plans nothing. It shows the same way every time: a bigger number, then the job handed to someone else ('Ek kaam karo...'). Under it, the competence that keeps this from being 'the man who has to be managed' (India review): he cannot plan, but he can make anyone come. The cook and the tent come in December's busiest week because he rang them by name, and his bigger number is right once: the lane comes for Nani's cake and fills the ten extra chairs he had stacked by the gate. Mami is right about the plates; he is right about the people. Under that: the man who is never still stands still only twice in the year, with Nani's palm on his head at her gate and on the gate step at midnight beside Mausa ji. ## Voice

**Register.** Fast, warm and run-on: a man walking between two building sites with his right arm up, talking to someone far away. His shape is the job that grows and the worry that stacks. Inside one bubble he chains clauses with commas, and the 'ek kaam' he started with becomes three ('Ek kaam karo, gate pe raho, sabko andar lo.'); his questions are worry checks about named people, stacked, and he waits only for the answer to the last one, 'Pakka?'. A long thought runs on into a second or third bubble, picked up with 'aur' or, once a scene at most, 'aur haan'. Every bubble still ends cleanly, on a full stop, a question mark or a '!', so on a phone nothing looks cut off. That is what tells his long runs from the other three: Papa's are whole sentences in order with the times in them, Masterji's a verdict and a wander, Mausi's a tripled word and a question she answers herself; his is a list that keeps growing and a stack he waits on, and he never answers his own question. The common fillers (arre, achha, haan, chalo, dekho, bas, theek hai) come into his talk the way they come into anyone's; what marks him is the shape and where his words come from: people, health and city logistics. Feeling is said out loud, warmly, to one person at a time, with a hug: he is one of the two elders who say it in plain words (Mausi says hers to the whole room, loud; he says his to one face). Fear he says only to Mami. Humour is big and kind and aimed at himself (he plans nothing, he talks too much, he cannot say no to sweets), never at anyone's age, town, shop or work. He talks most to people far away, Mummy first ('Didi!'); in chapter 5, with the phone in his pocket, to everyone at the gate. With Nani alone he slows to short whole sentences, the only person he slows for, and lets her finish before he speaks. When cross he does not go quiet or slow (that is Mami's tell in his chapter): he still asks after the other man's family first, then the run-on stops dead and breaks into single words, one downward chop of his free hand on each ('Aaj. Abhi. Yahin.'). Never at family, a woman, a worker's dignity or anyone on a card. When anyone brings a report of harassment or a dowry demand he believes it at once and moves ('Main aa raha hoon. Abhi.'), with no 'Pakka?' and no question back (rule 22). His voice is never raised except the chapter's one shout at 5.03 (rule 35). Warm, and never the comic one while he is the asker (rule 37).

**Hindi and English.** The most English elder. Hinglish: a whole English clause first, at speed, then a Hindi landing ('Don't worry, Bade Jija ji ko bulao.'). His English carries the jobs and the comfort (don't worry, no problem, sorted, last minute, booking, advance, I'll miss you), and his Hindi lands on family, food and health (Amma, Didi, ghar, sab, khaana, dawai, man, roz). Like most city people under fifty he also builds everyday verbs from an English word with kar or ho, and his are people and logistics verbs: 'miss kiya', 'call karo', 'book kar dijiye', 'check hua', 'dispatch kar dijiye'. Mummy's English verbs are the shop counter turned on home things ('MCB check karo', 'mains off karo'), so the two stay apart by what they turn into verbs, not by who may use the pattern. Mami, the other whole-clause switcher, runs the other way: a Hindi clause, then an English status. 'Ek kaam karo' to you, Tinku and Mami; 'Ek kaam kijiye' to vendors and elders. Everyday Urdu-rooted words are his too, as they are the whole town's: 'zaroor aaiye', 'fikr mat kijiye', 'kasoor', 'ain waqt'. Figures in Hinglish bubbles are digits ('60? 70 kar do!'), because romanised 'saath' is also 'with'. He cheers good news by making it bigger, not with 'Badhai ho' or 'Congratulations', which go to Mummy and Mami if the owner allows them, so he does not echo them in a shared panel. HINDI MODE: everyday spoken city Hindi in Devanagari that keeps the English loan words a Hindi-first reader already knows (नो प्रॉब्लम, सेट, एक्स्ट्रा, प्रमोशन, बीपी, टेंट, लिस्ट, वेटिंग, डिपार्टमेंट, क्रेडिट), so he still sorts in Hindi; where his Hinglish has a whole English phrase, Hindi mode uses the Hindi a Devanagari speaker would say ('बहुत गर्व है', 'सबकी याद आती है'), never English spelled out in Devanagari; 'चिंता मत करो', 'चिंता नहीं' or 'फ़िक्र मत कीजिए' for 'don't worry'; with Nani, whole Hindi sentences and आप. ENGLISH MODE: plain British English with his rhythm; 'oh, and' for 'aur haan'; 'Do one thing' at most once a chapter (his one Indian-English turn), otherwise 'One small job'; 'Oho!' stays; kin words stay as they are (Didi, Jija ji, Amma, Boss); never a spelled accent and never a word-for-word line ('everyone must come', not 'everyone comes').

**Habits**

'Aur haan' (English mode 'oh, and') carries the run into one more bubble at most once a scene; every bubble ends on a full stop, '?' or '!', never on a trailing comma.
- Stacks worry questions about named people and waits only for the last answer: 'Amma ka BP check hua? Dawai? Koi saath hai? Pakka?'. He never answers his own question (Mausi's shape) and never asks how you are (Nani's 'Aur tum.' closes that in the album). Each person in the stack gets a verb or a noun with them ('Amma ka BP check hua?', 'Amma andar hain?'), never a bare kin word. To you, Mummy is 'Tumhari Mummy' ('Tumhari Mummy kaisi hain?'), because 'Didi, kaisi hain?' said aloud, or with a comma, is him calling you Didi, and कैसी then gives you a gender.
- Ends a worry stack, or a reassurance he is given, with 'Pakka?' ('Sure?'); at 5.27 it turns round into a statement. Never after a report of harassment or a dowry demand (rule 22).
- People first, then business: every vendor gets 'Ghar mein sab theek?' and a question about their own people (a mother's knee, the children's exams) before 'Achha.' and the job. Names, not numbers, are his stock, and he keeps it even when cross ('Aa gaye, bhai? Ghar mein sab theek? Achha. Tent. Abhi.').
- Makes everything bigger in words, never in figures: aur, sab, sabko, extra, poora, 'phir aur upar, phir sabse upar'. 'Full' ('full fit') in at most two lines a chapter; the rest of the time 'poora'. The plus-ten is a gesture, the flashed open hand, with no figure said; the only spoken figures in his year are 5.03 and 5.27.
- Hands the how to someone else in the same breath: 'Bade Jija ji ko bulao.', 'Credit Jija ji ka.'. He plans nothing; he knows who can.

## Typing

- Device: a big plain black phone in a thick black rubber case with one corner cracked on a site, painted with the screen dark and no logo, every word live text laid on top (rule 30). In chapter 5 it stays in his trouser pocket: never painted, never typed on screen.
- Script: English and Roman Hinglish, never Devanagari (Nani's voice-typed Devanagari is the contrast). In the game's Hindi mode his messages show in Devanagari and keep his bursts and marks; where his Hinglish types a whole English phrase ('so proud', 'miss you all'), Hindi mode shows the Hindi a Devanagari typist would use ('बहुत गर्व है', 'सबकी याद आती है'), and single loans he would say anyway stay (बीपी, टेंट, वेटिंग, प्लीज़).
- Shape: the run-on typed as a burst. Four or five short messages, each one clause of it, 'aur haan' a message of its own at most once a burst, then 'calling' before you can reply.
- The first message is the person's name, capitalised, with his doubled mark ('Didi!!', 'Jija ji!!', 'In-charge!!', '[Name] bhai!!'); everything after it is lowercase, kin names included ('amma', 'nani', 'chhote'), with no full stops. To Mami in private the name comes with no mark at all ('Boss').
- Marks: '!!' is his; a worry question takes one '?', because '??' is Mausi's; never '!!!'.
- Numbers: his bigness is typed in words (sab, everyone, extra), never as figures and never as the plus-ten. A digit appears only when a fact someone must act on cannot be said another way, and then he would rather ring.
- Emoji: 🎉 for good news, ‼️ for worry, and 🤗 (a face with no person, gender or skin tone) for love typed in words ('miss you all 🤗'): he is the only one in the family who types love. Never a person emoji; no folded hands until the owner rules (realism notes).
- Capitals once a year, for joy (Tinku's course letter), never in anger: all caps reads as shouting, the 'aggro uncle' trap (realism notes).
- Voice notes from the back of an auto between sites, starting mid-word because he presses record late; shown as a voice-note strip with their words as live text, and they run on like his speech ('...aur haan, ek kaam aur,').
- Forwards: only weather warnings for Nani's district, each with one line of his own ('amma ko andar rakhiye na!!'), the warning painted blank. Never politics, religion, gods, famous faces or false news (rule 3; caricature traps).
- When: just before his eight o'clock call to Nani, at red lights, and late at night after Mami's list. He replies in seconds or not at all, because he rings instead, and never reads a long message to the end ('ok ok call me').
- Short forms: 'pls', 'ok ok', 'msg'. English verbs with kar come into his typing as into his talk ('book kar dijiye pls'); 'ok ok call me' stays as it is, because the readers marked it true. No spelled accent and no typo played for laughs.
- Thanks and sorry are typed plainly too ('thank you', 'sorry didi'), never as a code.
- In private with Mami, and only there, his asks come down, in words: 'sabko?' becomes 'ok sab nahi' becomes 'tumhari list'.
- The game has no group chat (owner, 23 Oct). On screen he is single messages on a painted blank phone or a message card under a panel, and a message on the player's own phone waits on rulebook Q9.

## Posture

**Stance.** Weight forward on the balls of his feet, as if about to walk off. Chest out, shoulders a little rounded from years of leaning into phone calls. Right elbow up (the phone, or in December the beckon); the umbrella hanging from the other forearm; feet apart, toes out. He is never still except twice in the year: bowed under Nani's palm at her gate, and on the gate step at midnight beside Mausa ji.

**Sitting.** He barely sits. When he does, he sits on a plastic chair turned half round, one arm hooked over its back, his body turned towards the gate as if someone is about to arrive, one knee bouncing; the umbrella hangs from the forearm on the chair-back. (The draft's 'front edge, never leaning back' is struck: eight files gave it.) Never with the umbrella planted upright between his knees: that is Nani's cane at her side, and the two must not echo.

**Walk.** Fast, short, heavy steps, talking as he walks and walking as he talks. He turns in a tight circle when he worries, and walks out of a panel and back in. In a lane he walks down the middle of it, beckoning to both sides.

**Gestures**

- Cross: one downward chop of the free hand on each single word, walking straight at the problem.

**Expressions**

- NEUTRAL: mid-stride, brows level, mouth moving, eyes already on the next thing.
- PLEASED: arms thrown wide, the umbrella swinging, a grin under the moustache that crinkles his eyes shut.

- CROSS: he stops circling and walks straight at the problem, chin forward, the free hand chopping once on each single word; never a raised voice, never at family, a woman or anyone on a card. Mami goes still and slow when cross, so in their shared chapter the two tells never match.
