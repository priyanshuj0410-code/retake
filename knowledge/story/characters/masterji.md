# Masterji (मास्टरजी), about 60, the tailor next door; chapter 3's voice

**Role.** Chapter 3's voice (the July flood week, painted in the Station look). Not family: the tailor who has shared Mummy's shop wall on the station road since 1999. Mummy asks the chapter's eight decisions; Masterji is the chapter's second judging voice (a woman and a man, rule 22), opens the chapter from his own flooded doorway and speaks its fixed ending, which hands the cracked drain to chapter 4's lane cup. Never on a card, never the asker. He speaks in chapter 3 only while rulebook question 11 is open; he may stand silent in another chapter's panel, painted small and face-on from his own model sheet (owner answer 6 settles the bible's older 'seen from behind'). **Essence.** The tailor next door who measures everything, cloth, mud, a neighbour's patience, the weather, and has an opinion on every finger's width of it; exact to a hair by feel, since his tape lost its marks long ago, and never once on time. The type in five words: the station road's slow measurer. The player who says 'I know this person' is Sunita, herself a small-town tailor (personas P3), so he is skilled, dignified and never the joke; Ravi, who runs his family's shop, knows the neighbour who shares his wall. What lifts him above the stock slow tailor is pictured, not only told: the new ready-made shop's bright shutter sits small at the far end of his road while he talks about mud.

## Inner life

Born in the junction town about 1966. At fourteen (1980) he was apprenticed to the station road's old tailor, who gave him the cloth measuring tape he still wears round his neck; its marks had worn off by the mid-1990s and he has measured by feel ever since, in finger widths and hand spans, which is why the tape is plain and why he never speaks in inches. He opened his own one-room shop on the station road in 1988 and cut himself a black jacket and a matching sleeveless waistcoat for the opening. The jacket hangs on the shop's back wall; the waistcoat, in a thin cotton blend, he has worn every working day since, relined twice, and it still fits 'because I measured right'. In 1999 the empty room next door became Mummy's electrical shop: one wall, one concrete step and every monsoon shared since. He married in 1990; his wife does the hand-finishing at home (buttons, buttonholes, hand hems) and keeps his measurement register. Their son, about 32, cuts shirts in a city garment factory, one pattern four hundred times a day, which Masterji calls 'not tailoring' and is quietly proud of. He lives in the lane behind the station road on the far side of the drain from the family's lane, which is why the drain is his thirty-year argument with Captain sahab. He works at a plain black treadle machine he fitted with a small motor, owns no wristwatch, tells the time by the trains, opens at ten, and dates everything by what was on his machine that year ('the year of Bhai sahab's wedding jacket'), never by a number. In January a ready-made clothes shop opened at the far end of the station road, with a bright new shutter; he gave it one monsoon, and in July it is still there.

WANTS THIS YEAR: the drain behind the station road mended before another monsoon ruins a bolt of cloth (in July the flood soaks two bolts on his bottom shelf). WANTS FROM YOU: a listener who does not walk off; he has forty-six years of stories and the station road has stopped stopping. FEARS: that the station road forgets him: the new ready-made shop with its bright shutter, a son who chose the factory, and a drain that floods his cloth every July. The fear is in the frame, not only in the backstory: the new shop's shutter sits small at the far end of the road in 3.02 while he talks about mud. CONTRADICTION ('X, but Y'): exact to a hair's breadth, but never on time for anything, his own shutter included. It shows the same way every time: every verdict is a measure by feel ('a finger's width out', 'a hair uneven') and every time is 'bas abhi' (right away), which means an hour; he opens at ten, calls Mummy's nine 'showing off', and blames the trains. Under it: he measures twice and talks three times, so the clock always loses. WHAT HE GETS RIGHT ON SCREEN (the plot uses it, so the men's competence is seen, not only the women's): his forecasts from the feel of cloth are right, and Mummy dries her stock by them (proposed 3.11 and 3.21); his reading of the crack at 3.28 is the one both lanes mend by in chapter 4. THE TURN: on the Saturday after the flood his shutter goes up when hers does, the one time in 27 years, and nobody says a word about it. In December he has Mama ji's wedding kurta ready three days early, the first deadline he has met in thirty years.

## Relationships

- NANI (नानी). History: in 1999, when Mummy's shop opened beside his, Nani came from her town to inspect the street and haggled with him for an hour over the price of one hem; he lost, and has respected her since. Friction: she still says his hems are a hair uneven, and after 44 years behind a grocery counter she may be right, so he re-measures every hem she touches. Tenderness: he edges her shawls free when she visits and asks after her by name every week; when the family comes back in May his first words to Mummy are 'Nani ji theek hain?' He calls her 'Nani ji' (the lane uses the children's word) and says आप; she calls him 'Master', one of only two people who drop the ji.
- MUMMY (मम्मी). History: 27 years sharing one wall, one step and every monsoon. Friction: his ten against her nine ('Showing off, Bhabhi ji'); his bolts of cloth lean on the shared wall and she says the damp comes through to her fan boxes. Tenderness: every monsoon they lift each other's shutters; in May, while the family is at Nani's, he lifts her shutter a foot every morning to air the stock and says nothing, and she knows only from the chalk stroke he leaves on it each day (a story fact, never painted: his one object stand-in is the tape). In the flood week his own bottom shelf is under water and he still stands in her doorway first. He calls her 'Bhabhi ji' and says आप; she calls him 'Masterji' and says आप (equal respect between neighbours). They are chapter 3's two judging voices, and their tells never match: she gets more polite when cross, he drops his wander.
- PAPA (पापा). History: he stitched Papa's one good jacket, the charcoal stand-collar one, for the 1997 wedding, and lets it out a little every few years without a word; to him that wedding is 'the year of Bhai sahab's jacket'. Friction: Papa arrives at every fitting to the minute, which Masterji finds suspicious ('A man who comes on time wants something'), and he says Papa stands like a timetable and spoils the jacket's line. Tenderness: they share the station road's mornings from either side of the platform, Papa's parcel office and his shutter, and Papa is the one man who waits for him without complaint. He calls him 'Bhai sahab', आप; Papa says 'Masterji', आप.
- MAMA JI (मामा जी). History: in the summer of 1999 he measured twenty-year-old Mama ji for his first interview shirt and stitched it free 'for luck'; Mama ji got the job and still says it was the shirt. Friction: now Mama ji orders from the city by phone, sends photographs of cloth, wants it 'by Friday' and haggles like a city man; Masterji says city cloth photographs better than it wears. Tenderness: he still keeps the 1999 slip in his register, and he cuts the December wedding kurta himself, early. He calls him 'Mama ji' (the lane's word) and says आप, now that Mama ji is a grown man and a customer (a ji with तुम read as a mismatch); Mama ji says 'Masterji', आप. The readers' one address table confirms it.
- TINKU (टिंकू). History: he stitched her first school uniform. Friction: he says she never stands still long enough to be measured; she scores his lateness out loud on Saturdays ('10:40, Masterji. Fourth late this month.'). Tenderness: she brings his eleven o'clock tea from Mummy's counter every Saturday, and he is the one grown-up who asks to see her scorebook and checks that her columns are straight: 'Barabar.' He calls her 'Tinku', तुम; she says 'Masterji', आप.
- MAUSI (मौसी). History: his customer for thirty years. Friction: she complains about every fitting, talks through every measurement so that he loses count ('Mausi ji, the measure goes when you talk'), and comes back every time. Tenderness: he is the one person who lets her finish a sentence, because he talks slower than she does. He calls her 'Mausi ji' (the lane's word), आप; she says 'Masterji', आप. They never share a chapter.
- MAUSA JI (मौसा जी). History: the lineman rewired Masterji's shop for nothing in 2001; Masterji has stitched his shirts at cost ever since, and neither has mentioned it once. Friction: Mausa ji's shirts come back scorched from live wires, which Masterji takes personally. Tenderness: the slow talker and the man of two words sit on the shop step together, one talking, one saying 'Hmm', both content. 'Mausa ji' and आप both ways.
- CAPTAIN SAHAB (कप्तान साहब). History: the two old men of the drain. They live on the two lanes the drain divides (proposed: Masterji on the far lane, Captain sahab on the family's), and since 1994 have argued every monsoon about whose lane's drain floods the station road. Friction: thirty monsoons of 'that drain sits low'. Tenderness: neither has ever missed the other's monsoon complaint, and 'Let the lane cup decide' hands thirty years of argument to a cricket match, as close as either will come to giving in. He calls him 'Captain', dropping the sahab, and says आप; Captain sahab calls him 'Master', आप. The clipped titles are thirty years of familiarity, not rudeness.
- MAMI (मामी). History: they have never met. She sends Mama ji's measurements by message, numbered, with inch marks, and he says she is the only customer in thirty years who measures correctly (he reads inches on a slip; he never says them). Friction: she asks for a delivery date and he gives one, which for him is a risk. Tenderness: two precise people; her numbered list is why the wedding kurta is ready three days early. He calls her 'Mami ji', आप.
- YOU (the player). History: he has known you since you could walk; he mended your school bag strap for nothing and still asks after it. ## Voice

**Register.** Slow, warm and chatty: the most digressive speaker in the cast but never the loudest (Mausi is louder, Mama ji faster, Captain sahab more clipped, Papa more exact about clocks). SENTENCE SHAPE (his alone): a verdict, then a wander; he starts on the matter and drifts to cloth or weather. Inside the bubble cap that is one or two short sentences of 4 to 9 words, often a rhetorical question; when he has two bubbles, the second wanders, and his beats are budgeted as pairs so the wander is never the line that gets cut. NO FIGURES IN HIS SPEECH: chapter 3's numeric voice is Mummy (times and prices); he measures by feel, in finger widths, hair's breadths and hand spans, never in inches, minutes or years (rule 2: no year on screen). Full stops, not exclamations. HOW HE ARGUES: with what was on his machine that year ('The year of Bhai sahab's jacket, I said the same'). HOW HE PRAISES: one word, 'Barabar' (level), and a tug of his tape ends. HOW HE SAYS SORRY: he never says it; he re-measures: 'Chalo, phir se naap lete hain.' | 'चलो, फिर से नाप लेते हैं।' | 'Come, we measure again.' HOW HE IS CROSS (rhythm and body, not volume): he never raises his voice; the wander stops, one short verdict and then silence, and he straightens out of his stoop; always at a thing (the drain, the sky, cheap cloth), never at a person. Mummy, his chapter's other voice, gets more polite when cross, so the two tells never match. HUMOUR: dry, aimed at the road, the weather or himself, never at a person's body or a woman's clothes. He is a voice, not the asker, so he may carry the chapter's light touch (rule 37 binds only the asker), but he is never the butt of a joke.

**Hindi and English.** Hindi frame with tailoring and shop English used as Hindi nouns: fitting, trial, alter, size, finish, pattern, ready-made (said with a sniff), shutter. His measures are the old body ones, said without figures: ungli bhar (a finger's width), baal bhar (a hair's breadth), baalisht (a hand span). He reads inches on a customer's slip but never says them. No whole English clauses (the opposite of Mama ji and Mami). In Hinglish mode his lines are Roman Hindi with those nouns, and any number in a bubble is written as a figure ('2 galiyon'), since 'do' is also a word; in English mode his habits carry over as 'level', 'a finger out', 'right away'. No accent is ever spelled out (the Apu trap in the realism notes).

**Habits**

- Opens with 'Dekhiye', to everyone, Tinku and you included: a counter habit, not a respect form. 'Dekho' is not his (Nani's file drops देखो, so the opener is his alone).
- Gives every verdict as a measure by feel, never a figure: 'a finger's width out', 'a hair uneven', 'a hand span short', 'crooked'.
- His second sentence wanders to cloth or weather, so a reader can spot him from the drift alone.
- Dates things by what was on his machine: 'the year of Bhai sahab's jacket', 'the summer of Mama ji's interview shirt'. Never a year (rule 2), never a count of years.
- Gives every time as 'bas abhi' (right away) and never a clock time (Papa's and Mummy's ground).
- Reads the weather from the feel of cloth: limp cotton means rain, stiff cotton means sun; he is right, and the chapter uses it.
- Tailoring metaphors for everything: a road has seams, a plan has a loose stitch, a family has a hem.
- Holds up his right thumb and forefinger a finger's width apart while he talks.
- 'Uff' (उफ़्फ़) is his one exclamation, for the drain, the sky and cheap cloth; nobody else in the cast uses it, and it fills the place where an elder would reach for a god-word (rule 3).
- When cross, the wander stops: one short verdict, silence, and he straightens out of the stoop.

**Catchphrases**

- 'Ungli bhar ka farq.' | 'उँगली भर का फ़र्क़।' | 'A finger's width out.' His verdict on anything: a hem, a drain, a plan. A measure by feel, never a figure, because his tape lost its marks.
- 'Barabar.' | 'बराबर।' | 'Level.' His only praise. In Hindi it also means 'equal', a quiet echo in a fairness game that nobody ever points out, because nobody preaches.

**Never says**

- Any word on a woman's dress, its length, tightness or modesty: no छोटा/तंग said of clothes on a woman, no शर्म, इज़्ज़त, 'decent', 'modern'.
- Any remark on a body's size or weight: no मोटा, पतला, 'too thin', 'put on weight'. He measures bodies and never comments on them.
- 'Bossy', 'argues', रौब, बहस: the words the continuity read found landing only on women.
- A figure as a verdict or a time: no inches, no minutes, no 'sharp', no 'ठीक 9 बजे' (Mummy is chapter 3's numeric voice; the minutes are Papa's).
- A year, or a count of years offered as proof, in a bubble (rule 2); he dates things by what was on his machine.
- 'About', 'roughly', लगभग, for a measurement.
- 'Sorry' (he re-measures instead) and 'I'm late' (the trains were late).
- 'Pakka' (Mama ji's and four others'), 'Dekho' (Nani's) and 'Suno' (Mausi's).
- A proverb: proverbs are Nani's register now (the India review); his sayings are his own measures.
- 'Ready-made' as praise.
- Beta, beti, bhaiya, didi or any gendered word for the player; any future that changes with gender in a line to the player.
- A card person's name, or any word from a note on that decision's cards, in a bubble (rule 36).
- Any doubt of a report of harassment or bullying, or of a dowry demand (rule 22).
- Any religious greeting, blessing, god-word or festival word (rule 3); 'Uff' does that work.

## In the story

**Best scenes**

- 3.02, his own flooded doorway, sceptical, hands behind his back, trousers turned up level, beside Mummy's back at her half-raised shutter, and far down the road, small, the new ready-made shop's bright plain shutter with no sign: 'All this mud, by Saturday?' then 'And the sky's still sulking.'
