# You (the player, a voice only)

**Role.** The protagonist of Saal Bhar, Retake's story mode: the grown-up child of the house, aged 22 to 30, whom the whole family phones. A voice only: never painted, never named, no gender, caste or religion (rules 7 and 8; owner answer 2 of 25 Oct). Never an asker, never on a card, never a judging voice; the one each chapter's asker hands the work to. Label 'You' | 'आप'; the screen reader hears 'You:' | 'आप:' (bubble spec). Round in the story's sense (your year changes, and it pays off with you), but open by design: rich in relationships and nearly empty of self-description, and with no clock of your own (when you sleep, when you reply, where you are at nine), so any of the six players can stand in your place, Shruti on her 9 am bus and Farida on night shifts included (the India review). Who would say 'I know this person': all six (the review: being the grown-up child everyone phones works for every player, the 61-year-old included). **Essence.** The family's switchboard. Every call comes to you, and you answer each person in their own language: railway words to Papa, shop words to Mummy, cricket words to Tinku, 'sorted' to Mama ji, plain Hindi to Nani. You ask to see before anything, you put someone else first, and you close each job with one word. You are on everyone's list as the one who does it, and nobody asks how you are, until Nani counts heads in the album and counts you last.

## Inner life

You grew up in the household's home in the junction town, in a lane off the station road, where Mummy's shutter and Papa's parcel office face each other across one platform. As a child you spent summers in Nani's front-room grocery in her own town, reading her bills and letters aloud, which is how you became her plain-truth person. At eight Mummy taught you to count change at her counter, and you are still the only one besides her who counts the drawer at closing (Mummy's file). On every trip Papa let you hold the tickets, and you never lost one (Papa's file). You did your Class 1 to 5 sums in Mausi's front-room tuition class, and she still calls you her pupil (Mausi's file). Masterji mended your school bag strap for nothing and still asks after it (Masterji's file). Captain sahab taught you, like every child in the lane, to hold the bat straight; you were the child who swung across the line, the one shot he says he never cured, and he still says 'Front foot.' when you pass (Captain sahab's file). Somewhere along the way, because you always picked up, you became the number the family rings first: Mama ji rings you, not his sister; Mausi rings you for the train times; and Tinku keeps a secret tally of how many times the family called you this year (Tinku's file). What fills your working day, where you studied, whether you sleep under the household's roof every night of the year, when you go to bed and when you answer, what you earn and whom you love are never said on screen or in any line, so each player brings their own. When a chapter needs you home for days, one plain line brings you ('Two days off. Saturday's shutter.'), never a job title. Nobody in the story asks when you will marry, comments on your looks, or tells you what to wear.

WANTS THIS YEAR (the story's want for you; what the player wants for themselves stays their own): everyone to Nani's eightieth in May and through the year, with nobody left off a list. WHAT EACH OF THEM WANTS FROM YOU (the method notes: the protagonist is never drawn, so each person is partly defined by what they want from you): Nani, the plain truth and someone to hold her work when she cannot; Mummy, half the phone calls, asked for with a pen laid down and never in words; Papa, to be the one who never needs reminding; Mama ji, to hear 'Sorted, Mama ji'; Tinku, to be treated as an equal; Mausi, your news first-hand, not through Mummy; Mausa ji, that you sleep while he sits up; Masterji, a listener who does not walk off; Captain sahab, a straight bat and a fair pick; Mami, a second pair of hands she need not explain things to twice. WANTS UNDERNEATH (never said; shown once, in the album): to be counted, once, as one of 'everyone' rather than as the one doing the counting. FEARS: the one person left off the list. Every decision in the year leaves someone out, so this fear is the year's own question, and the game never answers it for you. CONTRADICTION: everyone calls you, but nobody asks how you are, or waits for the answer when they do. This is something the family does to you, not a habit of yours: you do not dodge the question (that was the draft's tic, and it doubled Tinku's, who answers questions about herself with somebody else's score; comics review), so the stand-in stays open for each player. It shows the same way every time: every call to you is about someone else. Mama ji's worry list names everyone and never you, Mummy assigns, Papa checks, Mami puts a tick beside your line, and Mausi, the one who does ask, asks three things at once and talks over your one-word answer. No line of yours says how you are, because nobody stops to hear it. It turns once, in the album, when Nani counts heads round the table and counts you last ('Aur tum.' | 'और तुम।' | 'And you.'), the first time anyone asks and waits, and you say the only line of the year about yourself ('Nani, main bhi.', a proposal).

## Voice

**Register.** Short, calm and level: the steadiest voice in a loud house, and the one that puts the listener's name on every line, which is how a young person in an Indian home talks to elders (the readers marked it as ringing true). THE SHAPE: one or two short beats (a polite request, an offer, a short closed question, or 'Pehle ___'), with the listener's name on them. In Hinglish and Hindi the name opens the bubble, after it a comma ('Papa, zara dikhaiye.' | 'पापा, ज़रा दिखाइए।'); in English it closes the bubble ('Let me see, Papa.'). Only a small word comes before the name: your exclamation ('Badhiya. Papa, jaldi aaiye.'), a common filler ('Achha, Mummy.', 'Theek hai, Papa.'), or a set English phrase that keeps its own order in every mode ('Thank you, Mausa ji.', 'Good morning, Papa.'). Two to six words a bubble as a rule, eight at most, always inside rule 62's ten; a bigger job never makes a longer bubble, it waits for the next exchange. Your lines carry their verbs: requests end on -iye, offers ask in the subjunctive ('Torch main laaun?'), and comfort lines keep their 'hai' or 'hain' ('Nani, sab yahin hain.'); only questions of fact and the closing 'Sab set.' stay bare. Full stops, not exclamation marks: 'Badhiya.' is said level, and you raise your voice once in the year, at the noise in December ('Ek-ek karke!'). Questions are short and closed and ask for facts, never feelings ('Nani, dard?'). You speak last in an exchange, never open a scene, never speak between a decision's question and its pick, and sit in an outcome beat only as its fixed reply (rules 38 and 42). HOW THIS SHORT VOICE DIFFERS FROM THE OTHER SHORT ONES: Nani's closed fragments carry no name and send 'koi' to do the job; Mausa ji drops the subject and does the job himself; Captain sahab gives the call, then one reason; Tinku ends on a score and 'haan?'; Mami reads your words back and asks 'Correct?'. You name the listener, ask to see, offer rather than announce, and put someone else first. WHEN CROSS: you stay polite (your politeness never rises or falls, unlike Mummy's), but the name stands alone at the front, closed with a full stop, and 'Pehle' names whoever was left waiting or left off ('Mama ji. Pehle Nani se miliye.' | 'Mama ji. Go and see Nani first.'). Your anger is on someone else's behalf, never at a person. BUDGET (proposal, kept from the cast file): three to six `you` bubbles a chapter, at most one a scene, the same whatever the player will pick (rule 38).

**Hindi and English.** You mirror the listener, because being understood by each of them is your job, and that mirror is where most of your words come from. With Nani: plain everyday Hindi with Hindi verbs ('phone kijiye', not 'call kijiye'), and no English beyond her own nouns (doctor, BP, ward, ticket, cake, torch). With Mummy: her shop nouns (shutter, stock, bill, slip) on a Hindi frame, but never her counter verbs for home things ('MCB check karo', 'mains off karo' are hers). With Papa: careful Hindi with his railway English (PNR, coach, berth, confirm) and his 'Reached', which stays in English letters in every mode. With Mama ji: the most English you speak to an elder, and his own words handed back ('sorted', 'Don't worry'). With Tinku: young Hinglish, तुम and -ओ commands, a 'yaar' now and then, her name 'Tinku' (and 'scorer' at most once a chapter, at the moment the scorebook matters), and her scorebook noun 'score'. With Mausi, Mausa ji, Masterji, Captain sahab and Mami: polite Hinglish with -इए, using their own nouns only when you hand one back. YOUR OWN ENGLISH, when nobody sets it: the verbs of the phone and the booking, said with kar or ho the way nearly everyone under fifty says them ('call kijiye', 'video call kar lijiye', 'ticket book kar doon?', 'confirm kar diya', 'score update karo', 'shutter down kar doon?'), always in a shape that marks no gender (a polite command, an offer in the subjunctive, a ने past that agrees with the thing). Mummy turns home things into counter English; you turn family business into phone English, because you are the family's switchboard. YOUR OWN HINDI: everyday Hindustani, with the town's common words ('fikr mat kijiye', 'zaroor', 'waqt') and the fillers everybody says ('achha', 'theek hai', 'arre', 'haan', 'zara', 'bas' inside a line), bent back towards Hindi by a small-town home: the shape of the younger speakers in the Dehradun study (realism notes [1]). In Devanagari 'sorted' and 'set' are both 'सेट', never तय, which means 'decided'. HINDI MODE: lines are rebuilt from shapes that mark no gender (rule 66), never translated word for word, and carry only the English a Hindi-first home already says in Hindi (सॉरी, थैंक यू, सेट, स्टॉक, शटर, टिकट, बर्थ, बुक); a phone verb becomes फ़ोन करो and a congratulation becomes बहुत बढ़िया. Numbers: none in speech in any mode. In a message, digits, and only when the fact is a number (ward 3, bed 12, berth 36, 2 days off).

**Habits**

- The name on every line. Hinglish and Hindi: the name first, then a comma ('Nani, sorry.' | 'नानी, सॉरी।'). English: the name last ('Sorry, Nani.'). A set English phrase keeps its own order in every mode ('Thank you, Mausa ji.', 'Good morning, Papa.'). Name first in Hindi also keeps rule 66's third check quiet, since नानी, मौसी, मामी and मौसा जी end in -ी. The only lines without a name are to someone the scene leaves unnamed (a child at the table, a cousin on the phone) and to a group, which you call 'sab' | 'सब' | 'everyone'.
- You ask to see before anything, with the 'zara' everyone puts on a polite ask: 'Zara dikhaiye.' In every chapter your first line at the hand-over is 'Let me see' with the giver's name.
