# You (the player, a voice only)

**Role.** The protagonist of Saal Bhar, Retake's story mode: the grown-up child of the house, aged 22 to 30, whom the whole family phones. A voice only: never painted, never named, no gender, caste or religion (rules 7 and 8; owner answer 2 of 25 Oct). Never an asker, never on a card, never a judging voice; the one each chapter's asker hands the work to. Label 'You' | 'आप'; the screen reader hears 'You:' | 'आप:' (bubble spec). Round in the story's sense (your year changes, and it pays off with you), but open by design: rich in relationships and nearly empty of self-description, and with no clock of your own (when you sleep, when you reply, where you are at nine), so any of the six players can stand in your place, Shruti on her 9 am bus and Farida on night shifts included (the India review). Who would say 'I know this person': all six (the review: being the grown-up child everyone phones works for every player, the 61-year-old included). **Essence.** The family's switchboard. Every call comes to you, and you answer each person in their own language: railway words to Papa, shop words to Mummy, cricket words to Tinku, 'sorted' to Mama ji, plain Hindi to Nani. You ask to see before anything, you put someone else first, and you close each job with one word. You are on everyone's list as the one who does it, and nobody asks how you are, until Nani counts heads in the album and counts you last.

## Inner life

You grew up in the household's home in the junction town, in a lane off the station road, where Mummy's shutter and Papa's parcel office face each other across one platform. As a child you spent summers in Nani's front-room grocery in her own town, reading her bills and letters aloud, which is how you became her plain-truth person. At eight Mummy taught you to count change at her counter, and you are still the only one besides her who counts the drawer at closing (Mummy's file). On every trip Papa let you hold the tickets, and you never lost one (Papa's file). You did your Class 1 to 5 sums in Mausi's front-room tuition class, and she still calls you her pupil (Mausi's file). Masterji mended your school bag strap for nothing and still asks after it (Masterji's file). Captain sahab taught you, like every child in the lane, to hold the bat straight; you were the child who swung across the line, the one shot he says he never cured, and he still says 'Front foot.' when you pass (Captain sahab's file). Somewhere along the way, because you always picked up, you became the number the family rings first: Mama ji rings you, not his sister; Mausi rings you for the train times; and Tinku keeps a secret tally of how many times the family called you this year (Tinku's file). What fills your working day, where you studied, whether you sleep under the household's roof every night of the year, when you go to bed and when you answer, what you earn and whom you love are never said on screen or in any line, so each player brings their own. When a chapter needs you home for days, one plain line brings you ('Two days off. Saturday's shutter.'), never a job title. Nobody in the story asks when you will marry, comments on your looks, or tells you what to wear.

WANTS THIS YEAR (the story's want for you; what the player wants for themselves stays their own): everyone to Nani's eightieth in May and through the year, with nobody left off a list. WHAT EACH OF THEM WANTS FROM YOU (the method notes: the protagonist is never drawn, so each person is partly defined by what they want from you): Nani, the plain truth and someone to hold her work when she cannot; Mummy, half the phone calls, asked for with a pen laid down and never in words; Papa, to be the one who never needs reminding; Mama ji, to hear 'Sorted, Mama ji'; Tinku, to be treated as an equal; Mausi, your news first-hand, not through Mummy; Mausa ji, that you sleep while he sits up; Masterji, a listener who does not walk off; Captain sahab, a straight bat and a fair pick; Mami, a second pair of hands she need not explain things to twice. WANTS UNDERNEATH (never said; shown once, in the album): to be counted, once, as one of 'everyone' rather than as the one doing the counting. FEARS: the one person left off the list. Every decision in the year leaves someone out, so this fear is the year's own question, and the game never answers it for you. CONTRADICTION: everyone calls you, but nobody asks how you are, or waits for the answer when they do. This is something the family does to you, not a habit of yours: you do not dodge the question (that was the draft's tic, and it doubled Tinku's, who answers questions about herself with somebody else's score; comics review), so the stand-in stays open for each player. It shows the same way every time: every call to you is about someone else. Mama ji's worry list names everyone and never you, Mummy assigns, Papa checks, Mami puts a tick beside your line, and Mausi, the one who does ask, asks three things at once and talks over your one-word answer. No line of yours says how you are, because nobody stops to hear it. It turns once, in the album, when Nani counts heads round the table and counts you last ('Aur tum.' | 'और तुम।' | 'And you.'), the first time anyone asks and waits, and you say the only line of the year about yourself ('Nani, main bhi.', a proposal).

## Voice

**Register.** Short, practical and calm: two to five words a line, rarely seven, always within the 10-word cap on a spoken line (rule 62); the draft's player lines run three words at the median. Your sentence shape is your own (the comics review asks each voice for a shape as well as a vocabulary): one polite command or one bare noun, then the listener's name ('Let's see, Papa.', 'Cots, sorted.'), with the name first in Hindi. Papa keeps whole sentences with their verbs, Mummy lists, Mami numbers items, Tinku asks with 'haan?' and Captain sahab calls; you are the one who names the listener in every line. Few figures: the cast's numeric voices are Papa's minutes, Mummy's prices and Tinku's scores (India review), so your spoken lines carry no figure except a time or a ward number in a message. Polite to every elder with आप and -इए, Mummy and Papa alike. Unhurried when everyone else is loud: you are the steady line in a family whose loudest voice is Mausi and fastest is Mama ji. Dry rather than funny: when there is a joke it is a deadpan question in someone else's idiom, and it waits on rulebook Q5. Every line has a listener, named. You speak last in an exchange, never open a scene, never speak between a decision's question and its pick, and never sit in an outcome beat except as its fixed reply (rule 42). Proposed budget, now that the owner has lifted the time cap (answer 7 of 26 Oct) and the players' judge found you talk with the others only in the opening: three to six `you` bubbles a chapter instead of the bible's two or three, at most one a scene, always the same whatever the player will pick (rule 38).

**Hindi and English.** You mirror the listener, because being understood by each of them is your job. With Nani: plain everyday Hindi, no English beyond her own nouns (doctor, BP, ward, ticket, cake). With Mummy: Hinglish with her shop nouns (stock, bill, shutter) and English status words (done, sorted). With Papa: careful Hindi with his railway English (PNR, coach, berth, confirm). With Mama ji: whole English clauses, the most English you speak to an elder ('Mama ji, sorted. Don't worry.'). With Tinku: English-led with Hindi tags, तुम and -ओ commands. With the chapter voices: polite Hinglish with 'ji'. Your own base, when the listener sets nothing, is Hinglish on a Hindi frame with English nouns and English status words. That is the shape of the younger speakers in the Dehradun study, who built most clauses on English and switched whole clauses (realism notes [1]), bent back towards Hindi by a small-town home. English verbs do a job for you: 'Reached', 'Done', 'Booked', 'Sorted' carry no gender, which is why your messages lean on them (realism notes 3.2). In Devanagari 'sorted' is 'सेट', what a 25-year-old actually says (India review; Mama ji's file), never तय, which means 'decided'. In Hindi mode lines are rebuilt from shapes that mark no gender (rule 66), never translated word for word. Numbers are written as figures in every mode, so 'saath' in your Hinglish is only ever 'with', never sixty (India review).

**Habits**

- Every line names its listener: 'Let's see, Mami.', 'Tea first, Masterji.' In English and Hinglish the name comes last, after a comma; in Hindi it comes first ('मामी, दिखाइए।'), which is just as natural and keeps rule 66's third check quiet, since नानी, मौसी, मामी and मौसा जी all end in -ी. You never speak to the room, except 'everyone' | 'सब'.
- You ask to see before anything: 'Dikhaiye.' In every chapter your first line at the hand-over is 'Let's see' with the giver's name. To Tinku alone it is 'Dikhao', the one place your respect form drops, and readers hear the sibling.
- You close a job with the thing and one word: 'Cots, sorted.' | 'कॉट: सेट।' The same colon form runs your messages ('keys: sorted / band: kal').
- You put someone else first in the queue: 'Water first, everyone.', 'Tea first, Masterji.', 'Now, Nani's cake.' In Hindi this is पहले (before), never Mami's ordinal पहला, दूसरा: you name one thing first and never count on to a second. This is about order, never about dodging a question.
- When someone does ask about you, you answer in one plain word and let them move on: three questions from Mausi get one 'Haan ji, Mausi.' No line of yours says how you are, what you feel or what you want, because nobody waits to hear it (the contradiction).
- You hand people their own words back, never their figures: keeping time to Papa ('Even the battery kept time, Papa.'), her own praise to Tinku ('Not out, scorer.'), Nani's 'sab' to Nani ('Nani, everyone's close by.'). Only in story beats; never in an outcome beat, and never a judging note's words (rules 36, 38).
- No 'I', 'me', 'my' or 'please' in English and no मैं in Hindi, except in an offer in the subjunctive (मैं देखूँ?) and the album's one line, so nearly every line of yours can go on the button if the owner says yes to rulebook Q8.
- Your questions are short and closed and ask for facts, not feelings: 'Dard?', 'Time yaad hai?'
