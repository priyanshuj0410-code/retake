How to use this workbook. Who fills what, and the rules.

Solve's question sheet has Instructions and four pages, covered here in the same order, with one change: the sheet puts the three agreements at the end of the Solution Profile page, after question 13, and this workbook puts them first so the team lead reads them before anything is written. The live form was not viewed: check the order there when signed in. Every question is a card. The chip on each card says what it needs:

Readythe answer is settled: copy it in. Facts neededonly the team knows it: fill it in. Writea written answer, in your own words, from the material on the card. Team leadthe team lead does this personally.

Write in your own words

Solve says: "We do not recommend using generative AI tools to write your application for you", because such answers read as generic and "can strongly disadvantage your application." Use the material on each card as notes, and write the answer yourself. AI tools may then be used, as Solve suggests, to shorten a draft or check the grammar.

Women in India are about half as likely as men to be in the labour force (working or looking for work). One reason is the everyday decisions made about them at work: who is hired, sent, trusted and promoted. Retake turns those decisions into a game and shows each player, privately, where their choices leaned. Solve's support would take it into workplaces and test whether it works.

That is the one idea every answer comes back to. Keep the answers consistent with it.

Five claims we must not make

The form's Accuracy and Integrity Agreement says that exaggerating "may lead to the removal of my solution from the Challenge." So, in every answer:

  • No users yet. No workplace has used Retake, and no one outside the team has played it. The app collects nothing, so there is no usage count to quote.
  • No paying customers yet. Unless the team has a signed partner, the business model is a plan.
  • The reading is not validated. Today it counts picks. How many decisions a confident reading needs, and how they become one figure, is still being worked out, and three faults in how it counts are still open.
  • Retake shows choices inside the game. We do not claim it changes who gets hired or promoted. That is what a pilot would test.
  • Planned means planned. Only the First take is built. The daily puzzles, the streak, leaderboards and accounts are designed but not built: say "planned" each time. A workplace version and any summary for employers are not designed at all: call them ideas.

11What specific problem are you solving?Required200 wordsWrite
Solve asksThe specific problem within the Challenge; its scale where we work and globally; how many people it affects; what causes it.
Material
  • The gap. Solve's own Challenge page: "women remain 24% less likely than men to participate in the labor force." In India, 41.7% of women aged 15 and over are in the labour force, against 78.8% of men (Periodic Labour Force Survey 2023-24, MoSPI).
  • Part of it is decided inside workplaces. Who is hired, given the shift or the route, sent on the course, promoted, believed. When science faculty rated identical applications from "John" and "Jennifer", John was judged more competent and hireable and offered a higher salary (Moss-Racusin and colleagues, 2012).
  • The bias is common. UNDP's 2023 Gender Social Norms Index finds nearly 9 in 10 people worldwide hold at least one bias against women. Found in secondary reports: confirm in the UNDP report before citing.
  • Training rarely reaches the decision. Gender training at work is often a one-off session counted by attendance, so no one can see whether decisions get fairer. One-off diversity training has weak and short-lived effects (Dobbin and Kalev, 2016). Team: add an example from a company you know, if you have one.

12What is your solution?Required200 wordsWrite
Solve asksWhat it is, what it does, how it works and what technology it uses, in simple terms. About the solution, not the problem. A link to a video of a product demo, if relevant.
Material
  • A fairness puzzle game on the phone set in five illustrated Indian workplaces and homes: a railway junction, trucks on a ghat road, a travelling magic show, a cricket ground and a family home. The five chapters are set between 1971 and 1999, told as a grandmother's memories. What runs today is the First take: one sitting of five chapters and 40 decisions, ending in a private reading. Planned, not built: three daily puzzles, at Easy, Medium and Hard.
  • Each decision is a real kind of workplace or household call: who drives the ghat section tonight, who carries the cash to the next town, who captains the first XI, who gets the 2 seats on the new-engine course, who holds the keys to the cash box. It shows 3 to 5 people with their records.
  • Two of them have identical records, one woman and one man. One detail on their cards differs, such as "her family worries about her night runs". Later, a twin decision puts that detail on the man, with a different equal pair. The identical pair is there in all 40 decisions.
  • The reading comes from the pairs. The man both times is a lean towards men; the woman both times is a lean towards women; following the detail both times is no lean. In some twins the detail really matters, such as who holds the licence. Today the reading counts picks; how they become one figure is still being worked out.
  • What each player gets today: a private reading of five cards: how often they picked the woman or the man when the two were equal, one twin side by side, the part of life that leaned most, a tip, and a last card saying it is a first reading from one sitting. Hard-topic twins are not used for the twin and part-of-life cards. Planned, not built: daily practice on new cases, and leaderboards that reward fair play both ways.
  • What runs today, live at https://tel-retake.vercel.app: the First take, about 20 minutes by the title cards' estimate; the app does not time it. In Hinglish, Hindi (a draft) and English. It installs on a phone, works offline, asks for a birth date first and shuts out under-18s, and keeps everything on the phone. An idea for the workplace version, not yet designed: an anonymous summary for employers showing which kinds of decision lean. The product plan lists only a workplace kit, outside the app, for later; the employer summary is an idea built on it.

31What makes your solution innovative?300 wordsWrite
Solve asksHow it approaches the problem in a new or much improved way; how it differs from others; how it could change the field.
Material
  • It works on the decision, not on attitudes. Gender training at work is often a one-off session, and one-off diversity training has weak and short-lived effects (Dobbin and Kalev, 2016). Changing people's scores on implicit-bias tests does not reliably change what they do (Forscher and colleagues, 2019).
  • It reads decisions, not answers to a questionnaire, using the logic of the identical-CV studies, inside a game people choose to play. Whether the reading is a valid measure is still to be tested.
  • It reads a lean either way, towards men or towards women, and is built to separate being swayed by a word from being swayed by gender (not yet validated). No one is accused, and favouring women counts as much as favouring men. Players never see the words bias, test or gender.
  • Private by design: the game and its reading run on the phone, and nothing the player does is sent anywhere.
  • Planned: a daily habit, not a workshop. Three daily puzzles, at Easy, Medium and Hard, with leaderboards scored both ways. Not built yet: what runs today is the one-sitting First take.
  • Made for India, including frontline workers: set in Indian workplaces and homes, with Indian names and languages, from Hinglish to Hindi, on a phone, offline. The app's code is kept small for weak phones and connections: about 110 KB compressed, under a 130 KB budget; to work offline it saves about 3.8 MB, mostly pictures and fonts, on the first visit (not yet tried on a basic phone or a weak connection).
  • For the field, once a workplace version exists: a way for organisations to see whether their gender training changes decisions, which attendance counts cannot show. The employer summary this needs is an idea, not yet designed.
