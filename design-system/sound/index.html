Equal Lens Daily Sound

An ideation sheet for the game's music and sound effects. Things to hear and compare, not one answer. The owner decides.

Every sound here is made live in your browser. No audio files are downloaded. Sound starts only when you press a Play button.

Before you press Play

  • The first press wakes the sound. Browsers do not allow it earlier.
  • Start with the volume low, as set. If you hear nothing, check your phone's silent switch and media volume.
  • One music loop plays at a time. The sound effects in section 4 play over it, so you can hear them together.
  • Try a cheap phone speaker too. That is how many players will hear it.

The one rule that decides everything: sound never hints at a lean

No sound, motion or vibration may tell the player that a pick was good or bad, or which way it leaned. Every idea on this sheet was tested against that first.

  • No "right" or "wrong" sounds. No chime for one pick and a buzz for another.
  • The pick sound and the commit sound are identical whoever you pick.
  • The result sound never changes with the score or the lean. It takes no input at all.
  • So pitch, speed, length, loudness and tone colour are fixed for each event. We also leave out random variety, because a difference nobody planned can still read as a message.

The other binding rules

  • Sound is on by defaultYour decision. Music and sound effects are two separate switches, and both live in the "?" sheet, the one settings place.
  • One vibration pulseOne short pulse on the commit, and nowhere else. See how sound pairs with it in section 4.
  • No clocksNo ticking, no countdown, no tempo that speeds up. The game has no timers, so nothing may sound like one.
  • Nothing depends on soundIt is decoration and feedback. A player with sound off, or a phone on silent, loses nothing.
  • Reduced motion calms sound tooNo long flourishes. Section 4 says how.
  • Only after a tapBrowsers block audio until the player presses something. The game cannot start sound by itself.

1What we know

How daily puzzle games usually handle sound

  • The big daily word and number puzzles are close to silent. Wordle and NYT Games are the usual examples. The puzzle is the quiet part of the day.
  • Duolingo goes the other way. It has many short, rich effects, and a separate sound switch so people who dislike them can turn them off.
  • Many casual games on mobile web keep music off until the player asks. Browsers also block sound until the first tap, so no web game can start music on its own anyway.
  • Quiz games often use a chime for "right" and a buzz for "wrong". This game cannot. That habit is exactly what the one rule above rules out.
  • A middle path is small sounds that only say "the screen heard you": a tap, a send, a sheet opening.

We have no player data on sound for this game yet. This is craft judgement, not measurement. We have not invented any figures.

What matters for players in India

  • Playing in public. On a train, at a shop counter, in a tea break. Sound must be off until asked, short, soft and never startling. Nothing that could pass for a real alarm, horn or announcement.
  • Small phone speakers. Low-end Android phones have one small speaker with almost no bass, and a processor that is often busy. So: few notes at once, and sounds that carry their weight in the middle of the range, roughly 300 Hz to 4,000 Hz.
  • Data cost. Synthesised sound weighs nothing, because the browser builds it from a few lines of code. Recorded sound costs data. A simple sum: a 30 second loop at 96 kbps is 30 x 96,000 / 8 = 360,000 bytes, about 360 KB. Six such loops are about 2.2 MB, a lot to ask of a prepaid data plan for decoration.
  • The phone's own mute. Many people keep their phone on silent or the media volume at zero. On some iPhones the side switch silences web sound too. The game cannot read these settings and should not nag about them. The player hears nothing and loses nothing.
  • Earphones. Bluetooth earphones can delay sound a little. Nothing in this game is timed, so no sound ever carries timing.
  • Two languages. No spoken or sung words in any sound. English and Hindi players get the same game.

2Principles

  1. Say "we heard you", never "you were right". Sound confirms an action. It never judges one.
  2. One sound for one event, always. Same notes, same length, same loudness, for every person, every pick, every day.
  3. On, with an easy off. Two switches in the "?" sheet: Music and Sounds. Nothing plays before the player's first tap.
  4. Short and soft. Every effect under 600 ms, apart from the result reveal (up to 1.2 s, matching the picture) and the milestone. Nothing sudden or loud.
  5. No clocks. No ticking, no pulse that speeds up, no countdown tone. Nothing may feel like time running out.
  6. Middle of the range, mono, no words. It works on a tiny speaker, in one earbud, in either language.
  7. Make it, do not download it. Build every sound in the browser. The game ships code and no audio files.
  8. Stop when the player leaves. Pause when the screen locks or the tab is hidden, and never restart by itself. Reduced motion calms sound too.

4Sound effect map

Twelve events. Each has a Lugdi sketch and a Station sketch. Every one is fixed: it is a function of time only, so it cannot receive who you picked, the score or the lean. Play them over a music loop to hear the mix.

Sound and the one vibration pulse

On the commit, start the commit sound and the single short pulse in the same line of code, so they land together and feel like one thing. Then nothing else vibrates, ever.

  • The pulse does not depend on the sound switch. Sound off, pulse still fires, if the device has one.
  • Some phones, including iPhones in the browser, have no vibration. There the sound carries the moment alone. Check this on real devices when building.
  • The pulse length here is only a placeholder of 20 ms. The rulebook's own length applies.

Reduced motion should calm sound

When the device asks for reduced motion, switch on a calm set of sounds. The player's own two switches still decide whether any sound plays. The Calm sound switch at the top of this page shows it.

  • The result reveal becomes a short soft version, under half a second, instead of the full reprint or cascade.
  • The milestone is the stamp only, with no bell.
  • Music loses its stings, horns, drum fills, echo tails and the reedy lead, and sits a little lower.
  • Check the setting at start and listen for changes, so it follows the phone.

5Comparing the directions

These ratings are our judgement from the sketches, not measurements. Effort and risk are relative within each world.

Lugdi

3 directions

A. 70s pulp thriller

  • Effort: medium to high
  • Public risk: high
Fit with the world
The strongest match for the paperback and poster look. It says "pulp" within a second.
Risk
The busiest and cheekiest, so it gets repetitive inside a minute, can feel cheesy, and is the most likely to turn heads at a shop counter. A thriller mood can also make the people in a puzzle seem guilty, which works against the spirit of the one rule.
Cost and effort
Four layers and the most notes, so the most tuning. Nothing to download.
Accessibility
A busy rhythm competes with attention and with a screen reader's speech. The bass is built with middle harmonics so a phone speaker still carries it. Needs the music switch and a calm version without the echo.

B. Radio-serial drama

  • Effort: medium
  • Public risk: medium
Fit with the world
Warm and right for the era. It echoes the radio serials of those years, and the harmonium and tabla are familiar to many Hindi-speaking adults. Sits well with the letter and gossip puzzles.
Risk
A made-up tabla can sound like a toy, and a drone can tire. A sting on a clock can feel like a verdict arriving, so keep it to the cover.
Cost and effort
Three layers. A convincing tabla and harmonium are hard to build from scratch. A real recording would sound better, but it would cost data and rights.
Accessibility
Slow and steady, with few sudden changes once the sting is gone. The middle-range sound carries on small speakers. Calm sound drops the sting.

C. No music, only the print shop

  • Effort: low
  • Public risk: low, but may seem broken
Fit with the world
Quiet, tactile and true to a printed world. It makes the result reprint feel like part of the same room.
Risk
Low for annoyance, high for "is it even on?". On a train or a noisy counter it vanishes. A press clunk from a phone might puzzle people nearby.
Cost and effort
The lowest. Filtered noise and a few clunks. Nothing to download.
Accessibility
The gentlest. No tune, no beat, little for a sensitive listener to mind. Its low parts are lost on a phone speaker, but it is pleasant in earphones in bed.

Your pick for Lugdi

B, radio-serial drama, trimmed: the drone and the tabla only, played quietly, with the sting kept for the cover.

Why: it fits the era and the audience best. Its middle-range sound survives a small speaker. It is slow. And it pushes the player towards suspicion less than A does. If the tabla ever sounds like a toy, C is the fallback.

Station

3 directions

A. Platform bed

  • Effort: low to medium
  • Public risk: medium to high
Fit with the world
Very authentic. It feels like waiting on a platform, which is the world.
Risk
On a real platform it blends with real noise, and the horn may make someone look up. A murmur with no words can still sound like people next to you. Keep the horn for the cover, or leave it out.
Cost and effort
Filtered noise and a hum. Nothing to download.
Accessibility
Steady noise can mask speech for a person who hears less well, and some players dislike murmurs. No beat. Calm sound removes the horn.

B. Chime motif, used sparingly

  • Effort: low
  • Public risk: low
Fit with the world
Good. A small sound of its own, like a station bell, without copying the announcement chime. Sits well with enamel boards and tickets.
Risk
The least annoying because it is rare. Two real risks: it could come too close to a real announcement chime, so a player at a station might look up, and as "music" it can feel like nothing at all. It must never play on a pick or a result.
Cost and effort
The lowest. Four notes. The skill is in choosing the notes and the gaps. Nothing to download.
Accessibility
Short, soft and predictable. Easy to ignore or turn off. Nothing sudden. Best for people who find music tiring.

C. Rail rhythm groove

  • Effort: high
  • Public risk: high
Fit with the world
Lively and warm, like a journey. The shehnai adds local colour.
Risk
The highest. A reedy lead sits where phone speakers are loudest and ears are most sensitive, and it repeats quickly. A shehnai often marks weddings and auspicious days, which may feel wrong for a fairness puzzle. A fast steady clack can feel like a ticking clock.
Cost and effort
Rhythm, drone, tune and a reedy voice to build. A real shehnai recording would cost data and rights.
Accessibility
The busiest. A constant rhythm and a piercing tone are the hardest on a sensitive listener. It would need a calm version without the lead, and a slower tempo.

Your pick for Station

B, the chime motif, used on the cover. The result keeps its own flap sound (question 2).

Why: it is the cheapest, the least tiring and the safest in public. It cannot be mistaken for a real platform. A bed under the puzzle could come later from A, without the horn.

6Your answers

All eight are decided, and each answer is now part of the design rules. The music is picked too: Lugdi B, trimmed, and Station B, the chime (section 5).

  1. Should the game ever offer sound on the cover, or only in the "?" sheet?

    Example: Someone opens the game on the 8:10 local and never taps "?". They will never find the sound. A one-time "Want sound?" button on their first day would help them. But the rulebook says the "?" sheet is the one settings place.

    Options: never; one question on the first day only; a small speaker on the cover.

    Your answer: sound is on from the start, and players switch it off in the "?" sheet. Nothing on the cover.

  2. Should the result have a sound at all?

    Example: You send three people to the interview and the page reprints. The reprint sound is the same whatever happened. Is a sound that never changes a nice little ritual, or does it feel like the game is hiding something?

    Options: silent; the soft reprint or flap sketch; one tiny tick.

    Your answer: the soft reprint or flap sound, the same every time, with the calm version for reduced motion.

  3. Should Sunday's look-again screen be silent on purpose?

    Example: On Sunday you look back over the week's picks. Silence there could tell the player "this is a calmer moment, not a new round".

    Options: fully silent; only sheet and tab sounds; the full set.

    Your answer: yes, silent on purpose, apart from sheets opening and closing.

  4. Should the two worlds sound different?

    Example: On Monday in Lugdi, "Send" is a press clunk. The next Monday in Station, it is a lever and a latch. One shared set is simpler and steadier. Two sets make the weekly change feel real.

    Your answer: yes, two sets, one for each world.

  5. Should the milestone stamp have a bell?

    Example: Your seventh day in a row gets a stamp. At a shop counter, a bell may make people look at your phone.

    Options: stamp only; stamp and one soft note; silent.

    Your answer: yes, the stamp and one soft note, with no note under reduced motion.

  6. Is a train horn on the cover a charm or a risk?

    Example: A player waiting on a real platform opens the game, and a horn from their phone makes the person beside them look up for their train.

    Options: a very soft horn on the cover only; a station bell instead; none.

    Your answer: no horn in the game itself, and at most one soft sound on the cover.

  7. Should music fade out by itself if nobody touches the screen?

    Example: A player leaves the puzzle open on a table in the tea break and walks off. Their phone keeps playing to the room.

    Options: play until the screen locks; fade out after a few minutes of no taps.

    Your answer: yes, music fades out after a few minutes without a tap (3 minutes to start with).

  8. Is one short line under the sound switch too much text?

    Example: A player turns music on in a quiet train compartment. A line such as "Plays from your phone's speaker" would warn them. But game screens are meant to carry very few words.

    Options: no line; one line, shown only the first time the switch is turned on.

    Your answer: no line.

How the sketches are made
  • Plain Web Audio: oscillators, a shared noise buffer, filters, gain envelopes and one simple echo. No samples, no files, no outside scripts.
  • One audio context, created on your first press. Everything runs through a master compressor and a volume control, so nothing clips.
  • Every sound is mono. There is no panning and no stereo trick, so one speaker or one earbud loses nothing.
  • Music loops are scheduled a fraction of a second ahead by a small timer, so the beat stays steady even if the page is busy. Only one loop plays at a time.
  • Each sound effect is a function of time only. It has no way to receive who you picked, the score or the lean. Where a sound has a random-looking pattern, such as the flaps, the pattern is fixed, so it is identical every time.
  • Music stops when this page is hidden or the screen locks.
  • These are sketches for judging an idea. Final levels should be tuned on a cheap Android phone speaker and on earphones.
